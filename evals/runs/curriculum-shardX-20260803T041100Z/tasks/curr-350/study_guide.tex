\sstitleblock{Compass-and-Straightedge Constructions: Plan, Justify, Check}

\skillheading{Skill 1 --- Writing a construction as a plan}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{A plan is a numbered list of moves, and every arc step must answer three
questions:} where is the compass \emph{point}, did the \emph{opening} change, and what is
the new point or line \emph{called}?\par
\smallskip
\textbf{The four plans everything else is built from:} perpendicular bisector of a
segment; bisector of an angle; copy of a segment; copy of an angle. Every later
construction is one of these repeated or combined.\par
\smallskip
Legal tools only: unmarked straightedge and compass. No measuring.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad Plan the construction of the perpendicular bisector of a segment
$AB$.
\step{The line must pass through two points that are each equidistant from $A$ and from
$B$, so the plan is: manufacture two such points with equal arcs, then join them.}
\step{Step one: compass on $A$, opening more than half of $AB$, arc above and below. Step
two: same opening, compass on $B$, arc above and below, crossing at $M$ and $N$. Step
three: straightedge through $M$ and $N$.}
$\Rightarrow\quad \ans{\text{line } MN}$ is the perpendicular bisector
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad Write the plan for bisecting angle $DEF$. Name the two arcs that
must share one compass opening.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: arc from $E$ cuts the rays at $P$, $Q$; equal arcs
from $P$ and $Q$ meet at $R$; draw $\ans{\text{ray } ER}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{Skill 2 --- Justifying a construction}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{A justification never points at the picture.} It names what is equal
\emph{by construction} and then applies a theorem. Two moves cover almost everything:
\begin{itemize}[label={$\bullet$}, leftmargin=1.1cm, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{Equal radii.} Every point on one arc is the same distance from its centre,
        because all radii of a circle are equal. This is where the equal sides come from.
  \item \textbf{SSS, then CPCTC.} Equal radii give three pairs of equal sides, so the
        triangles are congruent, and the angles or halves you actually wanted follow from
        corresponding parts.
\end{itemize}
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad Given segment $AB$, arcs of radius $AB$ centred at $A$ and at $B$
meet at $C$. Prove $\triangle ABC$ is equilateral.
\step{The two arcs were drawn with one unchanged opening, so the claim to prove is about
radii --- use the definition of a circle rather than the drawing.}
\step{$C$ is on the circle centred at $A$ with radius $AB$, so $AC = AB$; $C$ is on the
circle centred at $B$ with the same radius, so $BC = AB$. All three sides are equal.}
$\Rightarrow\quad \triangle ABC \text{ is } \ans{\text{equilateral}}$
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad In the perpendicular-bisector construction the arc crossings are
$M$ and $N$. Name the congruent triangles and the criterion.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\triangle AMN \cong \triangle BMN$ by
$\ans{\text{SSS}}$, then CPCTC}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{Skill 3 --- Checking a construction on coordinates}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Every construction promises one testable property.} Name the property first, then
test it with the distance formula
\[ d = \sqrt{(x_2 - x_1)^2 + (y_2 - y_1)^2} \]
\textbf{Property by construction:} midpoint or perpendicular bisector $\to$ two distances
equal; copy of a segment $\to$ copy equals original; circumcentre $\to$ all three vertex
distances equal; inscribed regular hexagon $\to$ side equals radius.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad A bisector construction on segment $AB$ gives the crossing point
$M(6, 4)$, and the endpoint $A$ sits at $(2, 1)$. Test the construction at $M$.
\step{A perpendicular bisector promises equal distances to the two endpoints, so compute
$MA$ and then check the other endpoint the same way.}
\step{$MA = \sqrt{(6 - 2)^2 + (4 - 1)^2} = 5$, and repeating the computation from the
other endpoint returns the same radicand, so the two distances agree.}
$\Rightarrow\quad MA = \ans{5}$, equal to $MB$
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad A bisector construction gives the crossing point $M(7, 5)$, and
the endpoint $A$ sits at $(1, 1)$. Compute $MA$ to the nearest hundredth.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{7.21}$}
\end{tryitbox}
