\noindent\small\textbf{The argument this sheet is making:} centre and spread are not two
numbers to compute and move on from --- they are a \emph{choice}, and the shape of the
distribution makes it. Class A and Class B were built to have almost the same interquartile
range and wildly different ranges, so that a student who reports whichever statistic came
first gets a defensible answer for one class and a badly misleading one for the other. Grade
problems 5, 7, 8 and 10 on whether the shape is named and used, not on whether the adjective
is right.\normalsize

\problem{\textbf{Class A --- the median.}\par
With $n = 12$ the median sits between the 6th and 7th ordered values. Counting in:
$52, 55, 58, 60, 62, \underline{64}, \underline{66}, 68, 70, 73, 76, 96$.
\[ \frac{64 + 66}{2} \]
The median is $\ans{65}$.\par
\textbf{Why those two:} an even count has no single middle value, so the median is defined
as the midpoint of the two that straddle the middle. Note that the extreme score of $96$ had
no influence on this answer at all --- it is simply ``the largest'', and could have been
$960$ without moving the median. That resistance is the property problems 5 and 10 come back
to.}

\problem{\textbf{Class A --- the range.}\par
Range is the largest value minus the smallest:
\[ 96 - 52 \]
The range is $\ans{44}$.\par
\textbf{The value doing the work:} the $96$. Without it, the largest score is $76$ and the
range collapses to $76 - 52 = 24$ --- nearly half. The range is computed from exactly two
observations, both of them the most extreme in the data set, which makes it the least
resistant measure of spread there is.}

\problem{\textbf{Class A --- the mean.}\par
Add all twelve scores, then divide:
\[ 52 + 55 + 58 + 60 + 62 + 64 + 66 + 68 + 70 + 73 + 76 + 96 = 800 \]
\[ \frac{800}{12} = 66.666\ldots \]
Rounded to the nearest hundredth, the mean is $\ans{66.67}$.\par
\textbf{Compare with problem 1:} the mean sits $1.67$ points \emph{above} the median. Ten of
the twelve students scored below the mean. That is not an error --- it is what a long right
tail does, and problem 5 asks the student to say so.\par
\textit{Watch for:} averaging only the smallest and largest value, $(52 + 96)/2 = 74$. That
is the \emph{midrange}, not the mean, and on skewed data it is even less trustworthy than the
mean --- it uses only the two most extreme observations.}

\problem{\textbf{Class A --- quartiles and the IQR.}\par
Split the twelve ordered values into two halves of six.\par
\textbf{(a)} Lower half: $52, 55, \underline{58}, \underline{60}, 62, 64$. Its median is
$\dfrac{58 + 60}{2}$, so $Q_1 = \ans{59}$.\par
\textbf{(b)} Upper half: $66, 68, \underline{70}, \underline{73}, 76, 96$. Its median is
$\dfrac{70 + 73}{2}$, so $Q_3 = \ans{71.5}$.\par
\textbf{(c)} $\text{IQR} = 71.5 - 59$, so the IQR is $\ans{12.5}$.\par
\textbf{What the IQR is:} the width of the middle half of the data. Between $Q_1$ and $Q_3$
sit exactly six of the twelve students. Like the median, it never saw the $96$: $Q_3$ is
computed from the 9th and 10th values, so the top two observations could be anything at all.}

\problem{\textbf{Class A --- centre and shape together.}\par
\textbf{(a)} The mean is $66.67$ and the median is $65$, so
\[ \ans{66.67 > 65} \]
\textbf{(b)} The distribution is \textbf{skewed right}, with an \textbf{outlier} at $96$.\par
\textbf{The reasoning to require:} the mean is a balance point and moves toward whichever
side has values far from the centre; the median is a counting point and only cares about
order. So when the mean sits above the median, the far-from-centre values are on the high
side --- a right tail. The dot plot confirms it directly: eleven scores are packed between
$52$ and $76$, and one sits alone at $96$, twenty points clear of its nearest neighbour.\par
\textit{Grading note:} ``mean $>$ median so it is skewed right'' is the right shape from the
right evidence, and is worth full credit; the reasoning above is what you want if a student
asks why the rule works. Reject ``skewed right because the biggest number is on the right''
--- every ordered data set has its biggest number on the right.}

\problem{\textbf{Class B --- a symmetric distribution.}\par
\textbf{(a)} The twelve Class B scores are evenly spaced by $2$, from $60$ to $82$. Their
total is $852$:
\[ \frac{852}{12} \]
The mean is $\ans{71}$.\par
\textbf{(b)} The middle two values are $70$ and $72$:
\[ \frac{70 + 72}{2} \]
The median is $\ans{71}$.\par
\textbf{(c)} The two centres are identical. When mean and median agree, neither tail is
pulling harder than the other, which is the signature of a \textbf{symmetric} distribution
--- and the dot plot shows it: the points are evenly spread with no gap and no isolated
value.\par
\textbf{Why it happened here and not for Class A:} Class B has no observation far from the
rest. Every score is within $11$ points of the centre. Class A has one at $96$, thirty-one
points above its own $Q_1$, and the mean --- which weights every observation by its actual
value --- gets dragged toward it while the median does not.}

\problem{\textbf{Two measures of spread, disagreeing.}\par
\textbf{(a)} Range: $82 - 60$, so the Class B range is $\ans{22}$.\par
\textbf{(b)} $Q_1$ is the median of $60, 62, \underline{64}, \underline{66}, 68, 70$, which
is $65$; $Q_3$ is the median of $72, 74, \underline{76}, \underline{78}, 80, 82$, which is
$77$. So $\text{IQR} = 77 - 65 = \ans{12}$.\par
\textbf{(c)} Side by side:
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|l|c|c|}
\hline
 & \textbf{range} & \textbf{IQR} \\ \hline
Class A & $44$ & $12.5$ \\ \hline
Class B & $22$ & $12$ \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
By range, Class A looks twice as spread out as Class B. By IQR, the two classes are almost
identical.\par
\textbf{The IQR is telling the truth about the bulk of the data.} It measures the width of
the middle half, so it is computed entirely from observations near the centre and is
unaffected by how far the extremes reach. The middle half of Class A spans $12.5$ points and
the middle half of Class B spans $12$ --- the two classes really are alike in the region
where nearly everyone sits.\par
\textbf{Why the range disagrees.} It is computed from two observations, and one of them is
Class A's $96$. Remove that single score and Class A's range drops to $24$, within two points
of Class B's. A statistic that one observation out of twelve can double is describing that
observation, not the class.\par
\textbf{What to report:} both, honestly --- ``the two classes have similar middles
(IQR $12.5$ and $12$) but Class A has one score far above the rest''. That sentence is more
informative than either number alone, and it is the habit problem 10 asks for.}

\problem{\textbf{The commute histogram --- reading the shape.}\par
\textbf{(a)} The tallest bar is the third one, so the modal interval is
$\ans{20 \text{ to } 29}$ minutes, holding $14$ workers.\par
\textbf{(b)} Add every bar:
\[ 2 + 8 + 14 + 9 + 4 + 2 + 1 \]
The sample holds $\ans{40}$ workers.\par
\textbf{(c)} The distribution is \textbf{skewed right}: the bars rise quickly to a peak at
$20$--$29$ minutes and then fall away slowly, with a long thin tail running out to
$60$--$69$. Because that tail is on the high side, it will pull the mean above the median ---
the same relationship problem 5 established for Class A, now read off a shape rather than
computed from two numbers.\par
\textbf{The check worth mentioning:} the total in (b) is not decoration. Every frequency
question on a histogram needs the sample size, and a student who cannot produce it has not
finished reading the display --- problem 9 needs it immediately.}

\problem{\textbf{The commute histogram --- a tail probability.}\par
``At least $40$ minutes'' means the $40$--$49$, $50$--$59$ and $60$--$69$ intervals --- the
three bars in the right tail:
\[ 4 + 2 + 1 = 7 \text{ workers out of } 40 \]
\[ P(\text{at least } 40 \text{ minutes}) = \ans{7/40} \]
This is already in lowest terms, since $7$ is prime and does not divide $40$. As a decimal it
is $0.175$, so about one worker in six.\par
\textbf{Reading it back into shape:} seven workers out of forty spread across thirty minutes
of the range, against fourteen packed into a single ten-minute interval. That imbalance ---
few observations covering a wide stretch on one side --- is precisely what ``skewed right''
describes.\par
\textit{Watch for:} including the $30$--$39$ bar, which gives $16/40$. ``At least $40$''
excludes anything below $40$, and the $30$--$39$ interval lies entirely below it.}

\problem{\textbf{Challenge: reporting the right summary.} (Open synthesis --- read the
reasoning.)\par
The rule the student should arrive at: \textbf{mean and standard deviation for roughly
symmetric data; median and IQR when the data is skewed or has an outlier}, because the mean
and the standard deviation both weight every observation by its distance from the centre and
so are pulled by extremes, while the median and IQR are computed from position and are not.\par
\textbf{Class A --- median and IQR.} The distribution is skewed right with an outlier at
$96$. The mean of $66.67$ sits above ten of the twelve scores, so quoting it as ``the
typical score'' misdescribes the class; the median of $65$ has six scores on each side. The
standard deviation would be inflated by the same single value that doubled the range, so the
IQR of $12.5$ is the honest width.\par
\textbf{Class B --- mean and standard deviation.} The distribution is symmetric with no
outlier, mean and median both $71$. Here the mean loses nothing and gains something: it uses
every observation rather than just the middle ones, so it is the more precise summary, and
the standard deviation is likewise trustworthy when no value is dragging it.\par
\textbf{Commute times --- median and IQR.} The histogram is clearly skewed right, with a
tail running out to $60$--$69$ minutes. The mean commute would be pulled above the $20$--$29$
interval where most workers actually sit.\par
\textbf{Full credit requires,} for each of the three: (i) the shape named, (ii) the choice,
and (iii) what would go wrong with the other choice, in terms of extremes pulling the mean or
inflating the standard deviation. A student who writes only ``Class A is skewed so use the
median'' has (i) and (ii) but not (iii), and (iii) is where the understanding lives.\par
\textit{Do not accept} ``always use the median because it is safer''. On symmetric data the
mean is the better estimate, and a blanket rule is the absence of the judgement the problem
is testing.\par
Open synthesis --- $\ans{\text{open response}}$.}
