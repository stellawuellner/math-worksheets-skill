\aktitleblock{Shapes and Their Names --- Turning a Shape Does Not Change It}{Kindergarten / Grade 1}{}
\input{qa_r_curr046}

\noindent\small\textbf{What this sheet is teaching, and what to listen for:} at this age
children learn shape names from pictures that are almost always drawn the same way up ---
triangles resting on a flat bottom, squares with horizontal tops. The result is a very
common and very fixable misunderstanding: a square turned onto its corner gets a new name,
``diamond,'' and a triangle standing on its point stops being a triangle. The cure is not
to tell the child the rule but to have them \emph{count}. Sides and corners do not change
when a shape is turned, and counting is something a five-year-old can do and trust. When
you hear ``that's a diamond,'' answer with ``how many sides?'' rather than ``no.''\normalsize

\problem{\textbf{Sorting Shape Set One.}\par
Shapes A, D and F are triangles: three straight sides and three corners each, even though
A is upside down, D leans over, and F points sideways. Shapes B and E are squares --- B is
balanced on a corner and E is turned partway round, but both have four equal straight sides
and four square corners. Shape C is the circle: one curved edge, no corners at all.\par
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|}
\hline
triangles & squares & circles \\ \hline
$\ans{3}$ & $\ans{2}$ & $\ans{1}$ \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\textbf{If the child misses one:} it is nearly always A or B --- the upside-down triangle
and the square on its corner. Cover the rest of the row with your hand, turn the page
until that shape looks familiar, and let them name it. Then turn the page back and ask
whether it changed.}

\problem{\textbf{Which are there more of?}\par
From the counts in problem 1, there are $3$ triangles and $2$ squares, so there are more
triangles. Written as a comparison:
\[ \ans{3 > 2} \]
The child should circle \textbf{more triangles}.\par
\textbf{The point of asking:} the comparison can only be made after the shapes have been
named, so a child who called shape B a diamond will get $3$ and $1$ here and still answer
``more triangles''. The right answer for the wrong reason --- worth checking their tally in
problem 1 rather than just this circle.}

\problem{\textbf{Turned every which way.}\par
The first four shapes are all triangles: three straight sides, three corners. One points
down, one leans right, one leans left, one points sideways --- none of that changes the
count. The fifth shape is the rectangle, and it gets the X: four sides, four corners.\par
The child should have coloured $\ans{4}$ triangles.\par
\textbf{Listen for the reason.} ``It's not a triangle because it has four sides'' is the
answer you want. ``It's not a triangle because it looks different'' means the counting
habit has not taken hold yet --- go back and count sides on each of the five shapes
together.}

\problem{\textbf{Count them and see.}\par
Have the child touch each straight edge and say a number, then each corner and say a
number.\par
Sides: $\ans{4}$. \quad Corners: $\ans{4}$.\par
The four sides are all the same length and all four corners are square corners, so its
name is \textbf{square}. It is balanced on a corner, and that is all.\par
\textbf{About ``diamond'':} it is not a wrong word so much as a word about \emph{position}
rather than \emph{shape}. ``Diamond'' tells you which way it is turned; ``square'' tells
you what it is. A helpful thing to say: ``you can call it a diamond if you like --- and it
is still a square.''}

\problem{\textbf{Find the rectangles.}\par
The rectangles are the tall one standing on end, the one leaning over, and the wide one
lying flat --- $\ans{3}$ altogether. Each has four straight sides and four square corners,
with opposite sides the same length.\par
The other three are the circle (curved, no corners), the triangle (three sides), and the
hexagon (six sides).\par
\textbf{Worth naming out loud:} the three rectangles are not the same size or the same way
up, and they are all rectangles anyway. Size, position and turn are all things that can
change without the name changing. Sides and corners are what the name is made of.\par
\textit{A square is also a rectangle} --- true, and not something to raise here unless the
child raises it. If they do, say yes, a square is a special rectangle whose sides are all
equal, and let it rest there.}

\problem{\textbf{Which group has more four-sided shapes?}\par
\textbf{Group A} holds a square on its corner, a leaning rectangle, a triangle and nothing
else with four sides --- so two four-sided shapes.\par
\textbf{Group B} holds a square, a tall rectangle, a square on its corner and a leaning
rectangle --- four four-sided shapes.\par
Comparing the two counts:
\[ \ans{2 < 4} \]
so Group B has more.\par
\textbf{Why this problem is harder than problem 2:} the child has to apply a \emph{rule}
(four straight sides) rather than recall a \emph{name}, and the rule has to survive the
shapes being turned. A child who counts three in Group A has counted the triangle; a child
who counts three in Group B has missed one of the turned ones.}

\problem{\textbf{The same shape, turned.}\par
Both drawings are the same square. Counting corners on each gives four and four:
\[ \ans{4 = 4} \]
The two numbers are equal, and that is the whole lesson on this sheet: turning a shape
changes nothing you can count. Same number of sides, same number of corners, same name ---
\textbf{square}.\par
\textbf{The sentence to draw out of the child:} something like ``it's still a square
because it still has four sides and four corners.'' If they say ``because it's the same one
just turned,'' that is also correct and arguably better --- ask them how they would prove
it to someone who did not watch you turn it, and steer them to counting.}

\problem{\textbf{Your turn to draw.} (Open drawing --- check it with the child.)\par
There is no single right answer. Anything with three straight sides and three corners
counts, as long as it is not resting flat on a horizontal bottom edge: a triangle standing
on its point, one tipped over onto a side, one pointing sideways.\par
\textbf{Check two things:}
\begin{itemize}[label={$\bullet$}, leftmargin=1.1cm, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{The drawing.} Three straight sides that meet, and a closed shape. Wobbly
        lines are fine at this age; a gap where two sides should meet is worth pointing out
        gently, because a shape has to be closed.
  \item \textbf{The reason.} Full credit is a reason that names something countable ---
        ``it has three sides'' or ``it has three corners''. A reason that names the turn
        (``because I drew it like a triangle'') is the one to push on: ask ``how would you
        show me?'' and count together.
\end{itemize}
If the child draws a triangle sitting flat after all, do not mark it wrong --- ask them to
turn the paper and look at what they have. It is the same triangle, which is the answer to
the question they were actually asked.\par
Open drawing and explanation --- $\ans{\text{open response}}$.}
