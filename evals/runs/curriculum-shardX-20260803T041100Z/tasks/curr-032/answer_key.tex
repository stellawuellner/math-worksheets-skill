\problem{One full ten-frame and 3 extra dots. The filled frame is one ten, so count
on from ten: 11, 12, 13. Ten and 3 ones is $10 + 3$, and the numeral is $\ans{13}$.}

\problem{One ten and 7 ones. Start at ten and count on 7: 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17.
Written as a sum, $10 + 7$, so the numeral is $\ans{17}$.}

\problem{The word \textit{fourteen} is heard as ``four-teen'': the \textit{-teen} part
means one ten and the \textit{four} part means 4 ones. So the number is $10 + 4$ and
the numeral is $\ans{14}$.}

\problem{Number word: \textbf{eighteen}. Reading the numeral 18, the 1 stands for one
ten and the 8 stands for the ones. Take the ten away from 18 to see the ones:
$18 - 10$ leaves $\ans{8}$ ones. So 18 is one ten and 8 ones.}

\problem{One full ten-frame is one ten, and the second frame holds 9 dots. Count on
from ten: 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16, 17, 18, 19. Ten and 9 ones is $10 + 9$, so the
numeral is $\ans{19}$.}

\problem{Number word: \textbf{twelve}. Twelve is a name that must be memorised --- it
does not say ``two-teen'' --- but its place value works the same way. Reading 12, the
1 is one ten, so the ones are what is left after the ten: $12 - 10 = \ans{2}$ ones.}

\problem{The word \textit{sixteen} means six and one ten: $10 + 6$. Counting on from
ten confirms it: 11, 12, 13, 14, 15, 16. The numeral is $\ans{16}$.}

\problem{15 stickers in all. One ten of them are bagged, so subtract that whole ten
from the total: $15 - 10 = \ans{5}$ loose stickers. The 1 in 15 is worth ten stickers,
not one, which is why 14 is not the answer.}

\problem{Both numbers have one ten, so compare the ones: 13 has 3 ones and 15 has
5 ones. Since 3 ones is fewer than 5 ones, 13 comes first when counting, so the
true sentence uses $\ans{<}$.}

\problem{Sam's number word \textit{fourteen} is the numeral 14; Mia's \textit{seventeen}
is 17. Both have one ten, so compare the ones: 4 ones is fewer than 7 ones. Counting
forward we reach 14 before 17, so Sam's number is less than Mia's: $\ans{<}$.}
