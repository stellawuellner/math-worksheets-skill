\sstitleblock{Slope and Rate of Change}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{One idea, four places to find it.} A rate of change says how much $y$
moves for each $1$ that $x$ moves. Written as slope:
$m = \frac{y_2 - y_1}{x_2 - x_1}$.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Graph:} two lattice points. \quad \textbf{Table:} two rows. \quad
\textbf{Equation $y = mx + b$:} the number $m$ in front of $x$. \quad
\textbf{Story:} the ``per'' number.\\[3pt]
A \emph{positive} rate rises, a \emph{negative} rate falls, and the size of the
number is how steep it is.
\end{formulabox}

\skillheading{1. Slope from two points}

\begin{formulabox}
Label the points $(x_1, y_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2)$ \emph{before} subtracting, and
keep that order on the top and the bottom. Swapping one and not the other flips
the sign of your answer. On a graph, use points the line passes exactly
through.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad Find the slope through $(1, 4)$ and $(5, 16)$.
\step{Two points and nothing else, so this is the slope formula; the only real
decision is which point is which, and then keeping it.}
\step{$m = \frac{16 - 4}{5 - 1} = \frac{12}{4}$}

$\Rightarrow\quad \ans{3}$ \quad ($y$ rises $3$ for every $1$ that $x$ rises)
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad Find the slope through $(2, 9)$ and $(5, 3)$.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{-2}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{2. Rate of change from a table}

\begin{formulabox}
Pick any two rows and divide the change in the output by the change in the
input. If every pair of rows gives the \emph{same} answer, the table is linear
and that number is its rate; if two intervals disagree, there is no single
rate --- which is exactly how you prove a table is not linear.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad Find the rate of change of this table.
\par\smallskip
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|}\hline
$x$ & $1$ & $3$ \\ \hline
$y$ & $7$ & $15$ \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\step{A table gives points, so the rate is a slope --- read one column as
$(1, 7)$ and the other as $(3, 15)$.}
\step{$m = \frac{15 - 7}{3 - 1} = \frac{8}{2}$}

$\Rightarrow\quad \ans{4}$ per unit of $x$
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad $x = 0$ gives $y = 20$; \ $x = 2$ gives $y = 14$. What
is the rate?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{-3}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{3. Rate of change from an equation}

\begin{formulabox}
Get the rule into the form $y = mx + b$. Then $m$ is the rate and $b$ is only
the starting value --- $b$ never affects the rate, because it is added once and
never changes. Not sure? Evaluate at $x = 0$ and $x = 1$; the jump between
those two outputs is $m$.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad What is the rate of change of $y = 6x - 4$?
\step{The rule is already in $y = mx + b$ form, so the rate is read off, not
computed --- but check it once with two points to be sure.}
\step{At $x = 0$, $y = -4$; at $x = 1$, $y = 2$. The jump is
$2 - (-4) = 6$.}

$\Rightarrow\quad \ans{6}$ \quad (the $-4$ is the starting value, not part of
the rate)
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad What is the rate of change of $y = -7x + 5$?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{-7}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{4. Comparing two rates}

\begin{formulabox}
You cannot compare a table with an equation directly. Turn \emph{each} one into
a rate in the same units first, then compare the two numbers. With negatives,
compare positions on a number line: $-5$ is less than $-2$, even though $-5$ is
the steeper fall.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad Plan A costs $y = 9x$ dollars for $x$ hours. Plan B
costs $33$ dollars for $3$ hours. Which rate is larger?
\step{The two plans are given in different forms, so neither can be compared
until both are a cost per hour.}
\step{Plan A: the coefficient gives $9$ per hour. Plan B:
$33 \div 3 = 11$ per hour. Now compare $9$ with $11$.}

$\Rightarrow\quad$ Plan A's rate $\ans{<}$ Plan B's rate
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad One rate is $15$ per hour, the other is $12$ per hour.
Write $<$ or $>$: \ first \underline{\hspace{0.9cm}} second.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{>}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} bigger numbers in a table do not mean a bigger rate ---
they may just cover more hours. And when rates include negatives, ``fastest''
and ``greatest'' are different questions: $-5$ is the fastest fall and the
smallest number.
\end{watchoutbox}
