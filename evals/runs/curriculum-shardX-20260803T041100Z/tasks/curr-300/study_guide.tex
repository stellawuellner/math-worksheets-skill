\sstitleblock{Choosing Linear, Quadratic, or Piecewise Models}

\noindent\small The model is a \emph{conclusion}, not a starting assumption.
Read the evidence --- a difference table, or the wording of the story --- decide
the family, and only then write a formula.
\normalsize

\skillheading{1. Evidence for a linear model}
\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Evidence:} equal steps in the input give \textbf{constant first
differences} in the output; or the story names one fixed rate that never
changes (``\$15 per hour'', ``5 cm each minute'').\\[3pt]
\textbf{Model:} $y = mx + b$, where $m$ is the constant difference per unit
step and $b$ is the output when $x = 0$. If the table does not start at
$x = 0$, find $b$ by substituting any one row.\\[3pt]
\textbf{(!)} A start-up fee is added \emph{once}. Doubling the input never
doubles the output unless $b = 0$.
\end{formulabox}
\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} For $x = 0, 1, 2, 3$ a table gives $y = 3, 7, 11, 15$.
Predict $y$ at $x = 9$.
\step{The first differences are $4, 4, 4$ --- constant --- so the family is
linear and I only need a slope and an intercept.}
\step{$m = 4$ and $y = 3$ at $x = 0$, so $y = 4x + 3$, and at $x = 9$ that is
$4(9) + 3$.}
\par
$\ans{39}$
\end{examplebox}
\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} For $x = 0, 1, 2, 3$ a table gives $y = 2, 5, 8, 11$. Predict
$y$ at $x = 8$.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{26}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{2. Evidence for a quadratic model}
\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Evidence:} the first differences \emph{change}, but they change by the
same amount each time --- \textbf{constant second differences}. In a story:
anything that multiplies two quantities which both depend on the input
(area, revenue $=$ price $\times$ quantity, projectile height).\\[3pt]
\textbf{Model:} $y = ax^2 + bx + c$. The second difference equals $2a$ for
unit steps, so a second difference of $-32$ gives $a = -16$.\\[3pt]
\textbf{(!)} Never extend a quadratic table with its first difference. That
treats a changing rate as constant and the error grows with every step.
\end{formulabox}
\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} For $x = 0, 1, 2, 3$ a table gives $y = 1, 4, 11, 22$.
Predict $y$ at $x = 5$.
\step{First differences $3, 7, 11$ are not constant but the second differences
are both $4$, so the family is quadratic, not linear.}
\step{Second difference $4$ gives $a = 2$; $c = 1$ from $x = 0$; and $x = 1$
gives $2 + b + 1 = 4$, so $b = 1$. Then $y = 2x^2 + x + 1$ at $x = 5$ is
$50 + 5 + 1$.}
\par
$\ans{56}$
\end{examplebox}
\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} For $x = 0, 1, 2, 3$ a table gives $y = 2, 6, 16, 32$. Predict
$y$ at $x = 4$.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{54}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{3. Evidence for a piecewise model}
\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Evidence:} the story states a boundary where the \emph{rule} changes
--- ``the first 3 days\ldots{} after that\ldots'', ``up to 6 miles\ldots{}
beyond 6 miles\ldots''. One formula cannot describe both sides.
\[ F(t) = \begin{cases} \text{flat fee}, & t \le \text{boundary} \\
   \text{flat fee} + \text{rate} \times (t - \text{boundary}), &
   t > \text{boundary} \end{cases} \]
\textbf{(!)} Subtract the boundary before multiplying, and after solving check
that your answer really lies in the piece you used.
\end{formulabox}
\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} A locker costs $5$ dollars for the first $3$ days and $2$
dollars for each day after that. Find the cost of $7$ days.
\step{The rule changes at day $3$, so this is piecewise and only the days past
the boundary are charged at the daily rate.}
\step{Days past the boundary: $7 - 3 = 4$, so the cost is $5 + 2(4)$.}
\par
$\ans{13}$
\end{examplebox}
\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} A rental costs $6$ dollars for the first $4$ hours and $3$
dollars for each hour after that. Find the cost of $9$ hours.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{21}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{4. Comparing two models}
\begin{formulabox}
``When do the two agree?'' means \textbf{set the models equal and solve}, then
substitute back to get the matching output.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Two lines} meet at most once (or never, if the slopes match).
\textbf{A line and a parabola} can meet twice, once, or not at all --- so
finding two answers is normal, not an error.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Read the meeting point:} it is where the cheaper option changes. Before
it one model wins; after it the other does.
\end{formulabox}
\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Find every point where $y = x^2 + 2$ and $y = 3x$ agree.
\step{Agreement means equal outputs at the same input, so I set the two
expressions equal --- and because one is quadratic I expect up to two answers.}
\step{$x^2 + 2 = 3x$ gives $x^2 - 3x + 2 = 0$, so $(x-1)(x-2) = 0$ and
$x = 1$ or $x = 2$; then $y = 3x$ gives $3$ and $6$.}
\par
$\ans{(1,\ 3) \text{ and } (2,\ 6)}$
\end{examplebox}
\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Find the point where $y = 5 + 3x$ and $y = 11 + x$
agree.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{(3,\ 14)}$}
\end{tryitbox}
