\problem{Solve $x^{2} + 49 = 0$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Isolate the square: $x^{2} = -49$. A square root of a
negative number is imaginary, and there are two of them. \ans{x = 7i \text{ or } x = -7i}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Solve $3x^{2} + 75 = 0$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Divide by $3$ first: $x^{2} + 25 = 0$, so $x^{2} = -25$.
Both square roots count. \ans{x = 5i \text{ or } x = -5i}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Solve $x^{2} - 4x + 13 = 0$.}

\textbf{Solution:} The discriminant is $(-4)^{2} - 4(1)(13) = 16 - 52 = -36$,
which is negative, so both solutions are non-real.
\begin{align*}
  x &= \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{-36}}{2} = \frac{4 \pm 6i}{2}
\end{align*}
Dividing both parts by $2$ gives the standard form. \ans{x = 2 \pm 3i}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Solve $x^{4} - 81 = 0$, listing all four solutions.}

\textbf{Solution:} Factor as a difference of squares, twice.
\begin{align*}
  x^{4} - 81 &= (x^{2} - 9)(x^{2} + 9) = 0
\end{align*}
The first factor gives $x^{2} = 9$; the second gives $x^{2} = -9$, which is
where the two non-real solutions live. Stopping after the real pair is the
classic incomplete answer. \ans{x = 3,\ -3,\ 3i,\ -3i}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{A student solved $x^{2} + 16 = 0$ and reported only $4i$.
(a) Complete solution set? (b) What is missing and why?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $x^{2} = -16$, and a square root has two values. \ans{x = 4i \text{ or } x = -4i}

\textbf{Part (b) --- instructor-judged.} A full-credit explanation says the
reported set is incomplete because $(-4i)^{2} = 16i^{2} = -16$ as well, so
$-4i$ is a solution too, and a second-degree equation has exactly two solutions
counted with multiplicity. A student who substitutes $-4i$ back and shows it
gives zero has demonstrated it. Adding the missing value with no reason for why
a second one must exist does not earn the mark.

\vspace{0.15cm}
\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Solve $2x^{2} + 4x + 10 = 0$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Divide through by $2$ first --- it makes the discriminant
much easier: $x^{2} + 2x + 5 = 0$. Now the discriminant is
$4 - 20 = -16$, so
\[
  x = \frac{-2 \pm \sqrt{-16}}{2} = \frac{-2 \pm 4i}{2}.
\]
Dividing both parts by $2$: \ans{x = -1 \pm 2i}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Solve $(x - 5)^{2} + 9 = 0$.}

\textbf{Solution:} The square is already isolated, so no formula is needed:
$(x - 5)^{2} = -9$, hence $x - 5 = \pm 3i$. Adding $5$ to both sides gives the
standard form. \ans{x = 5 \pm 3i}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Solve $x^{4} + 3x^{2} - 4 = 0$, listing all four solutions.}

\textbf{Solution:} This is a quadratic in $x^{2}$: factor it as
$(x^{2} + 4)(x^{2} - 1) = 0$. The second factor gives $x^{2} = 1$; the first
gives $x^{2} = -4$. \ans{x = 1,\ -1,\ 2i,\ -2i}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$x^{2} - 2x + 5 = 0$. (a) Substitute $x = 1 + 2i$. (b) Write the
complete solution set.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $(1 + 2i)^{2} = 1 + 4i + 4i^{2} = -3 + 4i$, and
$-2(1 + 2i) = -2 - 4i$. Adding all three terms:
$(-3 + 4i) + (-2 - 4i) + 5$. \ans{0}

\textbf{Solution (b):} Because the left side is zero there, $1 + 2i$ is a
solution; the coefficients are real, so its conjugate is the other one. (The
quadratic formula confirms it: the discriminant is $4 - 20 = -16$.) \ans{x = 1 \pm 2i}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Find all zeros of $f(x) = x^{3} - 2x^{2} + 9x - 18$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Group in pairs and factor:
\begin{align*}
  x^{3} - 2x^{2} + 9x - 18 &= x^{2}(x - 2) + 9(x - 4) \\
                           &= (x - 2)(x^{2} + 9)
\end{align*}
The linear factor gives one real zero; $x^{2} = -9$ gives the conjugate pair.
A cubic has three zeros, and here only one of them is real. \ans{x = 2,\ 3i,\ -3i}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Solve $x^{2} + 6x + 34 = 0$.}

\textbf{Solution:} The discriminant is $36 - 136 = -100$, so
\[
  x = \frac{-6 \pm \sqrt{-100}}{2} = \frac{-6 \pm 10i}{2}.
\]
Both parts of the numerator are divided by $2$ --- dividing only the radical
part is the usual slip here. \ans{x = -3 \pm 5i}

\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{A monic quadratic with real coefficients has $4 - i$ as a solution.
(a) Write it in standard form. (b) Justify why $4 + i$ is the other solution.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The two factors are $x - (4 - i)$ and $x - (4 + i)$.
Multiplying them, the $i$ terms in the middle cancel:
\begin{align*}
  \bigl(x - (4 - i)\bigr)\bigl(x - (4 + i)\bigr)
    &= x^{2} - 8x + (4 - i)(4 + i) \\
    &= x^{2} - 8x + (16 + 1)
\end{align*}
\ans{x^2 - 8x + 17}

\textbf{Part (b) --- instructor-judged.} A full-credit justification says that a
polynomial with real coefficients has its non-real solutions in conjugate pairs,
so the partner of $4 - i$ is $4 + i$. A justification by construction earns full
credit too: the imaginary parts cancel only when the second factor is the
conjugate, which is what makes the middle coefficient the real number $-8$.
Asserting the pair rule with no reason attached does not.
