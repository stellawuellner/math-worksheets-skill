# Evaluating Perfect Square and Cube Roots — Grade 8 / Pre-Algebra

Three PDFs are ready:

- **`ss_roots_curr202.pdf` — Skills Summary (study guide, 2 pages).** Start here. Three
  sections, each with a rule box, a worked example, and a "try it" whose answer is printed
  upside down inside the box: (1) evaluating perfect square roots, (2) evaluating perfect
  cube roots, (3) roots inside a larger expression. A watch-out box flags the single most
  common slip on this topic — the same radicand has a different square root and cube root
  ($\sqrt{64}=8$ but $\sqrt[3]{64}=4$).
- **`ws_roots_curr202.pdf` — Worksheet (4 pages, 12 problems).** A fluency ramp: four
  single-root warm-ups, then square roots, cube roots, and mixed expressions interleaved so
  your child has to decide *which* root is being asked for rather than repeating one
  skeleton. Two applications (a square patio's side from its area, a cube crate's edge from
  its volume) carry units on the answer line. Problem 11 is a comparison, and problem 12 is
  a find-the-mistake challenge with two parts.
- **`ak_roots_curr202.pdf` — Answer Key (3 pages).** Every problem is re-printed with the
  reasoning written out, not just the answer, plus a Quick Answers strip for fast grading, a
  Curriculum block (standard 8.EE.A, difficulty range), and a "Common wrong answers" note.

## What is verified, honestly

- **11 of the 12 problems are fully machine-checked.** Every root, sum, difference and
  comparison was recomputed independently with SymPy before the PDFs were allowed to
  compile; a wrong answer would have blocked the build.
- **Problem 12 is split.** Part (a) — the corrected value of $\sqrt[3]{64}$ — is
  machine-checked. Part (b) — "explain what Kara did wrong" — is **instructor-judged**: it is
  a written explanation, so no computer can mark it. The answer key prints it as `---` in the
  Quick Answers strip and gives you the grading rubric underneath: a full-credit answer must
  name the square-root-for-cube-root confusion *and* back it with a multiplication check.
- The study guide's three worked examples and three try-it answers are verified the same
  way (6 of 6 machine-checked).
- Two misconception traps were declared and proved distinguishable: multiplying a
  coefficient into a radicand (problem 8) and taking a square root where a cube root was
  asked (problem 12). The second one's wrong value, 8, is the number planted in Kara's work.

No calculator is needed anywhere — every radicand on the sheet is a perfect square or a
perfect cube.
