\noindent\small Part (a) of each problem is a computation and is machine-verified. Part (b) is a
diagnosis in words: a model answer is given, together with what to accept and what not to. The
sentence to insist on names \emph{both} the property that was assumed and the property that was
actually established.\normalsize

\problem{$MNPQ$ is a rhombus with consecutive angles $(4x+10)^\circ$ and $(6x+20)^\circ$.}
\textbf{(a) Solution:} A rhombus is a parallelogram, so consecutive angles are supplementary.
\begin{align*}
(4x + 10) + (6x + 20) &= 180 \\
10x + 30 &= 180 \\
x &= 15
\end{align*}
\ans{x = 15}
The angles are $4(15) + 10 = 70^\circ$ and $6(15) + 20 = 110^\circ$.

\textbf{(b) Manual review --- expected reasoning.} No. A square is a rhombus with right angles, so
the implication square $\Rightarrow$ rhombus runs one way only. This rhombus has a $70^\circ$
angle, so it is not a square. \emph{Accept} any answer that points at the angles (or at the
diagonals not being congruent). \emph{Do not accept} ``no, because it does not look like one'' or
an answer that treats ``all sides congruent'' as decisive.

\problem{$A(1,1)$, $B(5,3)$, $C(7,7)$, $D(3,5)$, all four sides congruent. Priya: ``a square, so
both diagonals are about $8.49$''.}
\textbf{(a) Solution:} Use the distance formula on each diagonal.
\[ AC = \sqrt{(7-1)^2 + (7-1)^2} = \sqrt{72} \qquad
   BD = \sqrt{(3-5)^2 + (5-3)^2} = \sqrt{8} \]
\ans{AC \approx 8.49} \qquad \ans{BD \approx 2.83}

\textbf{(b) Manual review --- expected reasoning.} Priya's first step is sound: four congruent
sides do force a rhombus. The step that fails is the next one --- rhombus $\Rightarrow$ square,
which is the converse of a one-way implication. The diagonals settle it: $8.49 \neq 2.83$, so
$ABCD$ is not a rectangle and therefore not a square. Most specific correct name:
\textbf{rhombus}. \emph{Do not accept} ``Priya is right, she just miscalculated'' --- the
arithmetic was never the problem.

\problem{Always true or not always true, with a counterexample where it fails.}
\textbf{Manual review --- expected answers.}

\textbf{(a) Always true.} A rectangle has two pairs of parallel sides by definition, which is the
definition of a parallelogram. (This is the ``upward'' direction of the family tree.)

\textbf{(b) Not always true.} A parallelogram needs right angles to be a rectangle. Counterexample:
the parallelogram with vertices $(0,0)$, $(5,0)$, $(7,3)$, $(2,3)$ --- opposite sides are parallel
and congruent, but no angle is $90^\circ$.

\textbf{(c) Not always true.} Perpendicular diagonals give a rhombus only if the diagonals also
\emph{bisect} each other. Counterexample: the kite with vertices $(0,5)$, $(2,0)$, $(0,-3)$,
$(-2,0)$ --- its diagonals lie on the axes and so are perpendicular, but the sides measure
$\sqrt{29}$ and $\sqrt{13}$, so they are not all congruent.

\textbf{(d) Always true.} A square is by definition a quadrilateral with four congruent sides and
four right angles; four congruent sides alone is the definition of a rhombus.

\emph{Accept} any correct counterexample (a described drawing is fine). \emph{Do not accept} a bare
``false'' with no counterexample for (b) or (c).

\problem{$A(0,6)$, $B(3,0)$, $C(0,-2)$, $D(-3,0)$. Sam: ``perpendicular diagonals, so a rhombus
with every side about $6.71$''.}
\textbf{(a) Solution:}
\[ AB = \sqrt{(3-0)^2 + (0-6)^2} = \sqrt{45} \qquad
   BC = \sqrt{(0-3)^2 + (-2-0)^2} = \sqrt{13} \]
\ans{AB \approx 6.71} \qquad \ans{BC \approx 3.61}

\textbf{(b) Manual review --- expected reasoning.} Sam is right that the diagonals are
perpendicular, but that alone is not sufficient: for a rhombus the diagonals must be perpendicular
\emph{and} bisect each other. Here the diagonal $AC$ has midpoint $(0,2)$ while $BD$ has midpoint
$(0,0)$, so they do not bisect each other --- and indeed $AB \neq BC$. $ABCD$ is a
\textbf{kite}. \emph{Accept} either the midpoint argument or the two unequal side lengths as the
disproof; the sentence must say what perpendicular diagonals alone do \emph{not} guarantee.

\problem{$PQRS$ is a parallelogram, $PR = 3x - 5$, $QS = x + 11$. Sam gets $x = 3$ and says
``square''.}
\textbf{(a) Solution:} Setting the diagonals equal is the right move; the slip is in the algebra.
Moving $-5$ across the equals sign \emph{adds} $5$:
\[ 3x - 5 = x + 11 \;\Rightarrow\; 2x = 11 + 5 = 16 \;\Rightarrow\; x = \frac{16}{2} \]
\ans{x = 8}
Sam computed $11 - 5 = 6$ and halved it, which is where the $3$ came from. Check: both diagonals
measure $3(8) - 5 = 19$ and $8 + 11 = 19$.

\textbf{(b) Manual review --- expected reasoning.} Congruent diagonals in a \emph{parallelogram}
force a \textbf{rectangle} --- that theorem is genuine. But rectangle $\Rightarrow$ square is the
converse of a one-way implication and does not follow: a $19$-unit diagonal says nothing about the
side lengths. To reach ``square'' you would need one more fact, such as two congruent adjacent
sides or perpendicular diagonals. \emph{Do not accept} ``Sam is right once $x$ is fixed''.

\problem{Challenge: $W(-1,2)$, $X(3,4)$, $Y(5,0)$, $Z(1,-2)$, all four sides congruent.}
\textbf{(a) Solution:}
\[ WX = \sqrt{4^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{20} \qquad
   WY = \sqrt{6^2 + (-2)^2} = \sqrt{40} \qquad
   XZ = \sqrt{(-2)^2 + (-6)^2} = \sqrt{40} \]
\ans{WX \approx 4.47} \qquad \ans{WY \approx 6.32} \qquad \ans{XZ \approx 6.32}

\textbf{(b) Manual review --- expected reasoning.} Four congruent sides give a rhombus; congruent
diagonals in a parallelogram give a rectangle; a quadrilateral that is both is a
\textbf{square}. Unlike problem 2, the diagonal test passes here, and that single test --- comparing
$WY$ with $XZ$ --- is exactly the one that separates ``rhombus but not square'' from ``square''.
An equivalent sufficient test is showing one angle is right, e.g.\ the slopes of $WX$ and $XY$ are
$\tfrac{1}{2}$ and $-2$. \emph{Accept} either test; the answer must name the test, not just the
shape.
