\problem{The cardioid $r = 4 + 4\cos\theta$ is graphed one point at a time.
Find the exact value of $r$ when $\theta = \pi/3$.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad $\cos\dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{1}{2}$, so
$r = 4 + 4\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right) = 4 + 2$. \ans{r = 6}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{The graph of $r = 6\cos\theta$ is a circle that sits on the polar axis.
Find $r$ when $\theta = \pi/3$.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad $r = 6\cos\dfrac{\pi}{3} = 6\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)$, and the
point $(3, \pi/3)$ lies on the circle of diameter $6$. \ans{r = 3}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Find every $\theta$ in $[0, \pi)$ for which $r = 5\sin(3\theta)$ equals zero.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad $5\sin(3\theta) = 0$ exactly when $\sin(3\theta) = 0$, i.e.
$3\theta = 0, \pi, 2\pi, \ldots$, so $\theta = 0, \dfrac{\pi}{3}, \dfrac{2\pi}{3}, \ldots$
Only the first three of these lie in $[0, \pi)$. These are the angles at which the
three-petal rose returns to the pole.
\[ \boxed{\theta = 0, \ \tfrac{\pi}{3}, \ \tfrac{2\pi}{3}} \]

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Cardioid $r = 4 - 4\sin\theta$. \ (a) Find $r$ at $\theta = 3\pi/2$.
\ (b) Explain why the curve touches the pole at exactly one angle in one full turn.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $\sin\dfrac{3\pi}{2} = -1$, so $r = 4 - 4(-1) = 4 + 4$. \ans{r = 8}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad $r = 0$ requires $4 - 4\sin\theta = 0$,
that is $\sin\theta = 1$. On $[0, 2\pi)$ the sine equals $1$ at the single angle
$\theta = \pi/2$, so the cardioid arrives at the pole once and leaves again rather than
passing through it twice. Full credit for a response that reduces to $\sin\theta = 1$ and
notes that it has exactly one solution per turn.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Find the exact value of $r$ for the limacon $r = 2 + 3\cos\theta$ at
$\theta = 2\pi/3$.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad $\cos\dfrac{2\pi}{3} = -\dfrac{1}{2}$ (second quadrant, so the
cosine is negative), giving $r = 2 + 3\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}\right) = 2 - \dfrac{3}{2}$.
\ans{r = \tfrac{1}{2}}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Rose $r = 3\cos(2\theta)$. \ (a) Find $r$ at $\theta = \pi/6$.
\ (b) Sketch the whole curve and label the petal tip on the polar axis.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $2\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$ and $\cos\dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{1}{2}$,
so $r = 3\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)$. \ans{r = \tfrac{3}{2}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad Because $n = 2$ is even, the rose has
$2n = 4$ petals, each of length $3$. One petal points along the polar axis with its tip at a
distance of $3$ from the pole; the other three tips lie a quarter turn apart. The curve
returns to the pole at $\theta = \pi/4, 3\pi/4, 5\pi/4, 7\pi/4$. Award full credit for a
freehand four-petal sketch whose axis petal is labelled with the distance $3$.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{The circle $r = 8\sin\theta$ passes through the pole. Find every $\theta$ in
$[0, 2\pi)$ for which $r = 0$.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad $8\sin\theta = 0$ when $\sin\theta = 0$, which on $[0, 2\pi)$ happens
at $\theta = 0$ and $\theta = \pi$. Both angles give the same point --- the pole --- which is
why the circle is traced completely as $\theta$ runs over only half a turn.
\[ \boxed{\theta = 0 \ \text{and} \ \theta = \pi} \]

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Limacon $r = 2 + 4\cos\theta$. \ (a) Find $r$ at $\theta = 0$.
\ (b) Find $r$ at $\theta = \pi$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $\cos 0 = 1$, so $r = 2 + 4(1)$. \ans{r = 6}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad $\cos\pi = -1$, so $r = 2 + 4(-1) = 2 - 4$. Because
$a = 2 < b = 4$, this limacon has an inner loop and its $r$-values go negative. \ans{r = -2}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{For the rose $r = 6\sin(2\theta)$, find $r$ at $\theta = \pi/8$, rounded to two
decimal places.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Double the angle first: $2\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$. Then
$r = 6\sin\dfrac{\pi}{4} = 6\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{2}}{2}\right) = 3\sqrt{2} = 4.2426\ldots$
\ans{r \approx 4.24}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Limacon $r = 1 + 3\sin\theta$. \ (a) Find $r$ at $\theta = 3\pi/2$.
\ (b) Describe where that point lands and what the negative $r$-values trace.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $\sin\dfrac{3\pi}{2} = -1$, so $r = 1 + 3(-1) = 1 - 3$. \ans{r = -2}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad A negative $r$ reverses the ray: from the
pole, face $3\pi/2$ (straight down) and walk $2$ units backwards, which lands $2$ units up,
at the point $(2, \pi/2)$. The angles where $1 + 3\sin\theta < 0$ are exactly the ones that
draw the small inner loop. Full credit requires both the reversed direction and the
identification of the inner loop.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{The circles $r = 6\cos\theta$ and $r = 6\sin\theta$ cross twice.
\ (a) Solve $6\cos\theta - 6\sin\theta = 0$ on $[0, 2\pi)$.
\ (b) Sketch both circles and mark the crossing point that is not the pole.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $6\cos\theta = 6\sin\theta \Rightarrow \tan\theta = 1$, so
$\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{4}$ or $\theta = \dfrac{5\pi}{4}$.
\[ \boxed{\theta = \tfrac{\pi}{4} \ \text{or} \ \theta = \tfrac{5\pi}{4}} \]

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad $r = 6\cos\theta$ is the circle of diameter
$6$ resting on the polar axis; $r = 6\sin\theta$ is the same circle rotated onto the vertical
axis. They meet at the pole and again on the ray $\theta = \pi/4$, where both equations give
$r = 3\sqrt{2}$. Award full credit for two correctly placed circles with the non-pole crossing
marked on the $\pi/4$ ray.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{A point is given as $(-4, \pi/6)$. \ (a) Rewrite it with a positive first coordinate
and an angle in $[0, 2\pi)$. \ (b) Explain why both pairs mark the same location.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Reversing the ray adds half a turn and makes the distance
positive: the distance is $|-4|$ and the angle is $\dfrac{\pi}{6} + \pi = \dfrac{7\pi}{6}$.
\[ \boxed{r = 4 \quad \text{and} \quad \theta = \tfrac{7\pi}{6}} \]

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad A negative first coordinate means "walk
backwards along the ray". Walking $4$ units backwards along the $\pi/6$ ray is the same
physical motion as walking $4$ units forwards along the opposite ray, $\theta = 7\pi/6$.
Full credit for any response that names the reversal of the ray as the reason.
