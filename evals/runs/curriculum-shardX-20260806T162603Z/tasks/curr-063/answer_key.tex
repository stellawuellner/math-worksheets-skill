\problem{A shop had $78$ apples and sold $43$. About how many are left, rounding each to the nearest ten?}

\textbf{Solution:} Round both, then subtract. The ones digit of $78$ is eight, so it rounds up to eighty; the ones digit of $43$ is three, so it rounds down to forty. Eighty take away forty leaves the estimate. \ans{40 \text{ apples}}

\problem{A bus has $92$ seats and $57$ people are seated. About how many are free, to the nearest ten?}

\textbf{Solution:} $92$ rounds down to ninety and $57$ rounds up to sixty. Ninety take away sixty leaves the estimate. Rounding the second number up makes the estimate a little low, which is the safe direction when you are asking whether there is room. \ans{30 \text{ seats}}

\problem{A library owns $486$ books with $218$ on loan. About how many are on the shelves, to the nearest hundred?}

\textbf{Solution:} For hundreds, look at the tens digit. $486$ has eight tens, so it rounds up to five hundred; $218$ has one ten, so it rounds down to two hundred. Five hundred take away two hundred leaves the estimate. \ans{300 \text{ books}}

\problem{A farm collected $634$ eggs and packed $297$. Mia says $437$ are left. (a) Estimate to the nearest hundred. (b) Find the exact number. (c) Is Mia's answer reasonable?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $634$ rounds down to six hundred and $297$ rounds up to three hundred, so the estimate is six hundred take away three hundred. \ans{300}

\textbf{Solution (b):} Exactly: the ones need help, so regroup twice --- five hundreds, twelve tens, fourteen ones. Ones: fourteen take seven leaves seven. Tens: twelve take nine leaves three. Hundreds: five take two leaves three. \ans{337}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} A correct response rejects the claim and uses the estimate to do it. Full credit for the reasoning that rounding says about three hundred eggs should be left, while $437$ sits more than a hundred above that --- far too big a gap for rounding alone, and exactly the size of error a lost regrouping makes. Accept the exact answer used as supporting evidence, as long as the estimate is named as what raised the doubt. Calling the claim wrong without reference to the estimate earns half credit. Accepting the claim earns no credit.

\problem{A stadium has $852$ seats and $419$ were sold. About how many were unsold, to the nearest hundred?}

\textbf{Solution:} $852$ has five tens, so it rounds up to nine hundred; $419$ has one ten, so it rounds down to four hundred. Nine hundred take away four hundred leaves the estimate. \ans{500 \text{ seats}}

\problem{A school printed $725$ flyers and handed out $348$. (a) Estimate to the nearest hundred. (b) Find the exact number left.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $725$ rounds down to seven hundred (two tens) and $348$ rounds down to three hundred (four tens). Seven hundred take away three hundred. \ans{400}

\textbf{Solution (b):} Exactly: regroup for the ones --- six hundreds, eleven tens, fifteen ones. Ones: fifteen take eight leaves seven. Tens: eleven take four leaves seven. Hundreds: six take three leaves three. The exact answer sits a little below the estimate, because the number taken away was rounded down. \ans{377}

\problem{A jar held $64$ marbles and $28$ were taken. About how many are left, to the nearest ten?}

\textbf{Solution:} $64$ rounds down to sixty and $28$ rounds up to thirty. Sixty take away thirty leaves the estimate. \ans{30 \text{ marbles}}

\problem{A warehouse held $803$ boxes and shipped $465$. Ben wrote $438$. (a) Estimate to the nearest hundred. (b) Explain why $438$ cannot be right. (c) Find the exact number.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $803$ rounds down to eight hundred and $465$ rounds up to five hundred, so the estimate is eight hundred take away five hundred. \ans{300}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} A correct response compares the written answer with the estimate and says how big the gap is. Full credit for the reasoning that the estimate is about three hundred, while $438$ is more than a hundred above it --- rounding can shift an estimate by a few tens, never by a whole hundred, so a regrouping must have gone missing. Accept an answer that names the fault directly: the hundred was opened for the empty tens column but the hundreds digit was never reduced. Saying only that $438$ looks too big, without using the estimate, earns half credit. Recomputing the difference without saying what the estimate showed earns no credit.

\textbf{Solution (c):} The ones need help and the tens are empty, so open a hundred: seven hundreds and ten tens, then nine tens and thirteen ones. Ones: thirteen take five leaves eight. Tens: nine take six leaves three. Hundreds: seven take four leaves three. \ans{338}

\problem{A cinema sold $906$ tickets over a weekend, $448$ of them on Saturday. About how many on Sunday, to the nearest hundred?}

\textbf{Solution:} $906$ has zero tens, so it rounds down to nine hundred; $448$ has four tens, so it rounds down to four hundred. Nine hundred take away four hundred leaves the estimate. \ans{500 \text{ tickets}}

\problem{Two students estimated $512 - 288$, one to the nearest ten and one to the nearest hundred. (a) Nearest-ten estimate. (b) Nearest-hundred estimate. (c) Exact difference. (d) Which estimate is closer, and why?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} To the nearest ten, $512$ becomes five hundred and ten, and $288$ becomes two hundred and ninety. Subtracting those gives the first estimate. \ans{220}

\textbf{Solution (b):} To the nearest hundred, $512$ becomes five hundred and $288$ becomes three hundred. Subtracting those gives the second estimate. \ans{200}

\textbf{Solution (c):} Exactly: regroup for the ones --- four hundreds, ten tens, twelve ones. Ones: twelve take eight leaves four. Tens: ten take eight leaves two. Hundreds: four take two leaves two. \ans{224}

\textbf{Solution (d) --- instructor-judged.} A correct response picks the nearest-ten estimate and explains the size of the rounding. Full credit for the idea that rounding to tens moves each number by only a few, while rounding to hundreds can move it by dozens, so less is thrown away and the estimate stays nearer the true answer. Accept an answer that measures both gaps and uses those numbers as the reason. Choosing correctly with no reason earns half credit. Choosing the nearest-hundred estimate, or arguing that a rounder-looking number must be more accurate, earns no credit.
