Here are the three PDFs for the slope / parallel / perpendicular set:

- **Worksheet** — `ws_slopes_curr327.pdf` (5 pages)
- **Answer key** — `ak_slopes_curr327.pdf` (3 pages)
- **Study guide** — `ss_slopes_curr327.pdf` (2 pages)

**What the worksheet covers.** Ten problems on using slopes to establish that
lines are parallel or perpendicular. It is built for fluency: three quick
slope-from-two-points warm-ups (including a vertical line, whose slope is
undefined), then two full parallel/perpendicular decisions, two one-line
"slope of any line parallel/perpendicular to..." items, and three
figure-classification problems — a parallelogram, a right triangle, and a
closing quadrilateral that turns out to be a rectangle because its opposite
sides are parallel *and* its adjacent sides are perpendicular.

The four skills are interleaved rather than blocked after the warm-up, so the
student has to decide each time whether equality of slopes or a product of −1 is
the test that applies.

**What is verified, honestly.** The verification file holds 22 entries — one per
response the sheet asks for.

- **17 entries are machine-checked** with SymPy: every slope on the sheet
  (2/3, −3/5, undefined, 2 and 2, 1/2 and −2, −3/4, −1/2, 1/3, 1/3, 3, 1/3, −3,
  −1/2, −1/2, 2).
- **5 entries are instructor-judged**: the "state and justify" part of problems
  4, 5, 9 and the classification arguments in 8 and 10. These are written
  reasoning, so the key prints a grading rubric for each — including the extra
  slope the student must compute to complete the argument.

**5 of the 10 problems are fully machine-checked end to end**; the other five
each end in a justification only a person can mark. The key's "What is verified"
note names problems 4, 5, 8, 9 and 10, and the Quick Answers bank shows `---` in
exactly those slots. Nothing is marked `[unchecked]`.

Three problems carry declared misconception traps, so the key's "Common wrong
answers" block identifies a student who wrote run over rise (1.5), lost a minus
sign by subtracting in opposite orders (0.6), or took the reciprocal without
negating it (0.5).

**Study guide.** Four sections — slope from two points, the parallel test, the
perpendicular test, and classifying a quadrilateral from its four side slopes —
each with a rule box, a worked example that opens with the reasoning, and a
try-it whose answer is printed upside down. The watch-out box covers the single
most common perpendicular error: doing only one half of "flip and negate".
