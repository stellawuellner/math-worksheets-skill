\problem{\textbf{Solve $x^2 = 49$; is $x = 7$ complete?}\\
Step 1: rewrite so one side is zero --- $x^2 - 49 = 0$ --- because every method
in this unit reads a quadratic in that form.\\
Step 2: this is a difference of squares: $(x - 7)(x + 7) = 0$. The zero-product
property gives $x - 7 = 0$ or $x + 7 = 0$.\\
Step 3: both factors are legitimate, so
$\ans{x = -7 \text{ or } x = 7}$. Toby's set is \emph{incomplete}: he took the
principal square root of $49$ and stopped, but $(-7)^2 = 49$ as well.\\
\emph{Teaching point:} the square-root step is where roots go missing. Writing
$x = \pm\sqrt{49}$ keeps both.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Jae divides $x^2 = 3x$ by $x$.}\\
Step 1: name the assumption. Dividing both sides by $x$ is only legal when
$x \neq 0$, and nothing in the equation says that. Jae divided away the very
solution $x = 0$.\\
Step 2: solve correctly by moving everything to one side and factoring:
$x^2 - 3x = 0$, so $x(x - 3) = 0$.\\
Step 3: the zero-product property gives $x = 0$ or $x - 3 = 0$, so
$\ans{x = 0 \text{ or } x = 3}$.\\
\emph{Teaching point:} never divide both sides by an expression containing the
variable. Factor instead --- factoring keeps every root, division destroys one.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Rio's discriminant for $3x^2 - 7x - 6 = 0$.}\\
Step 1: list the coefficients exactly as the equation writes them, signs
included: $a = 3$, $b = -7$, $c = -6$. Rio substituted $c$ as $+6$ --- he took
the number and dropped its sign.\\
Step 2: with the correct $c$, the term $-4ac$ becomes
$-4(3)(-6) = +72$: a negative $c$ makes that term \emph{add}, which is exactly
the case Rio's shortcut cannot survive.\\
Step 3: $b^2 - 4ac = (-7)^2 + 72 = 49 + 72 = \ans{121}$.\\
Step 4: $121 > 0$ and is a perfect square, so the equation has two distinct
rational solutions --- the opposite of Rio's conclusion. (For the record they are
$x = 3$ and $x = -\frac{2}{3}$.)\\
\emph{Watch for:} $-23$, which is $49 - 72$. A negative discriminant is the
signature of this slip, and it wrongly reports ``no real solutions'' on an equation
that factors.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Mia's numerator on $2x^2 - 4x - 3 = 0$.}\\
Step 1: state the rule. The formula's numerator opens with $-b$, which means
\emph{the opposite of} $b$ --- not "a minus sign in front of the number you see".
Here $b = -4$, so $-b = +4$.\\
Step 2: with $a = 2$, $b = -4$, $c = -3$:
$b^2 - 4ac = 16 + 24 = 40$, and $\sqrt{40} = 2\sqrt{10}$.\\
Step 3: $x = \dfrac{4 \pm 2\sqrt{10}}{4} = 1 \pm \dfrac{\sqrt{10}}{2}$, so the
larger root is $\ans{x = 1 + \dfrac{\sqrt{10}}{2}}$, about $2.58$.\\
\emph{Watch for:} $0.58$. That is Mia's value --- her numerator started at $-4$
instead of $+4$, so her root is exactly $2$ less than the true one. A quick
substitution check catches it: her value does not satisfy the original equation.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Sam square-roots $(x - 3)^2 = 25$.}\\
Step 1: the lost line is the square-root step. $\sqrt{(x-3)^2} = |x - 3|$, so the
correct line reads $x - 3 = \pm 5$, not $x - 3 = 5$.\\
Step 2: split into the two branches: $x - 3 = 5$ gives $x = 8$; $x - 3 = -5$
gives $x = -2$. Sam kept the first branch and dropped the second.\\
Step 3: the complete solution set is $\ans{x = -2 \text{ or } x = 8}$.\\
Step 4: check both in the original: $(8-3)^2 = 25$ $\checkmark$ and
$(-2-3)^2 = 25$ $\checkmark$.\\
\emph{Teaching point:} expanding instead would give $x^2 - 6x - 16 = 0$ and
factor to $(x-8)(x+2)$ --- the same two roots. Two methods, one complete set.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Kai reads $(2x + 3)(x - 4) = 0$ off the page.}\\
Step 1: first mistake --- \emph{sign}. Setting a factor to zero flips the sign of
the constant: $x - 4 = 0$ gives $x = +4$, not $-4$, and $2x + 3 = 0$ gives a
negative $x$, not $+3$. Kai copied the numbers and kept their printed signs.\\
Step 2: second mistake --- \emph{an unfinished step}. The factor $2x + 3$ has a
coefficient, so solving it takes two moves: $2x = -3$, then divide by $2$.\\
Step 3: the solutions are $2x + 3 = 0 \Rightarrow x = -\tfrac{3}{2}$ and
$x - 4 = 0 \Rightarrow x = 4$, so
$\ans{x = -\tfrac{3}{2} \text{ or } x = 4}$.\\
\emph{Teaching point:} Kai's answers are each the negative of a true root or an
unfinished one. Substituting $x = 3$ gives $(9)(-1) = -9 \neq 0$, which is a
two-second refutation.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: is $4x^2 - 12x + 9 = 0$ really two-rooted?}\\
\textbf{(a)} Step 1: read the coefficients with their signs: $a = 4$, $b = -12$,
$c = 9$. The discriminant decides how many real solutions exist before any root
is computed.\\
Step 2: $b^2 - 4ac = (-12)^2 - 4(4)(9) = 144 - 144$, so
$\ans{\Delta = 0}$.\\
\textbf{(b)} Step 3: a zero discriminant means the $\pm$ contributes nothing, so
there is exactly \emph{one} distinct real solution (a double root):
$x = \dfrac{-b}{2a} = \dfrac{12}{8}$, giving $\ans{x = \tfrac{3}{2}}$.\\
Step 4: confirm by factoring --- $4x^2 - 12x + 9 = (2x - 3)^2$, a perfect square,
so the only zero is where $2x - 3 = 0$.\\
Step 5: the student's rule is wrong twice over. Roots do not come in
positive/negative pairs (that only happens when $b = 0$), and a quadratic has
two, one, or no real solutions depending on the sign of the discriminant.\\
\emph{Watch for:} $288$, from $(-12)^2 + 4(4)(9)$ --- adding $4ac$ instead of
subtracting it. That turns a perfect square into a two-root equation and hides
the double root entirely.}

\problem{\textbf{Explain: why $\pm$ is not optional.}\\
\emph{Open response --- flagged for manual review. Model answer below; accept any
equivalent reasoning.}\\
\textbf{(a)} Squaring destroys sign information: a number and its opposite have
the same square, so an equation of the form $u^2 = k$ (with $k > 0$) has two
solutions, $u = \sqrt{k}$ and $u = -\sqrt{k}$. Writing only $u = \sqrt{k}$ keeps
the branch you happened to think of and silently discards the other. The honest
step is $u = \pm\sqrt{k}$, or equivalently $|u| = \sqrt{k}$.\\
\textbf{(b)} The same $\pm$ sits in the numerator of
$x = \dfrac{-b \pm \sqrt{b^2 - 4ac}}{2a}$. It is what the square-root step in
completing the square left behind, and it is what produces two roots: one from
the plus branch, one from the minus branch. When $b^2 - 4ac = 0$ the two branches
land on the same number, which is why a double root is one solution, not two.\\
\textbf{(c)} Model habit: ``Before I hand it in, I count my solutions against the
discriminant, and I substitute every root back into the original equation. If I
have fewer roots than the discriminant predicts, I go back to the line where I
took a square root or divided by something containing $x$.''\\
\emph{What a full-credit answer must contain:} the observation that two numbers
share one square, the identification of $\pm$ in the formula as the source of the
second root, the discriminant-zero case named as the exception, and a habit that
is a check rather than a restatement of the rule.}
