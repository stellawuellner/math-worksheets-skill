\problem{\textbf{Graph A: how many books did Ana read?}\\
Step 1: find what one step is worth before reading any bar. The numbers up the
side go $0$, $2$, $4$, $6$, $8$, $10$, $12$, so each step is worth $2$ books,
not $1$.\\
Step 2: Ana's bar reaches the line labelled $8$.\\
Step 3: Ana read $\ans{8}$ books.\\
\emph{Watch for:} the answer $4$. That is the number of \emph{steps} in the bar.
Each step is two books, so four steps is eight books.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Nia said Ben read $3$ books.}\\
Step 1: name what Nia did. She counted the steps in Ben's bar and called each
step one book. Counting steps is fine --- calling each step one book is the
mistake.\\
Step 2: one step is worth $2$ books, because the side numbers climb $0$, $2$,
$4$, $6$ and so on.\\
Step 3: Ben's bar is $3$ steps tall, and $3$ steps of $2$ books each is
$3 \times 2$, so Ben read $\ans{6}$ books. Reading the side directly gives the
same answer: his bar stops at the line labelled $6$.\\
\emph{Full-credit answer says:} ``one step is $2$ books, so Ben read $6$.''}

\problem{\textbf{Graph A: how many more books did Cruz read than Dee?}\\
Step 1: read each bar off the side scale first. Cruz's bar reaches $12$; Dee's
bar reaches $4$.\\
Step 2: ``how many more'' means subtract: $12 - 4$.\\
Step 3: Cruz read $\ans{8}$ more books than Dee.\\
\emph{Watch for:} the answer $4$. That is the number of grid steps between the
two bar tops. Four steps of two books each is eight books --- the steps have to
be turned into books before they mean anything.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Sam said the most anyone read is $6$.}\\
Step 1: name what Sam did. He counted the steps in the tallest bar. The tallest
bar is Cruz's, and it is $6$ steps tall --- so Sam's counting was right and his
reading was wrong.\\
Step 2: each step is worth $2$ books, so $6$ steps is $6 \times 2$ books. Or
simply read the side: the bar stops at the line labelled $12$.\\
Step 3: the most books anyone read is $\ans{12}$.\\
\emph{Full-credit answer says:} ``Sam counted steps, not books.'' A quick check:
Cruz's bar is three times as tall as Dee's, and $12$ is three times $4$, so the
answer is sensible.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Rae said $5$ boxes had $5$ pencils.}\\
Step 1: name what Rae did. She read the number written under the marks and used
it twice. On a line plot those two numbers do different jobs: the number under
the line says \emph{what} was counted (how many pencils were in the box), and
the marks say \emph{how many} boxes were like that.\\
Step 2: count the marks stacked above the $5$: there are four of them.\\
Step 3: $\ans{4}$ boxes had $5$ pencils in them.\\
\emph{Full-credit answer says:} ``the $5$ under the line is the number of
pencils; the marks tell how many boxes.''}

\problem{\textbf{Graph B: which number of pencils happened most often?}\\
Step 1: ``happened most often'' means find the tallest stack of marks. The stack
above $5$ has four marks; the stacks above $4$, $6$ and $7$ have two, three and
one.\\
Step 2: the tallest stack is the one above $5$. Now answer the question that was
actually asked --- which \emph{number of pencils}, not how many marks.\\
Step 3: the number of pencils that happened most often is $\ans{5}$.\\
\emph{Watch for:} the answer $4$. That is how many marks are in the tallest
stack. It answers ``how many boxes'', which is a different question.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Theo's side counts.}\\
Step 1: count the straight sides in the picture, shape by shape. A: $3$ sides
(triangle). B: $4$ sides (rectangle). C: $5$ sides (pentagon). D: $6$ sides
(hexagon).\\
Step 2: compare with Theo's list. He wrote C as $6$ and D as $5$ --- those two
counts are swapped. A and B are correct.\\
Step 3: shape C has $\ans{5}$ straight sides.\\
\emph{Watch for:} the answer $6$, which is Theo's swapped number --- it belongs
to shape D. Counting corners is a good double-check here: each of these shapes
has exactly as many corners as it has straight sides.}

\problem{\textbf{Show what you know: a four-sided shape that is not a square.}\\
\emph{Open task --- flagged for manual review. A model answer is below; accept
any correct drawing and any equivalent sentence.}\\
\textbf{(a)} Model drawing: a rectangle six grid squares wide and two grid
squares tall. It has four straight sides and four square corners, so it is a
quadrilateral --- but the sides are not all the same length.\\
Other correct drawings: any rectangle that is longer than it is wide; a
parallelogram with slanted corners; a trapezoid; a four-sided shape with four
different side lengths.\\
\textbf{(b)} Model sentence: ``A square needs all four sides to be the same
length, and my shape has two long sides and two short sides.'' A sentence about
corners also earns credit if the drawing has a slanted corner: ``A square needs
four square corners, and mine are slanted.''\\
\emph{What a full-credit answer must contain:} a closed drawing with exactly
four straight sides that is genuinely not a square, and a sentence that names an
\emph{attribute} a square must have --- equal sides, or square corners --- rather
than just saying ``it looks different.''}
