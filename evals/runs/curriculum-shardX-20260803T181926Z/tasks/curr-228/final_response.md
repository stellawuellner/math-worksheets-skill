Here are three PDFs on solving systems of two linear equations algebraically.

**Worksheet (6 pages, 10 problems).** Every problem is a real situation — a
ticket sale, a garden's perimeter, a boat against a current, a cafe order, two
gym memberships, crates of fruit — and each one names what $x$ and $y$ stand for,
with units, and prints the system beneath the story so the algebra is the task
rather than the translation. The three methods are interleaved rather than
blocked:

- **Substitution** (1, 2, 7), including the two-expressions-for-$y$ case where
  the gym plans are set equal to each other.
- **Elimination by adding or subtracting** (3, 4), where the coefficients are
  already opposite or already identical and nothing needs multiplying.
- **Elimination after scaling** (5, 6, 8, 9, 10), from scaling one equation up
  to scaling both to a common multiple.

Problem 9 is the special case: two receipts from the same shop that cannot both
be right. Elimination collapses to $0 = 4$, and the student has to say what that
means — parallel lines, no solution. Problem 10 is the challenge: solve the
system, then test a third claim against the prices found and explain why three
equations in two unknowns can have no common solution.

**Answer key (3 pages).** Each solution names the method and says why it was
chosen before doing any arithmetic — which coefficient made adding rather than
subtracting the right move, which equation was scaled and by what — then finds
one variable, substitutes back, and checks the pair in both original equations.
Where an alternative route exists (eliminating the other variable, multiplying
by 5 instead of 3) the key says so.

**Study guide (2 pages).** Three sections — substitution, elimination by
adding or subtracting, and elimination after scaling — each with a rule box that
says when to reach for that method, a worked example, and a try-it with the
answer upside down inside the box. The closing watch-out box covers the two
outcomes students misread as errors: $0 = 4$ means no solution, $0 = 0$ means
infinitely many.

**Verification.** All ten systems were solved independently with SymPy and every
answer pair was confirmed against both equations, including problem 9, where the
checker confirms the system is genuinely inconsistent rather than merely
unsolved. The six study-guide boxes were verified the same way.

**Flagged for manual review:** problem 10 part (b) is an open justification —
there is no single value to compute, so it is marked for a human to read. The
answer key sets out what a full answer contains and which alternative arguments
should be accepted.

Standard tagged: 8.EE.C.8 (8.EE.C.8a–c). Difficulty ramps 1 to 5 across the
sheet.
