\noindent\small\textbf{The data.} Practice hours $x$: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8.
Exam scores $y$: 61, 61, 67, 79, 85, 85, 91, 103. Least-squares line
$\hat{y} = 52 + 6x$, correlation $r = 0.977$.\normalsize
\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{\textbf{Predict at $x = 5$ hours.}
\par\smallskip
A prediction from a regression line is a substitution, nothing more: put the
$x$-value into the equation the software returned.
\[
\hat{y} = 52 + 6(5) = 52 + 30
\]
The model predicts 82 points for a student practicing 5 hours per week.
\ans{\hat{y} = 82}

Note the hat: $\hat{y}$ is the model's prediction, not any student's score. The
next two problems turn on keeping those two numbers apart.}

\problem{\textbf{Read the observed score at $x = 5$ hours.}
\par\smallskip
Find the column headed 5 in the table and read the score beneath it. This is a
measurement, not a prediction.
\ans{85 \text{ points}}}

\problem{\textbf{Residual at $x = 5$ hours.}
\par\smallskip
A residual is always \emph{observed minus predicted}, in that order, so its sign
carries meaning:
\[
e = y - \hat{y} = 85 - 82
\]
\ans{3 \text{ points}}

The residual is positive, so the actual score sits \emph{above} the line: the
model \textbf{under-predicted} this student by 3 points.
\par\smallskip
\emph{Common wrong turn:} predicted minus observed gives $-3$ and reverses every
interpretation --- a positive residual would then mean over-prediction.}

\problem{\textbf{Coefficient of determination from $r = 0.977$.}
\par\smallskip
The coefficient of determination is the square of the correlation:
\[
r^2 = (0.977)^2 = 0.954529\ldots
\]
Rounded to three decimal places: \ans{r^2 \approx 0.955}

\textbf{Interpretation:} about 95.5 percent of the variation in exam scores is
accounted for by the linear relationship with practice hours. Say ``accounted
for,'' not ``caused by'' --- see problem 10.
\par\smallskip
\emph{Common wrong turn:} taking a square root instead of squaring gives 0.988,
which is larger than $r$ and therefore impossible: for $|r| \le 1$, squaring
always moves the value toward zero.}

\problem{\textbf{Predict at $x = 8$ hours, and compare.}
\par\smallskip
\[
\hat{y} = 52 + 6(8) = 52 + 48
\]
\ans{\hat{y} = 100}

The observed score at 8 hours was 103 points, so the residual is
$103 - 100 = 3$ points: this student also beat the model, by the same margin as
the 5-hour student. Both points sit above the line, which is visible on the
scatter plot.}

\problem{\textbf{Fraction of points above the line.}
\par\smallskip
Compute each residual $y - \hat{y}$ in order: at $x = 1$ through $x = 8$ the
predictions are 58, 64, 70, 76, 82, 88, 94, 100, so the residuals are
$+3, -3, -3, +3, +3, -3, -3, +3$. Four are positive.
\[
\frac{4}{8} = \ans{\tfrac{1}{2}}
\]
\textbf{Why this is expected:} the least-squares line is fitted so that the
residuals sum to zero. The positive and negative residuals must therefore
balance, which puts roughly half the points above the line and half below. A
line with, say, seven of eight points above it could not be the least-squares
line.
\par\smallskip
\emph{Grading note:} accept any explanation resting on ``the residuals sum to
zero'' or ``the line passes through the middle of the data.'' Exactly half is
not guaranteed in general --- roughly half is the claim.}

\problem{\textbf{Mean score, and the line at the mean.}
\par\smallskip
Add the eight scores and divide by 8:
\[
\frac{61 + 61 + 67 + 79 + 85 + 85 + 91 + 103}{8} = \frac{632}{8}
\]
\ans{\bar{y} = 79 \text{ points}}

Now substitute the mean practice time: $\hat{y} = 52 + 6(4.5) = 52 + 27 = 79$
points --- exactly the mean score.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{The property confirmed:} every least-squares line passes through the
point of averages $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$. That is a theorem about the fit, not a
coincidence of this data set, and it gives a fast check on any regression
equation a calculator hands back.}

\problem{\textbf{Percent of variation left unexplained.}
\par\smallskip
$r^2 = 0.955$ means 95.5 percent of the variation in scores is accounted for by
the model. What is left over is everything else:
\[
100 - 100(0.955) = 100 - 95.5
\]
\ans{4.5\%}

So 4.5 percent of the variation in exam scores is not explained by practice
hours --- it comes from prior knowledge, test-day conditions, teaching, and
ordinary measurement noise.
\par\smallskip
\emph{Common wrong turn:} answering 95.5 percent reports the explained share and
answers the opposite question.}

\problem{\textbf{Extrapolate to $x = 20$ hours.}
\par\smallskip
\[
\hat{y} = 52 + 6(20) = 52 + 120
\]
\ans{\hat{y} = 172}

\textbf{What is wrong with it:} the exam is out of 120 points, so a predicted
score of 172 is impossible. The arithmetic is correct; the model is being used
where it has no evidence. The data only covers 1 to 8 hours, and nothing in it
says the relationship stays linear beyond that --- in reality returns flatten
and the ceiling of 120 points binds.}

\problem{\textbf{Extrapolation and causation.} \emph{(Open response --- flagged
for manual review.)}
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Model answer, part (a).} The line was fitted only to practice times
between 1 and 8 hours, so it describes the pattern in that window and nothing
else. Problem 9 shows the consequence: at 20 hours it returns 172 points on a
120-point exam, an impossible value. A regression model carries no information
about behaviour outside the observed range, so predictions there are
unsupported no matter how well the line fits inside it.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Model answer, part (b).} A correlation of 0.977 says the points lie very
close to a straight line; it says nothing about what produced the pattern. This
was an observational study --- nobody assigned students their practice hours ---
so a lurking third variable can explain the whole relationship. \emph{Example
confounder:} prior mathematical preparation. Students who already understand the
material find practice worthwhile and also score well, so preparation drives both
variables at once. Other acceptable answers: motivation, hours of sleep, access
to a tutor, or the teacher a student happens to have.
\par\smallskip
\emph{Design that would settle it:} a randomized controlled experiment. Recruit
students, randomly assign each to a required number of practice hours, and
compare mean exam scores across the assigned groups. Random assignment balances
prior preparation and every other lurking variable across the groups on average,
so a difference in outcomes can be attributed to the practice itself.
\par\smallskip
\emph{Grading note:} part (a) needs the observed range 1 to 8 hours named and the
impossible value used as evidence. Part (b) needs three things: the statement
that correlation does not establish causation, one \emph{plausible} confounder
that could affect both variables, and a design with \emph{random assignment}.
A larger sample or a stronger correlation is not a fix, and an answer proposing
one should be corrected --- more data from an observational study cannot remove
a confounder.}
