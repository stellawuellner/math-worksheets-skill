\problem{\textbf{Ana's stickers: $5 + 5 + 5 + 5$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Count how many fives are being added. There are four of them,
and every group holds the same amount --- that is what makes a multiplication
possible.

\textbf{Step 2.} Four groups of five is written $4 \times 5$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Count on by fives: $5, 10, 15, 20$.

\ans{4 \times 5 = 20}

\textit{Listen for:} ``$4 + 5$''. Ask how many groups there are and how big
each group is; the two numbers play different jobs.}

\problem{\textbf{Array of dots: 3 rows of 6.}

\textbf{Step 1.} An array is read as \emph{rows} $\times$ \emph{how many in
each row}, so count the rows first instead of counting every dot.

\textbf{Step 2.} There are $3$ rows, and each row holds $6$ dots, so the
multiplication is $3 \times 6$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Three sixes: $6, 12, 18$.

\ans{3 \times 6 = 18}

\textit{Teaching note:} counting the dots one at a time gives the same answer
and is worth allowing once --- then ask which way was faster.}

\problem{\textbf{Write $7 + 7 + 7$ as a multiplication.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The same number, $7$, is added three times, so this is three
equal groups.

\textbf{Step 2.} Three groups of seven is $3 \times 7$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $7 + 7 = 14$, and $14 + 7 = 21$.

\ans{3 \times 7 = 21}

\textit{Listen for:} $7 \times 3$ written instead. That is the same total, and
saying why (three sevens and seven threes both make $21$) is a good answer.}

\problem{\textbf{Array of stars: 4 rows of 7 --- two multiplications.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Reading across the rows: $4$ rows with $7$ stars in each row.

\textbf{Step 2.} That is $4 \times 7$. Four sevens: $7, 14, 21, 28$.

\ans{4 \times 7 = 28}

\textbf{Step 3.} Now read the same picture down the columns. There are $7$
columns with $4$ stars in each, which is $7 \times 4$ --- the same array, so
the same total.

\ans{7 \times 4 = 28}

\textit{Why this matters:} an array can be read two ways, which is exactly why
$4 \times 7$ and $7 \times 4$ are equal. Not a rule to memorise --- a picture
to turn.}

\problem{\textbf{Marco's marbles: $6 \times 3$ as repeated addition.}

\textbf{Step 1.} $6 \times 3$ means \emph{6 groups of 3}, so the number that
repeats is $3$ and it repeats $6$ times.

\textbf{Step 2.} $3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 + 3$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Add in pairs: $3 + 3 = 6$, and there are three pairs, so
$6 + 6 + 6 = 18$.

\ans{6 \times 3 = 18}

\textit{Listen for:} $6 + 6 + 6$ (that is $3 \times 6$). It has the same total,
but it is the other picture --- three bags of six, not six bags of three.}

\problem{\textbf{$4 \times \underline{\hspace{1cm}} = 24$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The missing number tells how big each of the $4$ groups is, so
ask: four of what makes $24$?

\textbf{Step 2.} Count by fours and watch for $24$: $4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24$ ---
that is six fours.

\ans{4 \times 6 = 24}

\textit{Check it:} $6 + 6 + 6 + 6 = 24$ $\checkmark$ Splitting $24$ into four
equal groups is the same question, which is why this is also
$24 \div 4$.}

\problem{\textbf{Muffin tray: 6 rows of 4.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Rows first: the tray has $6$ rows, and each row holds $4$
muffins, so the multiplication is $6 \times 4$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Count by fours six times: $4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24$.

\ans{6 \times 4 = 24}

\textit{Teaching note:} a student who knows $4 \times 6 = 24$ may use it here
--- turning the tray sideways is a legitimate strategy, not a shortcut to
discourage.}

\problem{\textbf{Nia's jumps: $6 + 6 + 6 + 6 + 6$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Count the jumps, not the sixes on the page one by one: there
are $5$ jumps, and every jump is $6$ long.

\textbf{Step 2.} Five groups of six is $5 \times 6$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Count by sixes: $6, 12, 18, 24, 30$.

\ans{5 \times 6 = 30}

\textit{Listen for:} landing on $36$ --- one jump too many. Touching each $6$
while counting fixes it.}

\problem{\textbf{$\underline{\hspace{1cm}} \times 5 = 35$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Here the missing number is how many groups there are, and each
group holds $5$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Count by fives until $35$: $5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35$ ---
that took seven fives.

\ans{7 \times 5 = 35}

\textit{Sentence:} seven groups of $5$ makes $35$.}

\problem{\textbf{Ben wrote $4 + 6 = 10$ for $4 \times 6$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} $\times$ and $+$ ask different questions. $4 + 6$ puts two
piles together once; $4 \times 6$ makes \emph{4 groups} that each hold $6$.

\textbf{Step 2.} As a repeated addition: $6 + 6 + 6 + 6$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $6 + 6 = 12$, and $12 + 12 = 24$.

\ans{4 \times 6 = 24}

\textit{What Ben did wrong:} he added the two numbers instead of making four
groups of six, which is why his answer, $10$, is so much smaller than the real
product.}

\problem{\textbf{4 rows of 6, then one more row.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Adding a row does not change how many tiles sit in a row, so
the row size stays $6$ and only the number of rows changes.

\textbf{Step 2.} (a) $4$ rows and one more row makes $5$ rows.

\textbf{Step 3.} (b) $5$ rows of $6$ is $5 \times 6 = 30$ tiles.

\ans{5 \times 6 = 30}

\textit{Another good route:} the old array held $4 \times 6 = 24$ tiles, and
the new row adds $6$ more: $24 + 6 = 30$ $\checkmark$}

\problem{\textbf{Challenge: Kai's $32$ tiles.}

\textbf{Step 1.} (a) Each row holds $8$ tiles, so ask how many eights make
$32$: $8, 16, 24, 32$ --- four eights.

\ans{4 \text{ rows}}

\textbf{Step 2.} (b) Four rows of eight, written as repeated addition:
$8 + 8 + 8 + 8 = 32$.

\textbf{Step 3.} (c) Now each row holds only $4$ tiles, so the rows must be
shorter and there must be more of them. Count by fours: $4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24,
28, 32$ --- eight fours.

\ans{8 \text{ rows}}

\textit{Worth pointing out:} the same $32$ tiles made $4$ rows of $8$ and then
$8$ rows of $4$. Halving the row size doubled the number of rows.}
