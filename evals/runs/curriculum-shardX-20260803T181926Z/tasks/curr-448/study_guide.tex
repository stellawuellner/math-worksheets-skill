\sstitleblock{Interpreting Correlation and Least-Squares Regression}

\noindent\small A least-squares line $\hat{y} = a + bx$ is the line that makes
the sum of the squared residuals as small as possible. Three facts follow and
drive everything below: the residuals sum to zero, the line passes through
$(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$, and $r^2$ measures how much of the variation in $y$ the
line accounts for. The hat on $\hat{y}$ always means \emph{predicted}, never
observed.\normalsize

\skillheading{Skill 1 --- Predicting from the regression line}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Rule:} substitute the $x$-value into $\hat{y} = a + bx$. The slope $b$
is the predicted change in $y$ for a one-unit increase in $x$; the intercept $a$
is the predicted $y$ at $x = 0$, which is meaningful only if $x = 0$ is inside
the observed range. Always state the prediction with its units.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:}\quad A study reports $\hat{y} = 20 + 4x$, where $x$ is hours
of training and $y$ is a fitness score. Predict the score at 6 hours.
\step{A prediction is a substitution into the fitted equation --- no data point
is needed, and none is being claimed.}
\step{$\hat{y} = 20 + 4(6) = 20 + 24$.}

$\ans{44}$ points is the predicted score at 6 hours.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:}\quad For $\hat{y} = 15 + 3x$, predict $y$ when $x = 9$.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{42}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{Skill 2 --- Residuals: observed minus predicted}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Rule:} residual $e = y - \hat{y}$, in that order. A \textbf{positive}
residual means the point sits above the line and the model under-predicted; a
\textbf{negative} residual means it sits below and the model over-predicted. The
residuals of a least-squares fit always sum to zero, so about half are positive.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:}\quad At $x = 6$ the model above predicts 44 and the person
actually scored 50. Find and interpret the residual.
\step{The question compares one real observation with the line, so it is a
residual --- subtract the prediction from the observation, in that order.}
\step{$e = 50 - 44$, which is positive.}

$\ans{6}$ --- the point is 6 above the line, so the model under-predicted.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:}\quad Another person scored 38 where the model predicted 42.
Find the residual and say what its sign means.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{-4}$, over-predicted}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{Skill 3 --- Reading r and r-squared}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Rule:} $r$ runs from $-1$ to $1$: its \emph{sign} gives the direction and
its \emph{size} the strength of the linear pattern. Squaring gives
$r^2$, the fraction of the variation in $y$ accounted for by the line; the rest,
$1 - r^2$, is unexplained. Because $|r| \le 1$, squaring always moves the number
toward zero, so $r^2$ can never exceed $|r|$.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:}\quad Software reports $r = 0.86$. Find $r^2$ to two decimal
places and say what it means.
\step{The question asks how much variation the model accounts for, and that is
$r^2$ --- so square the correlation rather than reading $r$ directly.}
\step{$r^2 = (0.86)^2 = 0.7396$, which rounds to two places.}

$\ans{0.74}$ --- about 74 percent of the variation in $y$ is accounted for, and
26 percent is not.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:}\quad A different study reports $r = -0.60$. Find $r^2$ and say
what the negative sign told you that $r^2$ does not.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{0.36}$; only $r$ shows the direction}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{Skill 4 --- When not to trust the model}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Rule:} two limits no value of $r$ can remove.
\textbf{Extrapolation:} a line describes only the range of $x$ actually
observed; outside it the prediction has no evidence and often no meaning.
\textbf{Causation:} in an observational study a strong correlation can be
produced entirely by a lurking third variable. Only random assignment supports
a causal claim.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:}\quad The fitness model $\hat{y} = 20 + 4x$ was fitted for
$x$ between 1 and 8 hours, and scores are capped at 100. What does it predict at
40 hours, and why reject it?
\step{Checking an extrapolation means computing the prediction and then testing
it against what the context allows.}
\step{$\hat{y} = 20 + 4(40) = 20 + 160$, far past the 100-point ceiling.}

$\ans{180}$ --- impossible, so the model must not be used at 40 hours.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:}\quad For $\hat{y} = 15 + 3x$, fitted on $x$ from 2 to 10 and
capped at 100, find the prediction at $x = 50$ and say why it is
unusable.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{165}$, past the ceiling and far
outside the fitted range}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} A high $r^2$ says the line fits the observed points, not
that the model is right. It cannot detect a lurking variable, it cannot justify
a prediction outside the observed range, and it does not become causal evidence
when the sample gets larger. Write ``accounted for by,'' never ``caused by,''
when interpreting $r^2$.
\end{watchoutbox}
