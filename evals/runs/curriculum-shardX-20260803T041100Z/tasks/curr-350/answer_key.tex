\noindent\small\textbf{How to grade this sheet.} Six of the ten problems have no single
correct answer, only a correct \emph{plan} or \emph{argument}, and each one below lists what
must be present for full credit. The recurring gap is a plan whose arcs have no stated
centre or radius, and a justification that appeals to the picture instead of to equal radii.
The four coordinate problems are the antidote: they hand back the output of a construction
and ask the student to test the very property the construction promised.\normalsize

\problem{\textbf{Plan: perpendicular bisector of $AB$.}\par
\textbf{Full-credit plan:}
\begin{itemize}[label={$\bullet$}, leftmargin=1.1cm, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Open the compass to any radius greater than half of $AB$. Place the point on $A$
        and draw arcs above and below the segment.
  \item \emph{Without changing the opening}, place the point on $B$ and draw arcs above and
        below, crossing the first two. Call the crossings $M$ and $N$.
  \item Lay the straightedge on $M$ and $N$ and draw the line. That line is the
        perpendicular bisector of $AB$.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{The condition on the opening:} the radius must be more than half of $AB$. If it is
exactly half, the arcs touch at one point (the midpoint) and give no second point to draw a
line through; if it is less than half, the arcs never meet and there is nothing to join.\par
\textbf{Grading:} step 2 must say the opening is unchanged, and the crossing points must be
named on \emph{both} sides. A plan that draws one arc on each side gets only one crossing and
cannot determine a line.\par
Open plan --- $\ans{\text{open response}}$.}

\problem{\textbf{Plan: bisector of angle $DEF$.}\par
\textbf{Full-credit plan:}
\begin{itemize}[label={$\bullet$}, leftmargin=1.1cm, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Put the compass point on the vertex $E$ and draw an arc of any radius crossing both
        rays. Call the crossings $P$ (on $ED$) and $Q$ (on $EF$).
  \item Put the compass point on $P$ and draw an arc inside the angle. \emph{Keeping the
        same opening}, put the point on $Q$ and draw a second arc crossing it. Call the
        crossing $R$.
  \item Draw ray $ER$ with the straightedge. It bisects the angle.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{Which arcs must share an opening:} the two in step 2, from $P$ and from $Q$. That
shared radius is what makes $PR = QR$; together with $EP = EQ$ (both drawn with the step-1
radius) and the shared side $ER$, it gives $\triangle EPR \cong \triangle EQR$ by SSS, hence
$\angle PER \cong \angle QER$. Different openings in step 2 destroy $PR = QR$, the
triangles are no longer congruent, and $ER$ lands off the bisector.\par
Open plan --- $\ans{\text{open response}}$.}

\problem{\textbf{Coordinate check: the perpendicular bisector.}\par
\textbf{Property being tested:} every point on the perpendicular bisector of $AB$ is
equidistant from $A$ and $B$. So the test is $PA = PB$.
\[ PA = \sqrt{(-1 - (-2))^2 + (8 - 1)^2} = \sqrt{1 + 49} = \sqrt{50} \]
\[ PB = \sqrt{(-1 - 6)^2 + (8 - 7)^2} = \sqrt{49 + 1} = \sqrt{50} \]
Both equal $5\sqrt{2}$, which to the nearest hundredth is $\ans{7.07}$. The two distances
agree exactly, so $P$ passes the test and the construction is confirmed at this point.\par
Note the two radicands are the same numbers in the opposite order --- the horizontal and
vertical gaps swap between the two distances. That symmetry is what equidistance looks like
in coordinates.\par
\textit{Watch for:} $1 + 7 = 8$, adding the coordinate differences instead of squaring them,
adding, and taking the square root.}

\problem{\textbf{Justify: why the perpendicular-bisector construction works.}\par
(a) \textbf{Congruent triangles.} All four arcs were drawn with one compass opening $r$, so
by construction
\[ AM = BM = AN = BN = r \]
In $\triangle AMN$ and $\triangle BMN$: $AM = BM$, $AN = BN$, and $MN$ is common. So
$\triangle AMN \cong \triangle BMN$ by \textbf{SSS}.\par
(b) \textbf{From the congruence to the conclusion.} CPCTC gives
$\angle AMN \cong \angle BMN$. Now compare $\triangle AMX$ and $\triangle BMX$, where $X$ is
where $MN$ crosses $AB$: $AM = BM$, the included angles at $M$ are the congruent ones just
obtained, and $MX$ is common, so those triangles are congruent by \textbf{SAS}. CPCTC then
gives $AX = BX$ --- so $X$ is the midpoint --- and $\angle AXM \cong \angle BXM$. Those two
angles are a linear pair, so each is a right angle and $MN \perp AB$.\par
\textbf{Grading:} both halves need a named criterion. An answer that stops at ``the arcs are
equal so it must be the bisector'' has asserted the conclusion, not proved it.\par
Open justification --- $\ans{\text{open response}}$.}

\problem{\textbf{Coordinate check: the inscribed hexagon.}\par
\textbf{The claim the construction depends on:} in a regular hexagon inscribed in a circle,
each side is exactly as long as the radius --- which is why walking one unchanged compass
opening around the circle closes up in six steps.
\[ \text{side} = \sqrt{(5 - 10)^2 + (8.66 - 0)^2} = \sqrt{25 + 75.0} \approx \sqrt{100} \]
The side comes out to $\ans{10}$, matching the radius to the accuracy of the rounded
coordinate. The construction's promise holds.\par
Why the hexagon and not some other polygon: the centre and two consecutive vertices form a
triangle with two sides equal to the radius, and the side equal to the radius makes it
equilateral, so its central angle is $60^\circ$ --- and six of those fill the full turn
exactly.}

\problem{\textbf{Plan: a parallel through $P$ by copying an angle.}\par
\textbf{Full-credit plan:}
\begin{itemize}[label={$\bullet$}, leftmargin=1.1cm, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Draw any line through $P$ that also crosses $\ell$; call the crossing $T$. This is
        the transversal.
  \item Put the compass point on $T$ and draw an arc crossing both $\ell$ and the
        transversal. Keeping the same opening, draw the matching arc centred at $P$
        crossing the transversal.
  \item Set the compass to the distance between the two crossing points of the arc at $T$,
        then swing that distance from where the arc at $P$ meets the transversal, marking a
        point $S$.
  \item Draw line $PS$. It is parallel to $\ell$.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{The step that creates corresponding angles:} steps 2 and 3 together, which copy the
angle at $T$ to $P$ on the same side of the transversal. Because the copied angle and the
original are corresponding angles and are congruent, the converse of the corresponding-angles
postulate gives $PS \parallel \ell$.\par
\textbf{Why not a protractor:} a construction is a proof carried out with tools whose
guarantees are exact --- an unmarked straightedge and equal compass radii. A protractor reads
an angle to about a degree, so the resulting line would be approximately parallel, and no
theorem would apply to it.\par
Open plan --- $\ans{\text{open response}}$.}

\problem{\textbf{Coordinate check: copying a segment.}\par
\textbf{Property being tested:} a copied segment is congruent to the original, so
$CD = AB$.
\[ AB = \sqrt{(7 - 1)^2 + (10 - 2)^2} = \sqrt{36 + 64} = \sqrt{100} \]
\[ CD = \sqrt{(11 - 3)^2 + (2 - (-4))^2} = \sqrt{64 + 36} = \sqrt{100} \]
Both are $\ans{10}$, so the copy is exact.\par
The two legs are $6$ and $8$ for $AB$ and $8$ and $6$ for $CD$: the copy sits at a different
angle, which is precisely the point --- a compass transfers \emph{length}, not direction.}

\problem{\textbf{Justify: the equilateral triangle.}\par
Let $r$ be the compass opening, set to $AB$, so $r = AB$.\par
The arc centred at $A$ is part of the circle of radius $r$ about $A$, and $C$ lies on it, so
$AC = r$ \emph{by the definition of a circle}: every point of a circle is the same distance
from the centre. The arc centred at $B$ was drawn with the same opening, and $C$ lies on it
too, so $BC = r$ for the same reason.\par
Therefore
\[ AC = r = AB \qquad\text{and}\qquad BC = r = AB \]
so $AB = AC = BC$ and $\triangle ABC$ is equilateral by definition.\par
\textbf{Grading:} the phrase that earns the credit is ``all radii of a circle are equal''
(or the definition of a circle). An answer that says the three sides ``look the same length''
or that measures them has not used a construction argument at all.\par
Open justification --- $\ans{\text{open response}}$.}

\problem{\textbf{Coordinate check: the circumcentre.}\par
\textbf{What two perpendicular bisectors give:} a point equidistant from all three vertices
--- the circumcentre, the centre of the circle through $A$, $B$ and $C$. So the test is
$OA = OB = OC$.
\[ OA = \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{16 + 9} = \sqrt{25} \]
\[ OB = \sqrt{(4 - 8)^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{16 + 9} = \sqrt{25} \]
\[ OC = \sqrt{4^2 + (3 - 6)^2} = \sqrt{16 + 9} = \sqrt{25} \]
All three come out to $\ans{5}$, so $O$ is the circumcentre and the circumradius is $5$.\par
A useful check on the check: the triangle has its right angle at $A(0,0)$, and for a right
triangle the circumcentre is the midpoint of the hypotenuse. The midpoint of $BC$ is
$\left(\frac{8+0}{2}, \frac{0+6}{2}\right) = (4, 3)$, exactly the constructed point.\par
\textit{Watch for:} measuring from the midpoint of $AB$, the point $(4, 0)$, which gives
$\sqrt{4^2 + 6^2} \approx 7.21$ to $C$ and unequal distances --- the symptom of using one
bisector instead of two.}

\problem{\textbf{Challenge: the quarter point, and why trisection is different.}\par
(a) \textbf{Plan.}
\begin{itemize}[label={$\bullet$}, leftmargin=1.1cm, itemsep=2pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Construct the perpendicular bisector of $AB$ (problem 1's plan). It meets $AB$ at
        the midpoint $M$.
  \item Construct the perpendicular bisector of $AM$ the same way. It meets $AM$ at $Q$.
  \item $Q$ is the required point: $AQ$ is one quarter of $AB$.
\end{itemize}
(b) \textbf{Justification.} Each bisection is justified exactly as in problem 4: the four
equal arc radii give congruent triangles by SSS, CPCTC gives the equal halves, so the
crossing point really is a midpoint. Applying it twice,
\[ AM = \tfrac{1}{2}AB \qquad\text{and}\qquad AQ = \tfrac{1}{2}AM = \tfrac{1}{4}AB \]
The construction is exact, not approximate, because every step rests on equal radii rather
than on a measurement.\par
(c) \textbf{Why repeated bisection cannot trisect.} Halving repeatedly can only ever produce
points at $\tfrac{1}{2}$, $\tfrac{1}{4}$, $\tfrac{3}{8}$ and so on --- fractions whose
denominators are powers of two. One third is not such a fraction, so no number of bisections
lands on it; the points get close but never arrive.\par
Worth adding: a \emph{segment} can nevertheless be trisected with compass and straightedge,
using parallel lines and similar triangles rather than bisection. An \emph{angle} cannot be
trisected in general by these tools at all --- a much deeper result, and a good example of the
difference between ``my method fails'' and ``no method exists''.\par
Open synthesis --- $\ans{\text{open response}}$.}
