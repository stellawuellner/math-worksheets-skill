\begin{center}
\textit{Classifying postulates and theorems, applying a postulate, testing the
truth of a converse, and deciding when a biconditional is valid}
\end{center}

\noindent\small\textbf{Four words, four jobs.} A \textbf{definition} says what a
term means and is reversible by agreement. A \textbf{postulate} is accepted
without proof, so the system has somewhere to start. A \textbf{theorem} is
proved from definitions, postulates and earlier theorems --- you can ask
``prove it'' and get an answer. A \textbf{biconditional} (``$p$ if and only if
$q$'') packs a statement and its converse into one sentence, and is valid only
when \emph{both} are true. Obviousness decides nothing; provability does.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Directions.} Name the statement that authorises each equation
you write. A converse is never true automatically: disprove one with a single
counterexample, or say what proves it. Write the requested value on the answer
line.\normalsize

\problem[5cm]{$B$ lies between $A$ and $C$, with $AB = 2x+3$, $BC = 3x-1$, and
$AC = 27$.
\textbf{(a)} Name the statement that permits $AB + BC = AC$, and say whether it
is a definition, a postulate or a theorem. \textbf{(b)} Solve for $x$.}

\problem[6cm]{Classify each statement as a \textbf{definition}, a
\textbf{postulate} or a \textbf{theorem}, with the one-phrase reason that
decides it.

\problem[5cm]{$\overrightarrow{BD}$ lies in the interior of $\angle ABC$, with
$m\angle ABD = 4x$, $m\angle DBC = 3x+5$ and $m\angle ABC = 96^\circ$.
\textbf{(a)} Name the postulate that lets you add the two smaller measures.
\textbf{(b)} Solve for $x$, then check that your two measures add to $96$.}

\problem[5.5cm]{Consider the true statement: \emph{if $x = 6$, then $x^2 = 36$.}
\textbf{(a)} Write its converse. \textbf{(b)} Solve $x^2 = 36$ completely and
list \emph{every} real solution on the answer line. \textbf{(c)} Use the
solution set to decide whether the converse is true, naming the counterexample
if it is not.}

\problem[5.5cm]{Consider the biconditional: \emph{$2x - 7 = 9$ if and only if
$x = 8$.}
\textbf{(a)} Write the two conditionals it packs together. \textbf{(b)} Solve
$2x - 7 = 9$ and give the complete solution set. \textbf{(c)} Explain why the
solution set being \emph{exactly} one value is what makes the biconditional
valid.}

\problem[5.5cm]{The Vertical Angles Theorem: if two angles are vertical angles,
then they are congruent. Now let $\angle 1$ and $\angle 2$ be \emph{adjacent}
angles (they share a side and are not vertical) with $m\angle 1 = 5x - 8$,
$m\angle 2 = 3x + 22$, and $\angle 1 \cong \angle 2$.
\textbf{(a)} Write the converse of the theorem. \textbf{(b)} Solve for $x$ and
find both measures. \textbf{(c)} Explain how this pair settles the truth of the
converse.}

\problem[6cm]{``Through any two points there is exactly one line'' is a
\textbf{postulate}. ``If two distinct lines intersect, then they intersect in
exactly one point'' is a \textbf{theorem}. Both sound equally obvious. Explain
what makes the second a theorem while the first is a postulate, and name the
statement a proof of the second would rest on.
\noansline}

\problem[6cm]{A useful biconditional: \emph{a point $P$ lies on the
perpendicular bisector of $\overline{AB}$ if and only if $PA = PB$.} For a point
$P$ in the plane, $PA = 4x - 5$ and $PB = 2x + 7$.
\textbf{(a)} Solve for the $x$ that places $P$ on the perpendicular bisector.
\textbf{(b)} Find $PA$ and $PB$ and check they agree. \textbf{(c)} Which half of
the biconditional did you use --- the half starting from equal distances, or the
half starting from the bisector? Say why.}

\problem[6cm]{Consider the true statement: \emph{if a quadrilateral is a square,
then it has four right angles.}
\textbf{(a)} Write its converse. \textbf{(b)} Decide whether the converse is
true. \textbf{(c)} If it is false, sketch and label a quadrilateral satisfying
the hypothesis but not the conclusion, and name the property of a square your
figure fails. \textbf{(d)} Say what this means for the statement ``a
quadrilateral is a square if and only if it has four right angles''.
\noansline}

\problem[6.5cm]{\textbf{(a)} A definition is always reversible. Rewrite as a
single biconditional: \emph{an angle bisector is a ray that divides an angle
into two congruent angles.}
\textbf{(b)} Now take: \emph{if two angles are complementary, then they are
adjacent.} Can it be rewritten as a valid biconditional? Justify by examining
the conditional and its converse separately, and give a counterexample for
whichever direction fails.
\noansline}
