\problem{Multiply $\dfrac{2}{3} \times \dfrac{3}{8}$, cancelling first.

\textbf{Solution:} The $3$ on top of the first fraction and the $3$ on the
bottom of the second are a top-and-bottom pair, so they cancel:
\[ \dfrac{2}{3} \times \dfrac{3}{8} = \dfrac{2}{1} \times \dfrac{1}{8}
   = \dfrac{2}{8} = \dfrac{1}{4} \]
Cancelling is optional here --- multiplying straight across gives
$\dfrac{6}{24}$, which reduces to the same thing.
$\;$ $\ans{\frac{1}{4}}$

\textit{Watch for:} a student who ``keeps, changes, flips'' on a
multiplication gets $\dfrac{2}{3} \times \dfrac{8}{3} = \dfrac{16}{9}$. Flipping
belongs to division only.}

\problem{Divide $\dfrac{3}{4} \div \dfrac{2}{5}$.

\textbf{Solution:} The divisor --- the fraction \emph{after} the division sign
--- is the one that flips, because dividing by $\dfrac{2}{5}$ is the same as
multiplying by $\dfrac{5}{2}$:
\[ \dfrac{3}{4} \div \dfrac{2}{5} = \dfrac{3}{4} \times \dfrac{5}{2}
   = \dfrac{15}{8} \]
$\;$ $\ans{\frac{15}{8}}$ \quad (that is $1\tfrac{7}{8}$).

\textit{Watch for:} flipping the first fraction instead gives
$\dfrac{4}{3} \times \dfrac{2}{5} = \dfrac{8}{15}$ --- less than $1$, when the
answer must be more than $1$ because $\dfrac{3}{4}$ is bigger than
$\dfrac{2}{5}$.}

\problem{Find and fix Kai's work:
$\dfrac{2}{3} \div \dfrac{4}{5} = \dfrac{3}{2} \times \dfrac{4}{5}
= \dfrac{6}{5}$.

\textbf{(a) The mistake:} Kai flipped the \emph{dividend} $\dfrac{2}{3}$ (the
fraction being divided) instead of the \emph{divisor} $\dfrac{4}{5}$. Keep,
change, flip means keep the FIRST fraction exactly as it is.

\textbf{(b) Correct work:}
\[ \dfrac{2}{3} \div \dfrac{4}{5} = \dfrac{2}{3} \times \dfrac{5}{4}
   = \dfrac{10}{12} = \dfrac{5}{6} \]
$\;$ $\ans{\frac{5}{6}}$ \quad A quick sanity check: dividing by a number less
than $1$ makes the result bigger than $\dfrac{2}{3}$, and $\dfrac{5}{6}$ is.}

\problem{Find and fix Maya's work:
$\dfrac{4}{5} \times \dfrac{8}{9} = \dfrac{1}{5} \times \dfrac{2}{9}
= \dfrac{2}{45}$.

\textbf{(a) The mistake:} the $4$ and the $8$ are both on TOP. Cancelling
removes the same factor from a numerator and a denominator, which keeps the
value the same; removing a factor of $4$ from two numerators divides the
product by $16$, so the answer comes out far too small.

\textbf{(b) Correct work:} nothing here can cancel ($4$ and $9$ share no
factor; $8$ and $5$ share no factor), so multiply straight across:
\[ \dfrac{4}{5} \times \dfrac{8}{9} = \dfrac{32}{45} \]
$\;$ $\ans{\frac{32}{45}}$}

\problem{Find the missing factor: $\dfrac{3}{4} \times \makebox[1.2cm]{\hrulefill} = \dfrac{1}{2}$.

\textbf{Solution:} To undo multiplying by $\dfrac{3}{4}$, divide by
$\dfrac{3}{4}$ --- that is, multiply by its reciprocal $\dfrac{4}{3}$:
\[ \dfrac{1}{2} \div \dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{1}{2} \times \dfrac{4}{3}
   = \dfrac{4}{6} = \dfrac{2}{3} \]
Check: $\dfrac{3}{4} \times \dfrac{2}{3} = \dfrac{6}{12} = \dfrac{1}{2}$.
$\;$ $\ans{\frac{2}{3}}$}

\problem{Find and fix Jordan's work:
$\dfrac{6}{7} \div \dfrac{3}{8} = \dfrac{3}{7} \div \dfrac{3}{4}
= \dfrac{3}{7} \times \dfrac{4}{3} = \dfrac{4}{7}$.

\textbf{(a) The mistake:} Jordan cross-cancelled the $6$ and the $8$ while the
problem was still a division. Cross-cancelling is a shortcut for
multiplication only. Once the division is rewritten as
$\dfrac{6}{7} \times \dfrac{8}{3}$, the $6$ and the $8$ are both on top, so
they still cannot cancel with each other.

\textbf{(b) Correct work:}
\[ \dfrac{6}{7} \div \dfrac{3}{8} = \dfrac{6}{7} \times \dfrac{8}{3}
   = \dfrac{2}{7} \times \dfrac{8}{1} = \dfrac{16}{7} \]
(here the $6$ on top and the $3$ on the bottom legally cancel by $3$).
$\;$ $\ans{\frac{16}{7}}$ \quad (that is $2\tfrac{2}{7}$).}

\problem{Ali: $\dfrac{6}{5} \times \dfrac{5}{8} = \dfrac{3}{4}$. \quad
Sam: $\dfrac{5}{6} \times \dfrac{8}{5} = \dfrac{4}{3}$. Who is right?

\textbf{Solution:} \textbf{Sam} is right. The problem is
$\dfrac{5}{6} \div \dfrac{5}{8}$, so the divisor $\dfrac{5}{8}$ is what flips,
giving $\dfrac{5}{6} \times \dfrac{8}{5} = \dfrac{40}{30} = \dfrac{4}{3}$.
Ali took the reciprocal of the dividend $\dfrac{5}{6}$ instead, which answers
a different question. $\;$ $\ans{\frac{4}{3}}$

\textit{Check without computing:} $\dfrac{5}{6}$ is larger than $\dfrac{5}{8}$
(same numerator, smaller denominator wins), so the quotient must be greater
than $1$. Ali's $\dfrac{3}{4}$ is less than $1$, so it cannot be right.}

\problem{Granola: $\dfrac{3}{4}$ cup per batch, $\dfrac{9}{2}$ cups of oats.
How many batches? Nia wrote
$\dfrac{2}{9} \times \dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{1}{6}$ batch.

\textbf{(a) Why Nia cannot be right:} she has more oats than one batch needs,
so the answer must be greater than $1$; $\dfrac{1}{6}$ is less than $1$. She
flipped the dividend $\dfrac{9}{2}$ instead of the divisor $\dfrac{3}{4}$.

\textbf{(b) Correct work:} ``how many $\dfrac{3}{4}$-cup scoops fit in
$\dfrac{9}{2}$ cups'' is a division, and only the divisor flips:
\[ \dfrac{9}{2} \div \dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{9}{2} \times \dfrac{4}{3}
   = \dfrac{36}{6} = 6 \]
$\;$ $\ans{6\ \text{batches}}$}
