\noindent\small\textbf{What this sheet is teaching.} Students meet four curves at once and
usually try to memorise four pictures. The sheet replaces that with one decision procedure:
write the equation in general form, compute $B^2 - 4AC$, and let the sign name the family
before any completing-the-square work begins. Only then do the key features come out. Two
places to listen carefully: a student who names a circle ``ellipse'' has skipped the
$A = C$ test, and a student who reports $16$ as a radius has stopped one square root
early.\normalsize

\problem{\textbf{Read the coefficients, then name it.} \quad
$x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 4y - 12 = 0$

\par\smallskip
Matching against $Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 + Dx + Ey + F = 0$: there is no $xy$ term, so $B = 0$;
the $x^2$ and $y^2$ coefficients are both $1$.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
$A$ & $B$ & $C$ & $B^2 - 4AC$ \\ \hline
$1$ & $0$ & $1$ & $0 - 4(1)(1)$ \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\par
The indicator is $\ans{-4}$, which is negative, so the curve is an ellipse or a circle.
The extra condition decides it: $A = C$ (both are $1$), so it is a \textbf{circle}.
\par
\textbf{Why the indicator first?} Because completing the square on the wrong assumption
costs five lines. Naming the family costs one.}

\problem{\textbf{Same model, different signs.} \quad $9x^2 - 4y^2 = 36$

\par\smallskip
Move the $36$ across: $9x^2 - 4y^2 - 36 = 0$, so $A = 9$, $B = 0$, $C = -4$.
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
$A$ & $B$ & $C$ & $B^2 - 4AC$ \\ \hline
$9$ & $0$ & $-4$ & $0 - 4(9)(-4)$ \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\par
$B^2 - 4AC = \ans{144}$, positive, so the curve is a \textbf{hyperbola}.
\par
\textbf{The sign is the whole story.} Two squares with \emph{opposite} signs always give
$-4AC > 0$. A student who reads ``two squares'' and says ellipse has looked at the terms
instead of at their signs --- that is exactly the confusion problem 10 asks about.}

\problem{\textbf{A missing square.} \quad $y = x^2 - 6x + 5$

\par\smallskip
Rewriting as $x^2 - 6x - y + 5 = 0$: there is an $x^2$ term but no $y^2$ term, so
$A = 1$, $B = 0$, and $C = 0$.
\par
$B^2 - 4AC = 0 - 4(1)(0) = \ans{0}$, so the curve is a \textbf{parabola}.
\par
\textbf{The point of the rewrite.} $C = 0$ is not ``no coefficient'' --- it is the
coefficient zero, and it is what drives the indicator to zero. Every parabola with
horizontal or vertical axis has exactly one squared variable, which is why its indicator
can only be $0$.}

\problem{\textbf{Circle: find the center and the radius.} \quad
$x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 4y - 12 = 0$

\par\smallskip
\textbf{Solution.} Group and complete both squares:
\begin{align*}
  (x^2 - 6x) + (y^2 + 4y) &= 12 \\
  (x^2 - 6x + 9) + (y^2 + 4y + 4) &= 12 + 9 + 4 \\
  (x - 3)^2 + (y + 2)^2 &= 25
\end{align*}
Reading the standard form $(x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2$ off that line:
\par
$h = -\dfrac{D}{2} = -\dfrac{-6}{2} = \ans{3}$, \qquad
$k = -\dfrac{E}{2} = -\dfrac{4}{2} = \ans{-2}$, \qquad
$r = \sqrt{h^2 + k^2 + 12} = \sqrt{9 + 4 + 12} = \sqrt{25} = \ans{5}$.
\par
So the center is $(3, -2)$ and the radius is $5$.
\par
\textbf{Sign trap in the center.} The standard form has \emph{minus} $h$ and \emph{minus}
$k$ inside the brackets, so $(y+2)^2$ means $k = -2$, not $+2$. Ask the student to check by
substituting the center back: the two bracket terms must both vanish.}

\problem{\textbf{Parabola: vertex, axis, and where it crosses.} \quad $y = x^2 - 6x + 5$

\par\smallskip
\textbf{Axis of symmetry.} With $a = 1$ and $b = -6$,
$x = -\dfrac{b}{2a} = -\dfrac{-6}{2} = \ans{3}$, so the axis is the line $x = 3$.
\par
\textbf{Vertex $y$-value.} Put $x = 3$ back into the function:
$y = 3^2 - 6(3) + 5 = 9 - 18 + 5 = \ans{-4}$. The vertex is $(3, -4)$.
\par
\textbf{$x$-intercepts.} Solve $x^2 - 6x + 5 = 0$. It factors as $(x - 1)(x - 5) = 0$, so
$x = \ans{1}$ or $x = \ans{5}$.
\par
Since $a = 1 > 0$ the parabola \textbf{opens up}, and the vertex is its lowest point.
\par
\textbf{A free check.} The vertex $x$ must sit exactly halfway between the two
intercepts: the midpoint of $1$ and $5$ is $3$. If those two numbers disagree, one of them
is wrong, and the student can find out without the answer key.}

\problem{\textbf{Ellipse: how far out in each direction?} \quad
$\dfrac{x^2}{25} + \dfrac{y^2}{9} = 1$

\par\smallskip
\textbf{Solution.} The equation is already in standard form with the denominators $a^2 = 25$
under $x^2$ and $b^2 = 9$ under $y^2$:
\par
$a = \sqrt{25} = \ans{5}$, \qquad $b = \sqrt{9} = \ans{3}$, \qquad
$c = \sqrt{a^2 - b^2} = \sqrt{25 - 9} = \sqrt{16} = \ans{4}$.
\par
Because the larger denominator is under $x^2$, the major axis is horizontal. So the
vertices are $(\pm 5, 0)$, the co-vertices are $(0, \pm 3)$, and the foci are
$(\pm 4, 0)$.
\par
\textbf{The usual slip.} Students take $a$ to be the number under $x^2$ whatever its size.
$a$ is defined as the \emph{larger} of the two, and it is the one that goes with the long
direction --- here the denominators happen to be in the convenient order, which is worth
saying out loud so the student does not learn the wrong rule from a friendly example.}

\problem{\textbf{Two squares, same sign, different sizes.} \quad
$x^2 + 4y^2 + 2x - 24y + 33 = 0$

\par\smallskip
\textbf{Solution.} $A = 1$, $B = 0$, $C = 4$, so
$B^2 - 4AC = 0 - 4(1)(4) = \ans{-16}$.
\par
Negative, so it is a circle or an ellipse. Now the extra condition: $A = 1$ but $C = 4$, so
$A \neq C$ and the curve is an \textbf{ellipse}.
\par
It is not a circle precisely because the two squared terms are scaled differently --- the
curve is stretched more in one direction than the other. (Completing the square confirms
it: $(x+1)^2 + 4(y-3)^2 = 4$, an ellipse centred at $(-1, 3)$.)
\par
\textbf{What to check in the student's reasoning.} The written answer to ``why not the
other one?'' should mention that $A$ and $C$ are unequal --- not that ``it does not look
round''.}

\problem{\textbf{Careful with this one.} \quad $x^2 + y^2 + 10x - 4y + 13 = 0$

\par\smallskip
\textbf{Solution.} Complete both squares:
\begin{align*}
  (x^2 + 10x + 25) + (y^2 - 4y + 4) &= -13 + 25 + 4 \\
  (x + 5)^2 + (y - 2)^2 &= 16
\end{align*}
$h = -\dfrac{10}{2} = \ans{-5}$, \qquad $k = -\dfrac{-4}{2} = \ans{2}$, \qquad
$r^2 = 16$, \qquad $r = \sqrt{16} = \ans{4}$.
\par
Center $(-5, 2)$, radius $4$.
\par
\textbf{This is the problem the sheet is built around.} The right-hand side of the
standard form is $r^2$. A student who writes $r = 16$ has done every hard step correctly
and then reported the wrong quantity, and the mistake is invisible unless someone asks
``how far is that from the centre?''. Have them plot the centre and step $4$ units right;
$16$ units would run off the page.}

\problem{\textbf{A nonlinear model.} $y = -x^2 + 6x$, highest point on the axis $x = 3$.

\par\smallskip
\textbf{Solution.} Substitute $x = 3$ into the path equation:
\[ y = -(3)^2 + 6(3) = -9 + 18 = 9. \]
So the system has the single solution $\ans{x = 3}$, $\ans{y = 9}$, and the highest point
is $(3, 9)$.
\par
\textbf{What the coordinates mean.} The ball is at its highest when it is $3$ metres
horizontally from where it was thrown, and at that moment it is $9$ metres above the
ground.
\par
\textbf{Why the system has exactly one solution.} A vertical line meets a function's graph
at most once, and the axis of symmetry is a vertical line --- so the system cannot have two
answers. That is a structural fact about the parabola's key feature, not an accident of
these numbers. Students who solve $-x^2 + 6x = 0$ instead have found the two ground-level
points $(0,0)$ and $(6,0)$; those are the $x$-intercepts, not the maximum.}

\problem{\textbf{Explain the difference.} (Open explanation --- read the reasoning, not
just the verdict.)

\par\smallskip
\textbf{The two indicators.} For $4x^2 + 9y^2 = 36$: $A = 4$, $C = 9$, so
$B^2 - 4AC = -144$, negative with $A \neq C$ --- an \textbf{ellipse}. For
$4x^2 - 9y^2 = 36$: $A = 4$, $C = -9$, so $B^2 - 4AC = +144$ --- a \textbf{hyperbola}.
\par
\textbf{A model answer.} ``Mia is right about the first one. Both equations do have an
$x^2$ and a $y^2$ term, but that is not what decides the family --- the \emph{signs} of
those two coefficients are. When both squares are added, the curve closes up into an
ellipse. When one is subtracted, no closed curve is possible: as $x$ grows the two terms
can stay equal forever, so the graph runs off in two separate branches. The indicator
records exactly this: $-4AC$ is negative when $A$ and $C$ agree in sign and positive when
they disagree. Mia was looking at which letters are squared instead of at the signs in
front of them.''
\par
\textbf{Marking guide.} Full credit for any answer that names the \emph{sign difference}
between the two squared coefficients, or computes the two indicators and reads them.
Partial credit for correctly naming both curves without saying what in the equations made
them different. Not sufficient: ``one is an ellipse and one is a hyperbola because that is
what they look like when you graph them'' --- true, but it is the picture the sheet is
trying to replace with a test she can run on the equation.
\par
Open explanation --- $\ans{\text{open response}}$.}
