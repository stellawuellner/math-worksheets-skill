\problem{Data set 1 ($2,4,6,7,8,10,12,14,27$): the median.}

\textbf{Solution:} The nine values are already in order, so the median is the
fifth: four below it and four above. That value is $8$. \ans{8\ \text{mm}}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Data set 1: the interquartile range.}

\textbf{Solution:} Split at the median and leave it out of both halves.
Lower half $2, 4, 6, 7$ has median $Q_1 = \frac{4+6}{2} = 5$. Upper half
$10, 12, 14, 27$ has median $Q_3 = \frac{12+14}{2} = 13$. So
$\text{IQR} = 13 - 5$. \ans{8\ \text{mm}}

The IQR ignores the $27$ entirely --- that is exactly why it pairs with the
median rather than with the mean.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Data set 2: the residual of Model A at week 2.}

\textbf{Solution:} Model A predicts
$\hat{y} = 1.0 + 3.3(2) = 1.0 + 6.6 = 7.6$ cm, and the plant actually measured
$7$ cm. Residual is observed minus predicted:
$7 - 7.6$. \ans{-0.6\ \text{cm}}

A negative residual means the model over-predicted --- the line sits above the
point at that week.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Travel survey ($n = 200$). (a) Proportion who walk. (b) Which
population does it describe?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The Walk column totals $30 + 35 = 65$ out of $200$
students, and $\frac{65}{200}$ reduces by $5$ to $\frac{13}{40}$.
\ans{\tfrac{13}{40}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged, no machine check.} The survey
covered grades 9 and 10 at one school, so $\frac{13}{40}$ describes those 200
students directly, and --- if they were a random sample of those two grades ---
can be extended with caution to all grade 9 and 10 students at that school. It
must not be extended to the whole school, since grades 11 and 12 were never
asked, nor to students at other schools. Full credit names the population the
figure does describe and at least one group it does not. Do not accept ``the
sample is too small'': 200 is a decent sample, and coverage, not size, is the
problem here.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Data set 1. (a) The mean. (b) Which summary describes a typical day?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The nine values sum to
$2+4+6+7+8+10+12+14+27 = 90$, and $90 \div 9 = 10$. \ans{10\ \text{mm}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged, no machine check.} The mean is
$10$ mm and the median is $8$ mm. The gap is caused by the single $27$ mm day:
it enters the mean at full weight and drags it up, while the median only cares
about position and barely moves. For a \emph{typical} day the median is the
better summary, and the spread quoted alongside it should be the IQR rather than
the standard deviation. Full credit identifies $27$ as the cause and chooses a
summary with a reason. A defence of the mean is acceptable only if it
acknowledges the outlier and explains why the total rainfall still matters.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Model A's sum of squared residuals, from
$-0.3, -0.6, 0.1, -0.2, 0.5, -0.8$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Squaring removes the signs, so over- and under-predictions
both count against the model:
\[
  0.09 + 0.36 + 0.01 + 0.04 + 0.25 + 0.64 = 1.39 .
\]
So Model A's fit score is \ans{1.39}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Screen time and reading score: why the headline is not supported.}

\textbf{Solution --- instructor-judged, no machine check.} The study is
observational: nobody assigned students their screen time, so the two variables
were free to move together for reasons that have nothing to do with one causing
the other. A strong negative correlation across 500 students is real evidence
that the two go together, and no evidence at all about direction. The response
must say that observational data cannot establish causation \emph{and} name one
plausible confounder --- hours of sleep, family income, or simply how much free
time a student has, each of which could push screen time up and reading scores
down independently. Reverse causation is also open: weaker readers may choose
screens over books. Do not accept a bare ``correlation is not causation'' with no
variable named and no reason.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Chart A. (a) Households surveyed. (b) The modal number of cars.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Add the four bars: $18 + 62 + 45 + 15$. \ans{140}

\textbf{Solution (b):} The tallest bar is the one at $62$ households, which is
the category for one car. \ans{1}

The mode is a third summary model for ``typical'', alongside the mean and the
median in problems 1 and 5 --- and for a count like this it is the only one of
the three that is guaranteed to be a value the data actually takes.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Model B's residuals $-1.5, -1.5, -0.5, -0.5, 0.5, -0.5$. (a) Its fit
score. (b) Which model wins, and what that does not license.}

\textbf{Solution (a):}
\[
  2.25 + 2.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 + 0.25 = 5.5 .
\]
So Model B's fit score is \ans{5.5}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged, no machine check.} Model A wins:
$1.39$ against $5.5$, so it sits closer to the six measured weeks. The
qualification that must appear is that fitting weeks 1--6 better says nothing
reliable about week 20. That is extrapolation far outside the range of the data,
and a sunflower's height cannot keep rising by $3.3$ cm a week forever --- the
straight line is a description of a window, not a law. Full credit picks Model A
with the two numbers as evidence and states the extrapolation caveat; award
partial credit for the choice alone.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Travel survey. (a) Bus among grade 9. (b) Bus among grade 10.
(c) What the comparison supports.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Restrict to the grade 9 row: $\frac{40}{100}$, which
reduces to \ans{\tfrac{2}{5}}

\textbf{Solution (b):} The grade 10 row gives $\frac{25}{100}$, which reduces to
\ans{\tfrac{1}{4}}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged, no machine check.} Within this
survey a larger share of grade 9 students took the bus, $\frac{2}{5}$ against
$\frac{1}{4}$. The response must stop there. Each group has 100 students and
nothing in the data says how much a gap of this size varies from one survey to
the next, so the comparison describes these respondents rather than establishing
that grade 9 students in general prefer the bus. Full credit reports the
comparison in the right direction \emph{and} declines to generalise, with a
reason. Do not accept a flat ``grade 9 students prefer the bus''.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Week-8 predictions. (a) Model A. (b) Model B.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $\hat{y} = 1.0 + 3.3(8) = 1.0 + 26.4$. \ans{27.4\ \text{cm}}

\textbf{Solution (b):} $\hat{y} = 2.5 + 3.0(8) = 2.5 + 24$. \ans{26.5\ \text{cm}}

Inside the data the two lines are hard to tell apart; two weeks past the last
measurement they have already separated.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Synthesis. (a) The gap between the two week-8 predictions.
(b) A defensible two-sentence conclusion.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $27.4 - 26.5$, or directly
$\left(1.0 + 3.3(8)\right) - \left(2.5 + 3.0(8)\right)$. \ans{0.9\ \text{cm}}

The gap grows by $0.3$ cm for every further week, because the two slopes differ
by $0.3$.

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged, no machine check.} A defensible
answer says something the data supports --- over weeks 1 to 6 the sunflower grew
at roughly 3 cm per week, and a straight line describes those six measurements
closely, Model A most closely --- followed by a genuine qualification. Any one of
these counts: the models were fitted to six measurements of a single plant, so
they say nothing about sunflowers in general; week 8 is already outside the
observed range and the two models disagree there by $0.9$ cm, a gap that widens
by $0.3$ cm every further week; or growth cannot stay linear indefinitely. Full
credit is a supported claim plus one real qualification, in two sentences. An
unqualified prediction for week 20 earns nothing --- that is the exact move the
whole sheet is built to prevent.
