\aktitleblock{When Does a Scale Factor Get Squared or Cubed?}{High School Geometry}{}

\input{qa_scalefactor_curr344}

\problem{Rectangle of area $24$ cm$^2$, every length multiplied by $3$. Dev writes $24 \times 3 = 72$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Enlarging every length by $3$ stretches the rectangle in \emph{two} directions at once: the length becomes $3$ times longer and the width becomes $3$ times longer, so the area picks up a factor of $3$ twice.
\[ A' = 24 \times 3^2 = 24 \times 9 \]
So \ans{A' = 216\text{ cm}^2}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} A model response: Dev used the linear scale factor where the \emph{squared} scale factor belongs. Because both dimensions are stretched by $3$, the area is multiplied by $3 \times 3 = 9$, not by $3$. His answer of $72$ cm$^2$ is the area of a rectangle only three times as big, which cannot be right for a shape whose sides have all tripled.

\emph{Full credit:} names $k^2$ as the area factor and ties it to two dimensions being stretched. \emph{Half credit:} states the rule without the reason. \emph{No credit:} redoes the arithmetic with no account of the error.

\problem{Cube of edge $5$ cm, every length multiplied by $4$. Find the enlarged volume.}

\textbf{Solution:} The original volume is $5^3 = 125$ cm$^3$. A volume is stretched in three directions, so it is multiplied by $4^3 = 64$.
\[ V' = 125 \times 64 \]
So \ans{V' = 8000\text{ cm}^3}

Check the other way: the new edge is $5 \times 4 = 20$ cm, and $20^3 = 8000$ $\checkmark$

\problem{Similar cones, heights $6$ cm and $15$ cm; smaller volume $90$ cm$^3$. Mia writes $90 \times \frac{15}{6} = 225$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The linear scale factor is $\frac{15}{6} = 2.5$. Volumes of similar solids scale by the \emph{cube} of that factor.
\[ V' = 90 \times \left(\frac{15}{6}\right)^3 = 90 \times 15.625 \]
So \ans{V' = 1406.25\text{ cm}^3}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} A model response: Mia multiplied by the linear scale factor $2.5$ instead of by $2.5^3 = 15.625$. Similar solids have every length scaled by $2.5$, so a volume --- which is a product of three lengths --- is scaled by $2.5$ three times over. Her answer is too small by a factor of $2.5^2 = 6.25$.

\emph{Full credit:} identifies the linear factor as $2.5$ and states the cube rule. \emph{Half credit:} names the cube rule without computing the linear factor. \emph{No credit:} says the answer is wrong with no rule.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Similar prisms with surface areas $48$ cm$^2$ and $300$ cm$^2$. Find the linear scale factor.}

\textbf{Solution:} Surface areas scale by $k^2$, so the ratio of areas is $k^2$, and $k$ is its square root --- not the ratio itself.
\[ k^2 = \frac{300}{48} = 6.25 \qquad k = \sqrt{6.25} \]
So \ans{k = 2.5}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Similar solids of volume $54$ cm$^3$ and $250$ cm$^3$; smaller surface area $63$ cm$^2$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Volumes scale by $k^3$, so take the cube root of the volume ratio:
\[ k = \sqrt[3]{\frac{250}{54}} = \sqrt[3]{\frac{125}{27}} = \frac{5}{3} = 1.6666\ldots \]
So \ans{k \approx 1.67}

\textbf{Solution (b):} Surface areas scale by $k^2$, and here $k = \frac{5}{3}$ exactly, so
\[ S' = 63 \times \left(\frac{5}{3}\right)^2 = 63 \times \frac{25}{9} \]
giving \ans{S' = 175\text{ cm}^2}

Note that the exact fraction $\frac{5}{3}$ was used here rather than the rounded $1.67$ --- rounding early would have given $175.6$.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Model tank at $1 : 20$. (a) Model holds $1.5$ L. (b) Model shell uses $0.8$ m$^2$. (c) Why different multipliers?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} A capacity is a volume, so it scales by $20^3 = 8000$.
\[ 1.5 \times 8000 \]
So \ans{12000\text{ L}}

\textbf{Solution (b):} A sheet-metal area scales by $20^2 = 400$.
\[ 0.8 \times 400 \]
So \ans{320\text{ m}^2}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} A model response: the single scale factor $20$ acts on every \emph{length}. A surface is two-dimensional, so it is stretched twice over and grows by $20^2 = 400$. A capacity is three-dimensional, so it is stretched three times over and grows by $20^3 = 8000$. One linear ratio therefore produces two different multipliers, and they differ by exactly one more factor of $20$.

\emph{Full credit:} explains the difference through the number of dimensions and gives both multipliers. \emph{Half credit:} gives both multipliers with no reason. \emph{No credit:} says only that volume grows more than area.
