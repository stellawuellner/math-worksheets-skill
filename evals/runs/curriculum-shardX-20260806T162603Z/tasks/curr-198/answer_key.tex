\problem{Bag of 12 counters, 3 red. Probability of drawing red.}

\textbf{Solution:} Every counter is equally likely, so the probability is
favourable over total: $\dfrac{3}{12}$, which reduces to
$\dfrac{1}{4}$. \ans{\tfrac{1}{4}}

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{Spinner with 8 equal sections, 3 shaded.}

\textbf{Solution:} The sections are equal, so they are equally likely outcomes:
$\dfrac{3}{8}$. It is already in lowest terms. \ans{\tfrac{3}{8}}

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{Random sample of 50 students, 18 walk; school of 600.}

\textbf{Solution:} The sample says a fraction $\dfrac{18}{50}$ of students walk.
Applying that fraction to the whole school:
$\dfrac{18}{50} \times 600 = 0.36 \times 600 = 216$ students. Because the sample
was random, it is fair to scale it up like this. \ans{216}

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{Box of 12 counters, 5 yellow. Probability of not yellow.}

\textbf{Solution:} If 5 of the 12 are yellow then $12 - 5 = 7$ are not, so the
probability is $\dfrac{7}{12}$. (Equivalently
$1 - \dfrac{5}{12} = \dfrac{7}{12}$.) \ans{\tfrac{7}{12}}

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{School of 800; the 25 basketball-team members surveyed, 22 choose
basketball.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) \textbf{Instructor-judged.} No. The sample is not random
and not representative: only members of the basketball team were asked, and they
are far more likely to name basketball than a randomly chosen student would be.
Full credit needs that reason --- the sample was drawn from a group already tied
to the answer --- and not simply the word ``biased''.

(b) Scaling the sample anyway gives
$\dfrac{22}{25} \times 800 = 0.88 \times 800 = 704$ students. That is the point
of the problem: the arithmetic is correct and the estimate is still worthless,
because the sample was not random. \ans{704}

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{Spinner numbered 1 to 10.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) The even numbers are 2, 4, 6, 8, 10 --- five of the ten
outcomes: $\dfrac{5}{10} = \dfrac{1}{2}$. \ans{\tfrac{1}{2}}

(b) Greater than 7 means 8, 9 or 10 --- three outcomes, and 7 itself does not
count: $\dfrac{3}{10}$. \ans{\tfrac{3}{10}}

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{Random sample of 80 bulbs, 6 faulty; batch of 2000.}

\textbf{Solution:} The faulty fraction in the sample is
$\dfrac{6}{80} = 0.075$, so the batch is estimated to hold
$0.075 \times 2000 = 150$ faulty bulbs. \ans{150}

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{Club of 200 members; two ways of choosing 20.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) \textbf{Instructor-judged.} Method 2. Numbering all 200
members and drawing 20 at random gives every member the same chance of being
picked. Method 1 reaches only the members who arrive early, and those members
may differ from the rest in ways that matter. Full credit needs the equal-chance
idea stated about the whole membership.

(b) Twenty of the 200 members are chosen, and each is equally likely, so a named
member has probability $\dfrac{20}{200} = \dfrac{1}{10}$. \ans{\tfrac{1}{10}}

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{Jar of 20 marbles: 4 red, 6 blue, 10 white, drawn with replacement.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) $\dfrac{4}{20} = \dfrac{1}{5}$. \ans{\tfrac{1}{5}}

(b) Red or blue is $4 + 6 = 10$ of the 20 marbles:
$\dfrac{10}{20} = \dfrac{1}{2}$. \ans{\tfrac{1}{2}}

(c) A probability of $\dfrac{1}{5}$ over 200 draws gives an expected
$\dfrac{4}{20} \times 200 = 40$ reds. This is a long-run expectation, not a
promise: 37 or 44 reds would be perfectly ordinary. \ans{40}

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{300 fish tagged; a later random sample of 60 contains 9 tagged.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) $\dfrac{9}{60} = \dfrac{3}{20}$ of the sample was
tagged. \ans{\tfrac{3}{20}}

(b) If the sample is representative, the same fraction of the whole lake is
tagged. With $x$ fish in the lake,
$\dfrac{300}{x} = \dfrac{9}{60}$, so $9x = 300 \times 60 = 18000$ and
$x = 2000$ fish. \ans{x = 2000}

(c) \textbf{Instructor-judged.} A correct response names one assumption --- that
the tagged fish mixed evenly back through the lake, that every fish was equally
likely to be caught the second time, or that no fish entered or left in between
--- and then gives the direction of the error: if tagged fish are easier to
catch, the sample holds more than its fair share of them, the tagged fraction
comes out too large, and the estimated lake population comes out too small. Full
credit needs an assumption \emph{and} the direction of the error.
