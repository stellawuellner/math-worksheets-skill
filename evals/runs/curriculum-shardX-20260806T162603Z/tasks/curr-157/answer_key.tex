\problem{A runner covers $\dfrac{3}{4}$ mile in $\dfrac{1}{2}$ hour. Speed in miles per hour?}

\textbf{Solution:} Miles per hour means miles divided by hours: $\dfrac{3}{4} \div \dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{3}{4} \times \dfrac{2}{1} = \dfrac{6}{4} = \dfrac{3}{2}$. \ans{\tfrac{3}{2} \text{ mph}}

The rate is larger than the distance, which is the sanity check: half an hour is less than one hour, so a full hour covers more ground.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{A cyclist rides $\dfrac{5}{8}$ km in $\dfrac{1}{4}$ hour. Speed in kilometres per hour?}

\textbf{Solution:} $\dfrac{5}{8} \div \dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{5}{8} \times \dfrac{4}{1} = \dfrac{20}{8} = \dfrac{5}{2}$. \ans{\tfrac{5}{2} \text{ km/h}}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{A toy car travels $\dfrac{7}{8}$ metre in $\dfrac{1}{4}$ second. Speed in metres per second?}

\textbf{Solution:} $\dfrac{7}{8} \div \dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{7}{8} \times \dfrac{4}{1} = \dfrac{28}{8} = \dfrac{7}{2}$. \ans{\tfrac{7}{2} \text{ m/s}}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{A cart rolls $2\dfrac{1}{2}$ feet in $\dfrac{3}{4}$ second. Speed in feet per second?}

\textbf{Solution:} Convert the mixed number first: $2\dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{5}{2}$.

Then divide: $\dfrac{5}{2} \div \dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{5}{2} \times \dfrac{4}{3} = \dfrac{20}{6} = \dfrac{10}{3}$. \ans{\tfrac{10}{3} \text{ ft/s}}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{A printer produces $3\dfrac{1}{3}$ pages in $\dfrac{2}{5}$ minute. Pages per minute?}

\textbf{Solution:} $3\dfrac{1}{3} = \dfrac{10}{3}$, and $\dfrac{10}{3} \div \dfrac{2}{5} = \dfrac{10}{3} \times \dfrac{5}{2} = \dfrac{50}{6} = \dfrac{25}{3}$. \ans{\tfrac{25}{3}}

That is $8\dfrac{1}{3}$ pages per minute.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{First runner: $\dfrac{7}{8}$ mile in $\dfrac{3}{4}$ hour. Second runner: $\dfrac{5}{6}$ mile in $\dfrac{2}{3}$ hour. (a) First speed. (b) Second speed. (c) Compare.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $\dfrac{7}{8} \div \dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{7}{8} \times \dfrac{4}{3} = \dfrac{28}{24} = \dfrac{7}{6}$. \ans{\tfrac{7}{6} \text{ mph}}

\textbf{(b)} $\dfrac{5}{6} \div \dfrac{2}{3} = \dfrac{5}{6} \times \dfrac{3}{2} = \dfrac{15}{12} = \dfrac{5}{4}$. \ans{\tfrac{5}{4} \text{ mph}}

\textbf{(c)} Over the denominator 12: $\dfrac{7}{6} = \dfrac{14}{12}$ and $\dfrac{5}{4} = \dfrac{15}{12}$. \ans{\tfrac{7}{6} < \tfrac{5}{4}}

Neither runner's distance nor time is larger on its own in a way that settles this --- only the rates do.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{A pump fills $\dfrac{5}{6}$ of a tank in $\dfrac{1}{3}$ hour. How long for one whole tank?}

\textbf{Solution:} The question asks for hours per tank, so divide the time by the fraction of the tank filled:
\[ \frac{1}{3} \div \frac{5}{6} = \frac{1}{3} \times \frac{6}{5} = \frac{6}{15} = \frac{2}{5} \]
\ans{\tfrac{2}{5} \text{ hours}}

Check the direction: filling more than the amount already done takes longer than $\frac{1}{3}$ hour, and $\frac{2}{5} > \frac{1}{3}$.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{A car uses $\dfrac{2}{5}$ gallon to travel $9\dfrac{1}{3}$ miles. Miles per gallon?}

\textbf{Solution:} $9\dfrac{1}{3} = \dfrac{28}{3}$, and miles per gallon is miles divided by gallons:
\[ \frac{28}{3} \div \frac{2}{5} = \frac{28}{3} \times \frac{5}{2} = \frac{140}{6} = \frac{70}{3} \]
\ans{\tfrac{70}{3}}

That is $23\dfrac{1}{3}$ miles per gallon.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{First shop: $\dfrac{3}{4}$ kg for 6 dollars. Second shop: $\dfrac{2}{3}$ kg for 5 dollars. (a) First price per kilogram. (b) Second price per kilogram. (c) Compare.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Dollars per kilogram is dollars divided by kilograms: $6 \div \dfrac{3}{4} = 6 \times \dfrac{4}{3} = \dfrac{24}{3}$. \ans{8}

\textbf{(b)} $5 \div \dfrac{2}{3} = 5 \times \dfrac{3}{2} = \dfrac{15}{2}$. \ans{\tfrac{15}{2}}

\textbf{(c)} $\dfrac{15}{2}$ is $7\dfrac{1}{2}$, which is less than 8. \ans{8 > \tfrac{15}{2}}

So the second shop is cheaper per kilogram, even though its total price is lower \emph{and} its bag is smaller --- the only fair comparison is per kilogram.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{A pump moves $\dfrac{7}{8}$ of a pool in $1\dfrac{3}{4}$ hours. How long for the whole pool?}

\textbf{Solution:} First the rate: $1\dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{7}{4}$ hours, so the pump moves $\dfrac{7}{8} \div \dfrac{7}{4} = \dfrac{7}{8} \times \dfrac{4}{7} = \dfrac{1}{2}$ of a pool per hour.

Then the time for one whole pool: solving $\dfrac{1}{2}t = 1$ gives $t = 2$. \ans{2 \text{ hours}}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{A recipe uses $1\dfrac{1}{2}$ cups of flour for $\dfrac{2}{3}$ of a batch. How many cups for a whole batch?}

\textbf{Solution:} $1\dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{3}{2}$, and cups per batch is cups divided by batches:
\[ \frac{3}{2} \div \frac{2}{3} = \frac{3}{2} \times \frac{3}{2} = \frac{9}{4} \]
\ans{\tfrac{9}{4}}

That is $2\dfrac{1}{4}$ cups --- more than $1\dfrac{1}{2}$, as it must be, since $\frac{2}{3}$ of a batch is less than a whole one.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{A cyclist rides $5\dfrac{1}{4}$ miles in $\dfrac{3}{4}$ hour, then $2\dfrac{1}{2}$ miles in $\dfrac{1}{2}$ hour. (a) First leg speed. (b) Second leg speed. (c) Whole-ride speed. (d) Why is (c) not the average of (a) and (b)?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $5\dfrac{1}{4} = \dfrac{21}{4}$, so $\dfrac{21}{4} \div \dfrac{3}{4} = \dfrac{21}{4} \times \dfrac{4}{3} = 7$. \ans{7 \text{ mph}}

\textbf{(b)} $2\dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{5}{2}$, so $\dfrac{5}{2} \div \dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{5}{2} \times \dfrac{2}{1} = 5$. \ans{5 \text{ mph}}

\textbf{(c)} Total distance $\dfrac{21}{4} + \dfrac{5}{2} = \dfrac{31}{4}$ miles; total time $\dfrac{3}{4} + \dfrac{1}{2} = \dfrac{5}{4}$ hours. Divide:
\[ \frac{31}{4} \div \frac{5}{4} = \frac{31}{4} \times \frac{4}{5} = \frac{31}{5} \]
\ans{\tfrac{31}{5} \text{ mph}}

\textbf{(d) --- instructor-judged.} The plain average of 7 and 5 is 6, and the true answer is $\frac{31}{5}$, which is $6.2$.

Look for a response built on total distance over total time: the two legs are not ridden for equal times, and the faster leg lasts $\frac{3}{4}$ of an hour against the slower leg's $\frac{1}{2}$, so the faster rate carries more weight. Averaging the two rates would only be right if the two times were equal.
