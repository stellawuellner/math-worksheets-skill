\problem{\textbf{Write $4(x+7)$ without parentheses.}

Step 1. The $4$ outside multiplies everything inside, so there are two products
to write, not one: $4 \cdot x$ and $4 \cdot 7$.

Step 2. $4 \cdot x = 4x$ and $4 \cdot 7 = 28$, joined by the same $+$ that was
inside. $\ans{4x + 28}$

Step 3. Check with a number: at $x = 1$, the original is $4(8) = 32$ and the
answer is $4 + 28 = 32$ $\checkmark$.}

\problem{\textbf{Kim: $3(x+5) = 3x+5$. Test at $x = 4$.}

Step 1. Evaluate the original the way the parentheses say: inside first,
$4 + 5 = 9$, then $3 \cdot 9 = 27$. $\ans{27}$

Step 2. Evaluate Kim's version at the same $x$: $3(4) + 5 = 12 + 5 = 17$. The
two disagree, so Kim's expression is not equivalent --- one disagreement is
enough to prove it.

Step 3. Name the error. Kim multiplied only the first term inside the
parentheses. Distributing means every term gets multiplied: $3(x+5) = 3x + 15$,
and $3(4) + 15 = 27$ $\checkmark$ agrees with Step 1.

Step 4. The gap $27 - 17 = 10$ is exactly the $15 - 5$ that Kim never
multiplied --- worth pointing out, because it shows the error has a size, not
just a name.}

\problem{\textbf{Devon: $5x+3y = 8xy$. Test at $x = 2$, $y = 3$.}

Step 1. Evaluate the original: $5(2) = 10$ and $3(3) = 9$, so
$10 + 9 = 19$. $\ans{19}$

Step 2. Evaluate Devon's version: $8(2)(3) = 48$. Not equal to $19$, so the two
expressions are different.

Step 3. Name the error. Devon added the coefficients $5+3$ AND multiplied the
letters. Neither move is legal here: $5x$ and $3y$ are unlike terms because
their letter parts differ, so the expression $5x+3y$ is already as simple as it
gets.

Step 4. A model that helps: if $x$ is the price of a pen and $y$ the price of a
notebook, $5x+3y$ is the cost of five pens and three notebooks. There is no
single item priced $xy$, so no single term can hold both.}

\problem{\textbf{Simplify $7a + 4 - 2a + 9$.}

Step 1. Sort the terms into like groups, carrying each sign with the term it
belongs to: the $a$ terms are $7a$ and $-2a$; the constants are $+4$ and $+9$.

Step 2. Combine inside each group only: $7a - 2a = 5a$, and $4 + 9 = 13$.

Step 3. $\ans{5a + 13}$ --- and this cannot be shortened further, because $5a$
and $13$ are unlike.

Step 4. Check at $a = 2$: the original is $14 + 4 - 4 + 9 = 23$, and the answer
is $10 + 13 = 23$ $\checkmark$.}

\problem{\textbf{Nadia: $8-2(x-3)$ written as $8-2x-6$; test at $x=1$.}

Step 1. Read what is being distributed. It is not $2$ but $-2$, and it
multiplies BOTH terms in the parentheses: $-2 \cdot x = -2x$ and
$-2 \cdot (-3) = +6$.

Step 2. So $8 - 2(x-3) = 8 - 2x + 6 = 14 - 2x$. Nadia kept the second term
negative, which is where a sign error of this kind always lives.

Step 3. Evaluate the correct expression at $x = 1$: $14 - 2 = 12$.
$\ans{12}$

Step 4. Nadia's version at $x=1$ gives $2 - 2 = 0$, which is $12$ away from the
true value --- the whole $12$ she lost by not distributing the sign.}

\problem{\textbf{Simplify $5(2x-3)+4x$.}

Step 1. Distribute the $5$ across both terms:
$5 \cdot 2x = 10x$ and $5 \cdot (-3) = -15$, giving $10x - 15 + 4x$.

Step 2. Now combine like terms. The $x$ terms are $10x$ and $4x$; the constant
$-15$ has nothing to pair with, so it stays as it is.

Step 3. $10x + 4x = 14x$, so the expression is $\ans{14x - 15}$

Step 4. Check at $x = 2$: the original is $5(1) + 8 = 13$, and the answer is
$28 - 15 = 13$ $\checkmark$. Note what must NOT happen in Step 3: $14x$ and
$-15$ are unlike, so they never merge into a single number.}

\problem{\textbf{Factor $6x+15$, then check by distributing back.}

Step 1. Find the greatest common factor of the two terms. $6x = 2 \cdot 3 \cdot
x$ and $15 = 3 \cdot 5$, so the largest factor they share is $3$.

Step 2. Divide each term by $3$ and write what is left inside parentheses:
$6x \div 3 = 2x$ and $15 \div 3 = 5$. $\ans{3(2x + 5)}$

Step 3. Check by distributing back, which is the whole point of the exercise:
$3 \cdot 2x = 6x$ and $3 \cdot 5 = 15$, giving $6x + 15$ $\checkmark$.

Step 4. This is the same rule read right to left. A student who writes
$3(2x+5) = 6x+5$ has undone only half of the factoring --- and the check in Step
3 catches it immediately, which is why the check is worth doing every time.}

\problem{\textbf{(a) Why unlike terms cannot combine. (b) Simplify
$3(2x-4)-2(x-5)$ and test at $x=3$.}

\textbf{(a)} \emph{Open response --- flagged for manual review. Model answer
below; accept any equivalent reasoning.}\\
A letter stands for a number nobody has fixed yet, and $x$ and $y$ may stand for
different numbers. Combining $5x$ and $3y$ into one term would force them to
count the same thing. The substitution test proves it: at $x=2$, $y=3$ the
expression is worth $19$ while $8xy$ is worth $48$, and a single disagreement is
enough. Terms may be added only when their letter parts are identical, because
then they really are copies of the same unknown amount.\\
\emph{What a full-credit answer must contain:} the idea that a letter stands for
an unknown number, that unlike terms count different things, and (ideally) one
substitution that shows the two expressions disagree.

\textbf{(b)} Step 1. Distribute each bracket, signs included:
$3(2x-4) = 6x-12$, and $-2(x-5) = -2x+10$ --- the second one is where the sign
error lives, since $-2 \cdot (-5) = +10$.

Step 2. Combine like terms: $6x - 2x = 4x$ and $-12 + 10 = -2$, so the
simplified expression is $4x-2$.

Step 3. Substitute $x = 3$ into the simplified expression: $4(3) - 2 = 10$, and
the original gives $3(2) - 2(-2) = 6 + 4 = 10$ $\checkmark$. $\ans{10}$

Step 4. The classmate who keeps both signs inside the second bracket writes
$6x-12-2x-10 = 4x-22$ and gets $-10$ at $x=3$ --- same first bracket, same
combining, one sign.}
