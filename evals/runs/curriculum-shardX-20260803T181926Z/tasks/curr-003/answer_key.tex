\problem{\textbf{Two rows of buttons --- write the numeral.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The buttons sit in two equal rows, so count the top row first
and then keep counting into the second row instead of starting over.

\textbf{Step 2.} Top row: one, two, three --- that is $3$. Count on through the
second row: ``four, five, six''. That is $3 + 3$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The last number said is how many, so the numeral to write is
\ans{6}

\textit{Listen for:} a child who restarts at one on the second row. Ask ``how
many altogether?'' and point along both rows without lifting the finger.}

\problem{\textbf{Word card \emph{seven} with tally marks --- write the numeral.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The card, the tallies and the numeral all name one amount, so
counting the tallies tells us what numeral the word \emph{seven} is written as.

\textbf{Step 2.} The first bundle is five tally marks: count them, ``one, two,
three, four, five''. Then count on for the two singles: ``six, seven''. That is
$5 + 2$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The numeral that matches the word \emph{seven} is \ans{7}

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $3$ --- the bundle of five was counted as a
single mark. A bundle is five marks, not one.}

\problem{\textbf{Full five-frame and four loose counters --- circle the word.}

\textbf{Step 1.} A full five-frame is $5$ without recounting --- that is what
the frame is for --- so start at five and count on.

\textbf{Step 2.} ``Six, seven, eight, nine'' for the four loose counters. That
is $5 + 4$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Nine counters, so the word to circle is \emph{nine} and the
numeral is \ans{9}

\textit{Teaching note:} the two distractor words, \emph{four} and \emph{six},
are the counts a child gets by reading only the loose counters or by stopping
one short.}

\problem{\textbf{Card A shows $8$; card B shows dots. Write $>$ or $<$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The two cards use different representations, so they cannot be
compared until both are numbers. Card A is already a numeral: $8$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Count card B: the five-frame is full, which is $5$, and one
more star makes ``six''. That is $5 + 1$, so card B is $6$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Eight is more than six, so the true sentence uses the
\emph{greater than} sign: Card A \ans{>} Card B.

\textit{Listen for:} ``Card B is more because I can see the dots.'' A numeral
counts exactly as much as a picture of the same amount.}

\problem{\textbf{Full ten-frame and three loose dots --- write the numeral.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The ten-frame is full, so it is $10$ and does not need
recounting. Count on from ten for the loose dots.

\textbf{Step 2.} ``Eleven, twelve, thirteen'' --- that is $10 + 3$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The numeral is \ans{13}

\textit{Teaching note:} thirteen is ``ten and three more''. Saying it that way
while pointing at the frame and the loose dots is the first step toward place
value.}

\problem{\textbf{Word card \emph{twelve} with a ten-frame and two more.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The word and the picture name the same amount, so count the
picture to find out how the word \emph{twelve} is written as a numeral.

\textbf{Step 2.} The frame is full, so start at $10$ and count on for the two
extra circles: ``eleven, twelve''. That is $10 + 2$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The numeral is \ans{12}

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $21$ --- the digits were written in the order the
word sounds. Teen words say the ones part first but are written tens first;
the ten-frame picture is the check.}

\problem{\textbf{Sam's word card \emph{eleven} against Nina's dots.}

\textbf{Step 1.} One card is a word and the other is a picture, so turn both
into numbers before comparing.

\textbf{Step 2.} Sam: \emph{eleven} means one ten and one more, which is
$10 + 1$. Nina: her ten-frame is full and four stars sit beside it, which is
$10 + 4$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Both start from a full ten-frame, so only the extras decide.
One is less than four, so Sam \ans{<} Nina.

\textit{Teaching note:} comparing teen numbers by their extras is exactly how
place value will be used later --- same tens, so look at the ones.}

\problem{\textbf{Ben wrote $6$ for a word card reading \emph{sixteen}.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Ben's numeral and the word card disagree, and the picture
settles it. The first frame is full, so the picture already holds $10$ before
any of the loose dots are counted.

\textbf{Step 2.} Count on from ten through the second frame: ``eleven, twelve,
thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, sixteen''. That is $10 + 6$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The numeral that matches the picture and the word is
\ans{16}

\textit{What Ben did wrong:} he counted only the dots in the second frame and
ignored the full frame beside it, so he wrote $6$ instead of $16$. Sixteen is
``ten and six more'', and the word itself says \emph{six-teen}.}
