\problem{Six summer weeks: find the mean number of beach rescues.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Add the six rescue counts and divide by six:
\[ \frac{1 + 2 + 4 + 5 + 3 + 3}{6} = \frac{18}{6} \]
\ans{3}

\vspace{0.2cm}

\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Five towns: give the greatest number of rescues, and diagnose Ola's conclusion.}

\textbf{(a) Solution:} \quad Read along the rescues row --- not the ice-cream row above it. The
largest entry is Cardel's. \ans{7}

\textbf{(b) Diagnosis (instructor-judged).} A correct response says Ola has found an association
and called it a cause. The two rows do rise together, but a table of totals cannot show that one
produced the other, and there is no plausible mechanism by which eating ice cream would put a
swimmer in danger. Accept any wording that separates a pattern from a cause. Correcting the number
without addressing the claim is not a diagnosis.

\vspace{0.2cm}

\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Five towns: the Cardel-Ashby difference, and a third factor.}

\textbf{(a) Solution:} \quad Cardel had $7$ rescues and Ashby had $2$, so the difference is
$7 - 2$. \ans{5}

\textbf{(b) Third factor (instructor-judged).} The natural answer is hot weather. A hot spell
sends more people out to buy ice cream \textit{and} more people into the sea, so both rows climb
together without either causing the other. Town size or a busy tourist season are acceptable
alternatives, provided the response explains the effect on \textit{both} rows. Naming a factor
that would move only one row does not earn full credit.

\vspace{0.2cm}

\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Jack wrote $\frac{45}{200} \times 100 = 22.5\%$. Correct it and say what went wrong.}

\textbf{(a) Solution:} \quad The question asks about the instrument players, so the denominator is
the size of that group, $60$, not the whole sample:
$\dfrac{45}{60} \times 100 = 0.75 \times 100$. \ans{75}

\textbf{(b) Diagnosis (instructor-judged).} A correct response says Jack used the wrong
denominator: he divided by all $200$ students when only the $60$ instrument players were being
asked about. Accept any wording that names the right denominator. Note the size check --- the
answer must be a percentage of a group in which $45$ out of $60$ succeeded, so it cannot possibly
be under a quarter.

\vspace{0.2cm}

\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Book survey: mean of the more-than-$100$-books group, and the headline.}

\textbf{(a) Solution:} \quad $\dfrac{78 + 85 + 82 + 91 + 84}{5} = \dfrac{420}{5}$. \ans{84}

\textbf{(b) Judgement (instructor-judged).} A correct response says the headline is not
justified. The survey observed students as they already were; nobody changed how many books
anyone owned, so all it shows is that the two groups differ. Homes with many books usually differ
in other ways too --- income, reading habits, time spent with an adult reading --- and any of those
could produce the gap. Accept any wording that rejects the causal claim and points at the
observational nature of the survey.

\vspace{0.2cm}

\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Book survey: mean of the fewer-than-$100$-books group, and what evidence would be
needed.}

\textbf{(a) Solution:} \quad $\dfrac{70 + 75 + 68 + 77 + 70}{5} = \dfrac{360}{5}$. \ans{72}

\textbf{(b) Proposal (instructor-judged).} The strongest answer describes an experiment: take a
group of similar students, decide \textit{at random} which of them receive extra books, keep
everything else the same, and compare their later scores. A response that instead names a
confounding factor --- family income, reading at home --- and says it would have to be held equal
also earns full credit. Repeating that the survey is not enough, with no proposal at all, earns
partial credit.

\vspace{0.2cm}

\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Fire services: Ely's damage above Alton's, and Rita's reasoning.}

\textbf{(a) Solution:} \quad Work in the damage row: $210 - 40$. Subtracting the firefighter
counts instead would give $24$, which is a count of people rather than an amount of money.
\ans{170}

\textbf{(b) Diagnosis (instructor-judged).} A correct response either turns the direction around
--- towns with more fires need more firefighters, so the damage comes first --- or names town size
as the factor behind both: bigger towns have more buildings, so more and larger fires, so both
more firefighters and more damage. Sending more firefighters to a fire reduces damage; it cannot
create it. Accept either argument as long as it explains the pattern in the table.

\vspace{0.2cm}

\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Two studies: find both means and their difference, then say which can support a cause.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad
\[ \frac{82 + 88 + 79 + 91 + 85}{5} = \frac{425}{5}, \qquad
   \frac{74 + 80 + 71 + 83 + 77}{5} = \frac{385}{5} \]
The two means are $85$ and $77$, and the gap between them is the difference of those two numbers.
\[ \boxed{85 \qquad 77 \qquad \text{difference} = 8} \]

\textbf{Judgement (instructor-judged).} A correct response chooses Study 1 and explains the
reason: the students were put into the two groups \textit{at random}, so the groups started out
alike and the sleep schedule was the only planned difference between them. That is what lets the
eight-mark gap be attributed to the schedule. Study 2 only recorded what students had already
chosen for themselves, so the more motivated students could easily account for both the app and
the higher scores. Accept any wording that contrasts random assignment with students choosing for
themselves.
