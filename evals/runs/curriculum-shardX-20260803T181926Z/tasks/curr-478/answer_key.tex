\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle\int \frac{5}{(x-2)(x+3)}\,dx$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Part (a): the denominator is already factored into two distinct
linear factors, so write
$\frac{5}{(x-2)(x+3)} = \frac{A}{x-2} + \frac{B}{x+3}$ and clear denominators:
$5 = A(x+3) + B(x-2)$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Substituting the roots kills one term each time. $x = 2$ gives
$5 = 5A$, so $A = 1$; $x = -3$ gives $5 = -5B$, so $B = -1$.
\ans{\dfrac{1}{x-2} - \dfrac{1}{x+3}}

\textbf{Step 3.} Part (b): each piece is a logarithm, since
$\int \frac{dx}{x-r} = \ln|x-r|$.
\ans{\ln|x-2| - \ln|x+3| + C}

\textit{Check by recombining:} $\frac{1}{x-2} - \frac{1}{x+3} =
\frac{(x+3)-(x-2)}{(x-2)(x+3)} = \frac{5}{(x-2)(x+3)}$, the original integrand.
Recombining is faster than differentiating and catches a sign error immediately.}

\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle\int \frac{5x+1}{(x-1)(x+2)}\,dx$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Part (a): what matters is the \emph{degree}, not the shape ---
the numerator has degree 1 and the denominator degree 2, so no division is
needed and constants over each factor still suffice. Clearing:
$5x+1 = A(x+2) + B(x-1)$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $x = 1$ gives $6 = 3A$, so $A = 2$; $x = -2$ gives $-9 = -3B$,
so $B = 3$.
\ans{\dfrac{2}{x-1} + \dfrac{3}{x+2}}

\textbf{Step 3.} Part (b): integrate term by term, keeping each constant as the
coefficient of its logarithm.
\ans{2\ln|x-1| + 3\ln|x+2| + C}

\textit{Where marks are lost:} the coefficients belong outside the logarithms,
not inside. $2\ln|x-1|$ is $\ln(x-1)^2$, not $\ln|2x-2|$.}

\problem{\textbf{Marginal cost, $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1}
\frac{24}{(x+1)(x+3)}\,dx$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Part (a): $24 = A(x+3) + B(x+1)$. At $x = -1$, $24 = 2A$ so
$A = 12$; at $x = -3$, $24 = -2B$ so $B = -12$. The decomposition is
$\frac{12}{x+1} - \frac{12}{x+3}$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Part (b): antidifferentiate and evaluate.
$\left[12\ln|x+1| - 12\ln|x+3|\right]_{0}^{1}
= 12\ln\frac{2}{4} - 12\ln\frac{1}{3}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Combine the logarithms:
$12\left(\ln\frac{1}{2} + \ln 3\right) = 12\ln\frac{3}{2}$.
\ans{12\ln\frac{3}{2}}

\textbf{Step 4.} Part (c): the integrand is dollars per unit and $x$ is measured
in units, so the integral is in dollars --- about 4.87 dollars of added cost for
that increase in production.

\textit{Note on the arithmetic:} writing the evaluated form as a single
logarithm before computing avoids four separate rounding steps and is what
``exact answer'' asks for.}

\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle\int \frac{2}{x(x-1)(x+1)}\,dx$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Part (a): three distinct factors need three constants:
$2 = A(x-1)(x+1) + Bx(x+1) + Cx(x-1)$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $x = 0$ gives $2 = -A$, so $A = -2$; $x = 1$ gives $2 = 2B$, so
$B = 1$; $x = -1$ gives $2 = 2C$, so $C = 1$.
\ans{\dfrac{-2}{x} + \dfrac{1}{x-1} + \dfrac{1}{x+1}}

\textbf{Step 3.} The negative constant is not a slip: at $x = 0$ the two
surviving factors are $(-1)(1) = -1$, so the substitution divides 2 by $-1$.

\textbf{Step 4.} Part (b): integrate each piece.
\ans{-2\ln|x| + \ln|x-1| + \ln|x+1| + C}

\textit{Equivalent form:} the answer can be written
$\ln\left|\frac{x^2-1}{x^2}\right| + C$. Both are correct; the log-rule form is
worth showing students because it makes the $x \to \infty$ behaviour visible.}

\problem{\textbf{Filtration, $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{3}
\frac{12}{(t+1)(t+3)}\,dt$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Part (a): $12 = A(t+3) + B(t+1)$. At $t = -1$, $12 = 2A$ so
$A = 6$; at $t = -3$, $12 = -2B$ so $B = -6$. So
$r(t) = \frac{6}{t+1} - \frac{6}{t+3}$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Part (b): $\left[6\ln|t+1| - 6\ln|t+3|\right]_{0}^{3}
= 6\ln\frac{4}{6} - 6\ln\frac{1}{3} = 6\left(\ln\frac{2}{3} + \ln 3\right)$.
\ans{6\ln 2}

\textbf{Step 3.} That is about 4.16 litres in the first 3 minutes, since the
rate is in litres per minute and $t$ is in minutes.

\textbf{Step 4.} Part (c): both factors $t+1$ and $t+3$ increase with $t$, so
their product increases and the quotient $12/[(t+1)(t+3)]$ decreases. The filter
slows as it clogs --- no graph required.

\textit{Why the answer is so clean:} $\ln\frac{2}{3} + \ln 3 = \ln 2$. Simplify
inside the bracket before multiplying by 6.}

\problem{\textbf{Leak, $\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty} \frac{2}{t(t+2)}\,dt$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Part (a): $2 = A(t+2) + Bt$. At $t = 0$, $2 = 2A$ so $A = 1$;
at $t = -2$, $2 = -2B$ so $B = -1$. The antiderivative is
$\ln|t| - \ln|t+2| = \ln\left|\frac{t}{t+2}\right|$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Part (b): on $[1, b]$ this evaluates to
$\ln\frac{b}{b+2} - \ln\frac{1}{3} = \ln\frac{b}{b+2} + \ln 3$. As
$b \to \infty$, $\frac{b}{b+2} \to 1$ and $\ln 1 = 0$, so only the constant
survives.
\ans{\ln 3}

\textbf{Step 3.} Part (c): the limit exists and is finite, so the improper
integral converges, and its value is that limit --- about 1.10 kilograms of
chemical in total.
\ans{\ln 3}

\textit{The step students skip:} $\ln\frac{b}{b+2}$ must be combined into one
logarithm \emph{before} the limit. Taking $\lim(\ln b) - \lim(\ln(b+2))$ is
$\infty - \infty$, which is undefined and tempts a wrong conclusion of
divergence.}

\problem{\textbf{Logistic growth, $\displaystyle\int_{2}^{4}
\frac{10}{p(10-p)}\,dp$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Part (a): $10 = A(10-p) + Bp$. At $p = 0$, $10 = 10A$ so
$A = 1$; at $p = 10$, $10 = 10B$ so $B = 1$. The decomposition is
$\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{10-p}$, and both constants are positive.

\textbf{Step 2.} The sign question: $\int \frac{dp}{10-p} = -\ln|10-p|$, so the
minus sign appears at the \emph{integration} step, from the inner derivative
$-1$, not in the constant $B$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Part (b): $\left[\ln|p| - \ln|10-p|\right]_{2}^{4}
= \ln\frac{4}{6} - \ln\frac{2}{8} = \ln\frac{2}{3} - \ln\frac{1}{4}$.
\ans{\ln\frac{8}{3}}

\textbf{Step 4.} Part (c): the growth rate $\frac{p(10-p)}{10}$ is largest at
$p = 5$, so the stretch from 4 to 6 sits astride the fastest growth while the
stretch from 2 to 4 does not. The same rise in population therefore takes less
time. (Computing it confirms the reasoning: about 0.81 years against the
0.98 years this problem found.)

\textit{What to accept in part (c):} any argument that compares the size of
$p(10-p)$ on the two intervals, or that names $p = 5$ as the maximum growth
rate. A numerical answer is not required.}

\problem{\textbf{Challenge: total emission, $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\infty}
\frac{6}{(t+1)(t+4)}\,dt$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Part (a): $6 = A(t+4) + B(t+1)$. At $t = -1$, $6 = 3A$ so
$A = 2$; at $t = -4$, $6 = -3B$ so $B = -2$. The antiderivative is
$2\ln|t+1| - 2\ln|t+4| = 2\ln\left|\frac{t+1}{t+4}\right|$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Part (b): on $[0, b]$ this is
$2\ln\frac{b+1}{b+4} - 2\ln\frac{1}{4} = 2\ln\frac{b+1}{b+4} + 2\ln 4$. The
quotient tends to 1, so its logarithm tends to 0.
\ans{2\ln 4}

\textbf{Step 3.} Part (c): the limit is finite, so the total emission converges.
\ans{\ln 16 = 2\ln 4}

\textbf{Step 4.} That is about 2.77 kilograms released over all time, even
though the process never stops emitting.

\textit{The conceptual half of part (c):} decaying to 0 is not enough. The rate
$\frac{1}{t+1}$ also decays to 0, yet
$\int_{0}^{b}\frac{dt}{t+1} = \ln(b+1) \to \infty$ --- the total diverges. What
matters is \emph{how fast} the rate decays: this problem's integrand behaves
like $6/t^2$ for large $t$, which decays fast enough to give a finite total.
Accept $1/t$, $1/(t+1)$, or any $1/t^{\,q}$ with $q \le 1$.}
