\problem{$\sum \frac{1}{n^2+3}$ by limit comparison with $\sum \frac{1}{n^2}$. (a) The limit. (b) The conclusion.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The quotient simplifies before any limit is taken:
\[ \frac{1/(n^{2}+3)}{1/n^{2}} = \frac{n^{2}}{n^{2}+3} \longrightarrow \ans{1} \]

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The limit is finite and \emph{positive}, so the two series converge or diverge together. The comparison series is the $p$-series with $p = 2 > 1$, which converges, so $\sum \frac{1}{n^2+3}$ converges. Positivity matters because a limit of $0$ or $\infty$ gives only a one-way conclusion. Full credit says that.

\problem{$\sum n e^{-n^2}$ by the integral test. (a) Evaluate $\int_1^{\infty} xe^{-x^2}dx$. (b) Conditions and conclusion.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} With $u = x^2$, $du = 2x\,dx$:
\[ \int_1^{\infty} xe^{-x^{2}}dx = \left[-\tfrac{1}{2}e^{-x^{2}}\right]_1^{\infty} = 0 + \tfrac{1}{2}e^{-1}. \]
So the integral equals \ans{\frac{1}{2e} \approx 0.184}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} On $[1,\infty)$ the function $f(x) = xe^{-x^2}$ is positive, continuous, and decreasing (its derivative $e^{-x^2}(1-2x^2)$ is negative for $x \ge 1$). All three conditions hold, the integral is finite, so the series converges. The integral is \emph{not} the sum --- it bounds the tail, and the series sum lies between the integral and the integral plus the first term. Full credit gives both halves.

\problem{$\sum_{n\ge0} \frac{2^n}{n!}$. (a) Limit of $\frac{2}{n+1}$. (b) The exact sum.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The denominator grows without bound while the numerator is fixed, so the ratio limit is \ans{0}

Since $0 < 1$, the ratio test gives absolute convergence.

\textbf{Solution (b):} This is the Maclaurin series for $e^{x}$ evaluated at $x = 2$, so the sum is \ans{e^{2} \approx 7.389}

\problem{Alternating harmonic series. (a) Limit of $1/n$. (b) Error bound after $100$ terms. (c) Justify.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The term sizes are $\frac{1}{n}$, whose limit is \ans{0}

\textbf{Solution (b):} The remainder after $N$ terms is at most the first omitted term. Using $n = 1$ through $n = 100$, the first omitted term has size $\frac{1}{101}$. \ans{\frac{1}{101} \approx 0.0099}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} The alternating series test needs two things: the term sizes $\frac1n$ decrease, and their limit is $0$. Both hold, so the series converges. The remainder bound then says $|S - S_N| \le a_{N+1}$, which with $N = 100$ gives $\frac{1}{101}$. Full credit states the bound in that form rather than guessing a term count.

\problem{$\sum \frac{3n+1}{n^3+5}$ by limit comparison with $\sum \frac{1}{n^2}$. (a) The limit. (b) Conclusion and choice.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \[ \frac{(3n+1)/(n^{3}+5)}{1/n^{2}} = \frac{3n^{3}+n^{2}}{n^{3}+5} \longrightarrow \ans{3} \]

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The limit is finite and positive, and $\sum \frac{1}{n^2}$ converges ($p = 2$), so the given series converges. The comparison series was chosen by keeping only the leading power top and bottom: $\frac{3n}{n^3} = \frac{3}{n^2}$, and the constant multiple does not affect convergence. Full credit explains that choice.

\problem{$\sum \frac{n!}{3^n}$. (a) Limit of $\frac{n+1}{3}$. (b) Conclusion, and what the terms show.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The numerator grows without bound while the denominator is constant, so the ratio limit is \ans{\infty}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} A ratio limit greater than $1$ (here infinite) means the series diverges. Independently, the terms themselves grow without bound: factorial growth outruns any fixed exponential, so $\frac{n!}{3^n} \to \infty$ and the nth-term test also gives divergence. Full credit gives both reasons.

\problem{$\sum_{n\ge2}\frac{1}{n(\ln n)^2}$ by the integral test. (a) The improper integral. (b) Substitution, conditions, conclusion.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} With $u = \ln x$, $du = \frac{dx}{x}$, the integral becomes $\int_{\ln 2}^{\infty} u^{-2}\,du = \left[-u^{-1}\right]_{\ln 2}^{\infty}$, so
\[ \int_2^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x(\ln x)^{2}} = \ans{\frac{1}{\ln 2} \approx 1.443} \]

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The substitution is $u = \ln x$. On $[2, \infty)$ the integrand is positive, continuous and decreasing, so the integral test applies; the integral is finite, so the series converges. Full credit names the substitution and lists the three conditions.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{$\sum_{n\ge0}\frac{(-1)^n}{2n+1}$. (a) The exact sum. (b) Remainder bound after five terms.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} This is the Leibniz series, the arctangent series at $x = 1$: \ans{\frac{\pi}{4}}

\textbf{Solution (b):} The first five terms are $n = 0$ through $n = 4$, so the first omitted term is the one with $n = 5$, of size $\frac{1}{2(5)+1}$. \ans{\frac{1}{11} \approx 0.0909}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{$\sum \frac{n^2 3^n}{n!}$. (a) Limit of $\frac{3(n+1)}{n^2}$. (b) The cancellation and the conclusion.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The denominator grows faster than the numerator, so the ratio limit is \ans{0}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The ratio of consecutive terms is
\[ \frac{(n+1)^{2}3^{n+1}}{(n+1)!}\cdot\frac{n!}{n^{2}3^{n}}
   = \frac{(n+1)^{2}}{n^{2}}\cdot 3 \cdot \frac{n!}{(n+1)!}
   = \frac{3(n+1)^{2}}{n^{2}(n+1)} = \frac{3(n+1)}{n^{2}}. \]
The factorial contributes the single factor $(n+1)$ in the denominator and the power of three contributes one factor of $3$. Since the limit is $0 < 1$, the series converges absolutely. Full credit shows that cancellation.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{$\sum \frac{n+2}{n^3-n+4}$; the ratio test returns $1$. (a) Limit-comparison ratio. (b) Sum of $\sum 1/n^2$. (c) Explain.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \[ \frac{n+2}{n^{3}-n+4}\cdot n^{2} = \frac{n^{3}+2n^{2}}{n^{3}-n+4} \longrightarrow \ans{1} \]

\textbf{Solution (b):} The comparison series is the $p$-series with $p = 2$, whose sum is \ans{\frac{\pi^{2}}{6}}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} For terms that are a ratio of polynomials, consecutive terms differ by a factor that tends to $1$, so the ratio test is guaranteed to return $1$ and is guaranteed to be inconclusive --- it is the wrong tool for rational terms, not merely unlucky. Limit comparison against $\frac{1}{n^2}$ gives a finite positive limit, and since that $p$-series converges, so does the given series. Full credit explains why the ratio test had to fail.
