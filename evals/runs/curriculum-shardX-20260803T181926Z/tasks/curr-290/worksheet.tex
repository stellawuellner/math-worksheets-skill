\wstitleblock{Mixed Factoring: Choosing and Combining Methods}{Algebra 1 \quad Polynomials}{}

\begin{center}
\textit{Choosing among greatest common factor, trinomial, and special-form
methods, and combining more than one technique in a single expression}
\end{center}

\noindent\textbf{Directions.} Factor each expression \emph{completely}. Nothing
tells you in advance which method to use --- that decision is the work. Run the
checklist below in order on every problem, and expect several expressions to
need more than one technique before they are finished.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2cm, itemsep=2pt, topsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{Common factor first.} Pull out the greatest common factor of every
term, including any shared variable. Then look at what is left in the bracket
and start the checklist again.
\item \textbf{Count the terms.} Two terms and a subtraction sign point at
$A^2 - B^2 = (A - B)(A + B)$. Three terms point at a trinomial.
\item \textbf{Test for a perfect square} before doing trial pairs:
$A^2 \pm 2AB + B^2 = (A \pm B)^2$ needs square ends and a middle term equal to
twice the product of their roots.
\item \textbf{Leading coefficient.} If it is $1$, hunt for a factor pair of the
constant that adds to the middle coefficient. If it is not, use grouping on the
product of the outer coefficients.
\item \textbf{Complete means complete.} Check every bracket you write to see
whether it factors again. A sum of two squares does not.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\noindent\textbf{Show the technique you chose} beside each answer, e.g.
``GCF, then difference of squares''.
