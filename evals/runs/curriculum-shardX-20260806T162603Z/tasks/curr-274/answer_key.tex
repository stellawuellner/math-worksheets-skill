\problem{$a_1 = 5$, $d = 3$; a student wrote $a_7 = 5 + 7(3) = 26$. (a) Correct $a_7$. (b) Name the error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} From term $1$ to term $7$ there are six jumps, not seven: $5 + 6(3) = 5 + 18$. \ans{a_7 = 23}

\textbf{(b) --- instructor-judged.} A correct answer says reaching term seven from term one takes six jumps, so the step was added one time too many. Full credit for naming the jump count as one less than the term number; partial credit for saying only that the answer is three too big.

\problem{$a_4 = 20$, $a_9 = 45$; a student divided by $4$. Find the step correctly.}

\textbf{Solution:} Terms $4$ and $9$ are five jumps apart --- count them: $4\to5\to6\to7\to8\to9$. So $d = \frac{45 - 20}{5} = \frac{25}{5}$. Check: $20 + 5(5) = 45$ $\checkmark$ \ans{d = 5}

\problem{$a_n = 4n + 1$ for $n \ge 1$; a student read the first term off at $n = 0$. (a) $a_1$. (b) $a_5$. (c) Name the error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The rule starts at $n = 1$, so substitute $1$: $4(1) + 1$. \ans{a_1 = 5}

\textbf{Solution (b):} $4(5) + 1 = 20 + 1$. \ans{a_5 = 21}

\textbf{(c) --- instructor-judged.} A correct answer says the rule is stated for term numbers starting at one, so substituting zero names a term that does not exist; the first term is what the rule gives at input one. Full credit for tying the mistake to the stated starting index; no credit for an answer that only reports the right number.

\problem{How many terms in $a_6, a_7, \ldots, a_{20}$? A student wrote $20 - 6 = 14$. (a) Correct count. (b) Why subtraction alone fails.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Subtracting the indices counts the jumps between listed terms; the list holds one more item than that: $20 - 6 + 1 = 14 + 1$. \ans{\text{count} = 15}

\textbf{(b) --- instructor-judged.} A correct answer explains that subtracting the two index numbers counts the gaps between listed terms, and a list has one more term than it has gaps, so one must be added back. A short illustration ($a_6, a_7, a_8$ is three terms but two gaps) earns full credit; stating the add-one rule with no reason earns partial credit.

\problem{$a_1 = 12$, $d = -3$; the student's rule was $a_n = 12 - 3n$. (a) Correct $a_8$. (b) Which term equals $-18$?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The correct rule is $a_n = 12 - 3(n-1)$, which gives $12$ at $n = 1$ as it must. At $n = 8$ there are seven jumps: $12 - 7(3) = 12 - 21$. \ans{a_8 = -9}

\textbf{Solution (b):} Solve $12 - 3(n-1) = -18$: then $3(n-1) = 30$, so $n - 1 = 10$. Check: $12 - 3(10) = -18$ $\checkmark$ \ans{n = 11}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$a_n = 3 + 4(n-1)$; the student summed from $n = 0$ to $6$. (a) Sum of the first six terms. (b) The student's total. (c) Why they differ.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The first six terms are $3, 7, 11, 15, 19, 23$. Pairing first with last, $\frac{6}{2}(3 + 23) = 3(26)$. \ans{\text{sum} = 78}

\textbf{Solution (b):} Starting at $n = 0$ adds one more value, $3 + 4(-1) = -1$, on top of the six: $78 + (-1)$. \ans{\text{total} = 77}

\textbf{(c) --- instructor-judged.} A correct answer says starting the index at zero adds one extra term --- the value the rule would give one step before the sequence begins --- so the total shifts by that value; here the phantom term is $-1$, which is why the wrong total is one smaller. Full credit for naming the extra term and its effect on the total.

\problem{Auditorium: $14$ seats in row $1$, $2$ more per row; the student wrote $14 + 20(2)$. (a) Seats in row $20$. (b) Which row has $46$?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Row $20$ is nineteen rows past row $1$: $14 + 19(2) = 14 + 38$. \ans{\text{seats} = 52}

\textbf{Solution (b):} Solve $14 + 2(n-1) = 46$: then $2(n-1) = 32$, so $n - 1 = 16$. Check: $14 + 2(16) = 46$ $\checkmark$ \ans{\text{row} = 17}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$a_1 = 2$, $a_n = a_{n-1} + 7$; the student claimed $a_{100} = 702$ and $9$ terms from $a_1$ to $a_{10}$. (a) Correct $a_{100}$. (b) Correct count. (c) What the two share.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Reaching term $100$ takes ninety-nine steps: $2 + 99(7) = 2 + 693$. \ans{a_{100} = 695}

\textbf{Solution (b):} From $a_1$ to $a_{10}$ inclusive: $10 - 1 + 1$. \ans{\text{count} = 10}

\textbf{(c) --- instructor-judged.} A correct sentence says both mistakes come from confusing a count of items with a count of gaps between them: the term number counts terms, while the number of steps added counts gaps, and a run of items always has one more item than gap. Full credit for stating the shared cause rather than describing the two errors separately.
