\sstitleblock{Three Kinds of Evidence About a Limit}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{The limit is where the outputs are heading, not where they land.}
$\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=L$ says the outputs get and stay arbitrarily close to $L$ as
$x$ closes in on $c$ from both sides. The value $f(c)$ is a separate
question.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Two-sided test:} the limit exists exactly when
$\lim_{x\to c^-}f(x)=\lim_{x\to c^+}f(x)$.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Continuity at $c$:} $f(c)$ is defined, the limit exists, and the two
are equal. Fail the third and you have a \emph{removable} discontinuity (a
hole); fail the second and you have a \emph{jump}.
\end{formulabox}

\skillheading{1. Symbolic: factor, cancel, then substitute}

\begin{formulabox}
Substituting first is always the right opening move. If it returns a number,
that is the limit. If it returns $\tfrac00$, that is not a verdict --- it says
numerator and denominator share the factor $x-c$. Cancel it and substitute
again. Useful factorings: $x^2-a^2=(x-a)(x+a)$ and
$x^3-a^3=(x-a)(x^2+ax+a^2)$. For a compound fraction, combine the inner
fractions first.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Find $\lim_{x\to 4}\dfrac{x^2-16}{x-4}$.
\step{Substituting gives $\tfrac00$, so the factor $x-4$ must be sitting in
both parts --- factor rather than conclude anything.}
\step{$x^2-16=(x-4)(x+4)$, so for $x\neq 4$ the expression is just $x+4$, and
substituting gives $4+4$.}

The limit is $\ans{8}$.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Find $\lim_{x\to 5}\dfrac{x^2-25}{x-5}$.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{10}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{2. Numerical: close in from both sides}

\begin{formulabox}
Pick inputs on \emph{both} sides of $c$ and step them closer --- for $c=1$,
use $0.9,\,0.99$ and $1.1,\,1.01$. If the two columns settle on the same
number, that number is your estimate. A table can only suggest a limit, so
always follow it with algebra. If the two sides settle on \emph{different}
numbers, the two-sided limit does not exist, and the table has told you
something the algebra would have to work for.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Estimate $\lim_{x\to 1}\dfrac{x^2-1}{x-1}$ numerically.
\step{The expression is undefined at $1$, so no single substitution says
anything --- approach it instead.}
\step{At $x=0.99$ the value is $\ans{1.99}$, and at $x=1.01$ it is
$\ans{2.01}$.}

The two sides squeeze the same number, so the estimate is $\ans{2}$ --- and
cancelling $x-1$ confirms it exactly.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Build a short table on each side of $x=2$ for
$\dfrac{x^2-4}{x-2}$ and estimate the limit.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{4}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{3. Graphical: read each side separately}

\begin{formulabox}
Trace the curve in from the left, then from the right, and read the
\emph{height} each side is heading for. Open circle: that point is excluded but
the height still counts for the limit. Filled dot: that is $f(c)$, and it
counts for continuity only. When a graph is given by branch formulas, confirm
each side by substituting into the branch that side lives on.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} $h(x)=2x-1$ for $x<3$ and $h(x)=x+3$ for $x>3$. Find
$\lim_{x\to 3^-}h(x)$.
\step{A one-sided limit only ever uses the branch on that side, so the
right-hand rule is irrelevant here.}
\step{To the left of $3$ the rule is $2x-1$, which is continuous, so substitute
$x=3$ into it: $6-1$.}

The left-hand limit is $\ans{5}$.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} For the same $h$, find $\lim_{x\to 3^+}h(x)$. Do the two
one-sided limits agree?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{6}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} a filled dot is not the limit --- at a removable
discontinuity the curve heads for the hole, not for the stray point above it.
And $\tfrac00$ is never an answer: it is the signal to factor, rationalize, or
combine fractions and try again.
\end{watchoutbox}
