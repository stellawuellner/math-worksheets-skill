\problem{$(x+5)^2 + (y-2)^2 = 36$; a student gave the centre as $(5,-2)$. (a) Correct $x$-coordinate. (b) Why negative.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The standard form is $(x - h)^2$, so $(x + 5)^2$ has to be read as
$\bigl(x - (-5)\bigr)^2$. \ans{-5}
(The $y$-coordinate the student gave is wrong for the same reason, in the other direction:
$(y-2)^2$ gives $k = 2$, not $-2$.)

\vspace{0.15cm}
\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The subtraction is built into the standard form,
so a plus sign inside a bracket can only come from subtracting a negative number. Reading the
printed digits straight off gives the centre reflected through the origin. Full credit refers
to the subtraction in the standard form; ``you flip the sign'' with no reason attached is
partial credit.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$(x-1)^2 + (y+4)^2 = 49$; a student gave the radius as $49$.}

\textbf{Solution:} The right-hand side of the standard form is $r^2$, not $r$.
\[ r = \sqrt{49} \]
\ans{7}
A quick sanity check: a radius of $49$ would put the circle's edge nearly $50$ units from a
centre only $1$ unit from the origin --- far larger than the equation's numbers suggest.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\frac{x^2}{9} + \frac{y^2}{25} = 1$; a student gave the semi-major axis as $3$. (a) Correct value. (b) The error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Compare the denominators: $25 > 9$, so the larger one, under $y^2$,
carries the major axis.
\[ \sqrt{25} \]
\ans{5}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The student assumed the $x$ denominator is
always the larger one and took $\sqrt{9} = 3$. The major axis belongs to whichever
denominator is bigger, and here that is the $y$ term, so the major axis runs
\emph{vertically}, from $(0,-5)$ to $(0,5)$. Full credit states the compare-the-denominators
rule and says the axis is vertical. Reporting only the corrected number is not a diagnosis.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\frac{x^2}{169} + \frac{y^2}{25} = 1$; a student computed $\sqrt{169+25} \approx 13.93$.}

\textbf{Solution:} This is an ellipse, so the foci lie \emph{inside} it and the rule
subtracts.
\[ c = \sqrt{169 - 25} = \sqrt{144} \]
\ans{12}
The student used the hyperbola rule. The check that catches it every time: a focus of an
ellipse must be closer to the centre than the vertex, and $13.93$ is further out than the
vertex at $13$.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\frac{x^2}{16} - \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$; a student computed $\sqrt{16-9} \approx 2.65$.}

\textbf{Solution:} This is a hyperbola, so the rule adds.
\[ c = \sqrt{16 + 9} = \sqrt{25} \]
\ans{5}
The mirror image of problem $4$'s error. Here the check is the other way round: a hyperbola's
focus must lie \emph{beyond} its vertex, and $2.65$ is inside the vertex at $4$.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$x^2 + y^2 + 6x - 8y - 11 = 0$; a student gave the centre as $(6,-8)$. (a) Centre. (b) Radius. (c) The two mistakes.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Complete both squares. Half of $6$ is $3$ and half of $-8$ is $-4$:
\begin{align*}
  (x^2 + 6x + 9) + (y^2 - 8y + 16) &= 11 + 9 + 16 \\
  (x + 3)^2 + (y - 4)^2 &= 36
\end{align*}
Reading $(x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2$ gives \ans{x = -3} and \ans{y = 4}.

\textbf{Solution (b):} The right-hand side is $r^2 = 36$, so the radius is $\sqrt{36}$.
\ans{6}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} Two separate faults, and both must be named.
First, the linear coefficients were copied across without being \emph{halved}: completing the
square puts half of each coefficient inside its bracket. Second, the signs were not
\emph{reversed}: the standard form subtracts, so $+3$ inside the bracket means a centre
coordinate of $-3$. Full credit names the halving and the sign reversal as two distinct
moves, since the quoted answer got both wrong.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\frac{y^2}{36} - \frac{x^2}{64} = 1$; a student gave the asymptote slopes as $\pm\frac{8}{6}$.}

\textbf{Solution:} The positive term is the one in $y$, so its denominator supplies the
numerator of the slope: the asymptotes are $y = \pm\frac{a}{b}x$ with $a = \sqrt{36} = 6$ and
$b = \sqrt{64} = 8$.
\[ \frac{6}{8} \]
\ans{\dfrac{3}{4}}
The student used $\frac{8}{6}$, the reciprocal --- the slope pattern for a left-right
hyperbola, applied to an up-down one. A sketch settles it: this hyperbola's branches open
vertically but its asymptotes still rise less steeply than $1$, because $6 < 8$.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$x^2 + y^2 - 4x + 10y + 20 = 0$; one student said $r = \sqrt{20}$, another said the centre is $(2,5)$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Complete both squares. Half of $-4$ is $-2$ (adding $4$); half of $10$
is $5$ (adding $25$):
\begin{align*}
  (x^2 - 4x + 4) + (y^2 + 10y + 25) &= -20 + 4 + 25 \\
  (x - 2)^2 + (y + 5)^2 &= 9
\end{align*}
The radius is $\sqrt{9}$. \ans{3}

\textbf{Solution (b):} From $(y + 5)^2$, the $y$-coordinate of the centre is \ans{-5}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} The two errors are different and both must be
named. The first student treated the constant term as if it were already $r^2$ and took
$\sqrt{20}$; in fact the constant has to move across and the two completing constants
($4$ and $25$) have to be added, which turns $-20$ into $9$. The second student halved the
linear coefficient correctly but kept its sign: $+10$ halves to $5$, and the standard form
then subtracts, giving $-5$. Full credit distinguishes the two faults. Describing one error
twice, or calling both ``careless arithmetic'', does not.
