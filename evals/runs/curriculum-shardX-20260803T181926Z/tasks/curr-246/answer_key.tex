\noindent\small\textbf{How this sheet is built:} students can usually say ``positive'' or
``negative'' from a glance and cannot say what makes them sure. So every problem asks for a
\emph{number} first and a \emph{description} second --- a comparison, an extreme value, a
residual, a range --- and the description has to cite that number. Grade the citation, not
the adjective. A student who writes ``strong'' with no reference to how close the points sit
to the line has produced the same answer a guess produces.\normalsize

\problem{\textbf{Plot A --- direction from the two end points.}\par
\textbf{(a)} The leftmost point is at $x = 1$ with $y = 52$; the rightmost is at $x = 8$
with $y = 88$. Comparing them:
\[ \ans{52 < 88} \]
\textbf{(b)} The direction is \textbf{positive}. The comparison in (a) says the right-hand
end of the plot is higher than the left-hand end, so $y$ tends to increase as $x$
increases. Every point in between continues the climb, which is what makes the comparison
representative rather than a coincidence of the two ends.\par
\textit{Grading note:} accept ``as $x$ goes up, $y$ goes up'' as the reason. Do not accept
``it goes up to the right'' with no numbers --- (a) exists so the claim has evidence.}

\problem{\textbf{Plot B --- the other direction.}\par
\textbf{(a)} Leftmost point $(0, 46)$, rightmost point $(35, 16)$:
\[ \ans{46 > 16} \]
\textbf{(b)} The direction is \textbf{negative}: as $x$ increases, $y$ tends to
\textbf{decrease}.\par
Both plots so far are strongly linear, and the only thing that differs is the sign of the
change. That is exactly what ``direction'' names --- nothing else.}

\problem{\textbf{Plot D --- reading the form.}\par
\textbf{(a)} Scan the heights, not the $x$-values. The lowest point on Plot D is $y = 6$,
and it sits above $x = \ans{4}$.\par
\textbf{(b)} From left to right the points \emph{fall} from $30$ down to $6$, and then
\emph{rise} again to $33$. A straight line cannot do that: a line goes one way only. So the
form is \textbf{nonlinear} --- here, a U-shaped (curved) pattern.\par
\textbf{Why the turning point is the evidence:} the $x$-value where the pattern reverses is
the thing a line does not have. Naming it is a more convincing answer than ``it looks
curvy''.}

\problem{\textbf{Residuals: how far one point sits from its trend.}\par
\textbf{(a)} Plot A, at $x = 5$: the line predicts $5(5) + 47 = 72$ and the point is at
$73$. Residual is actual minus predicted, in that order:
\[ 73 - 72 \]
The Plot A residual is $\ans{1}$.\par
\textbf{(b)} Plot E, at $x = 5$: the line predicts $5(5) + 25 = 50$ and the point is at
$44$:
\[ 44 - 50 \]
The Plot E residual is $\ans{-6}$.\par
\textbf{(c)} The Plot A point is closer to its line. Its residual is one unit from zero,
while the Plot E residual is six units from zero --- and it is the \emph{distance} from
zero that matters, not the sign. The negative sign on the Plot E residual says only that
the point sits \emph{below} its line.\par
\textit{Watch for:} predicted minus actual, which flips both signs. It gives the same
distances, so (c) still comes out right, but the sign then means the opposite of what the
student says it means. Insist on actual $-$ predicted.}

\problem{\textbf{Plot C --- what ``no association'' looks like.}\par
\textbf{(a)} The highest $y$-value on Plot C is $63$, sitting above $x = \ans{6}$.\par
\textbf{(b)} The lowest is $41$, sitting above $x = \ans{5}$.\par
\textbf{(c)} On Plot A the highest and lowest values sat at the two \emph{ends} --- lowest
on the far left, highest on the far right --- which is what a direction is. Here the two
extremes sit side by side in the middle of the plot, at $x = 5$ and $x = 6$. Knowing $x$
tells you nothing useful about $y$: the points zig-zag between about $41$ and $63$ all the
way across. That is \textbf{no association}.\par
\textbf{The habit worth building:} ``no association'' is a description, not a failure to
find one. A student should be able to say what would have to be true for there to be a
direction, and then point out that it isn't.}

\problem{\textbf{Plot F --- finding the outlier.}\par
\textbf{(a)} The lowest $y$-value on Plot F is $10$, and it sits above $x = \ans{7}$.\par
\textit{Watch for:} answering $1$. That is the smallest $x$-value, not the location of the
smallest $y$-value --- the question asks where the lowest \emph{height} is. The point at
$x = 1$ has $y = 20$, which is twice the height of the point at $x = 7$.\par
\textbf{(b)} Cover $(7, 10)$ and the remaining seven points read
$20, 26, 31, 37, 42, 48, 59$ --- climbing by roughly $5$ or $6$ each step. Direction:
\textbf{positive}. Form: \textbf{linear}. Strength: \textbf{strong} (the points sit almost
exactly on a line).\par
\textbf{(c)} The outlier makes the association \emph{look} weaker than it is. One point
sitting $35$ or so below the pattern spoils the visual impression of a tight line, even
though seven of the eight points are nearly perfectly linear. The honest description of
Plot F is ``strong positive linear association with one outlier at $x = 7$'' --- and that
sentence is more useful than either ``strong'' or ``weak'' alone.}

\problem{\textbf{Putting a number on strength.}\par
\textbf{(a)} Range means largest value minus smallest value.\par
Plot A residuals run from $-1$ to $1$:
\[ 1 - (-1) \]
so the range is $\ans{2}$.\par
Plot E residuals run from $-7$ to $8$:
\[ 8 - (-7) \]
so the range is $\ans{15}$.\par
\textbf{(b)} \textbf{Plot A is stronger.} Its residual range of $2$ means every one of its
eight points sits within one unit of the trend line; Plot E's range of $15$ means its
points wander up to eight units either side.\par
\textbf{What the range of the residuals measures:} the width of the band the points occupy
around the line --- how thick the cloud is. That is precisely what ``strength'' means. It
is not the slope: both plots here have the same trend slope of $5$, and they differ enormously
in strength. Slope answers ``how fast'', strength answers ``how reliably''.\par
This is the single most valuable idea on the sheet, and the one problem 10 comes back to.}

\problem{\textbf{Plot D --- why ``direction'' is the wrong question.}\par
\textbf{(a)} The highest $y$-value anywhere on Plot D is $\ans{33}$, at $x = 8$.\par
\textbf{(b)} The student's evidence is correct: the last point ($33$) really is higher than
the first ($30$). The conclusion is still wrong, and the reason is \textbf{form}.\par
``Positive association'' means $y$ tends to increase \emph{throughout} --- it is a statement
about the whole plot, not about its two end points. Plot D's form is a curve: $y$ falls for
the first half and rises for the second. Comparing only the ends hides everything in
between, and here the two ends happen to be nearly the same height ($30$ and $33$) with a
value of $6$ buried in the middle.\par
\textbf{The rule to state:} check the form \emph{before} you name a direction. On a
nonlinear plot, ``positive'' and ``negative'' do not describe anything --- the right
description is ``decreasing then increasing''.}

\problem{\textbf{What one outlier does to a summary.}\par
\textbf{(a)} Add all eight $y$-values and divide by $8$:
\[ 20 + 26 + 31 + 37 + 42 + 48 + 10 + 59 = 273 \qquad \frac{273}{8} \]
The mean is $\ans{34.125}$.\par
\textbf{(b)} Drop the outlier $10$ and divide the remaining total by $7$:
\[ 273 - 10 = 263 \qquad \frac{263}{7} = 37.5714\ldots \]
Rounded to the nearest hundredth, $\ans{37.57}$.\par
\textbf{(c)} Removing one value out of eight moved the mean by about $3.4$ --- from $34.13$
up to $37.57$. That is a large move for a single point, and it happens because the mean
adds every value in with equal weight, so a value far from the rest pulls hard.\par
\textbf{What it shows:} in a small data set, one unusual point can drive a summary. Before
quoting a mean, look at the plot and ask whether one point is doing the work. This is the
numerical version of what problem 6(c) said in words.\par
\textit{Watch for:} subtracting the outlier from the total but still dividing by $8$, which
gives $263 \div 8 = 32.875$ --- a ``mean without the outlier'' that is lower than the mean
with it, which cannot be right when the value removed was the smallest one.}

\problem{\textbf{Challenge: two complete descriptions, and a claim to judge.} (Open
synthesis --- read the reasoning.)\par
\textbf{Plot A.} \emph{Direction:} positive --- the leftmost $y$ is $52$ and the rightmost
is $88$, and the points climb steadily throughout. \emph{Form:} linear --- the points follow
the straight line $y = 5x + 47$ with no bend. \emph{Strength:} strong --- the residuals
range only from $-1$ to $1$, so every point sits within one unit of the line.\par
\textbf{Plot E.} \emph{Direction:} positive --- $y$ tends to rise from $35$ at the left to
$71$ at the right, though it zig-zags on the way. \emph{Form:} linear --- there is no bend or
turning point; the trend is $y = 5x + 25$. \emph{Strength:} weak to moderate --- the
residuals range from $-7$ to $8$, so points sit as much as eight units off the line.\par
\textbf{Judging the claim.} \emph{Disagree.} Plot E does show a positive linear
association, so there is a link, but ``strong'' is not supported. The residual range of
$15$ is more than seven times Plot A's range of $2$, and individual points miss the line by
up to eight units --- more than the whole spread of Plot A's points. A defensible sentence
is: ``Plot E shows a positive linear association of moderate strength; predictions from its
trend line could easily be off by seven or eight.''\par
\textbf{Grading:} full credit needs (i) all three words used for both plots, (ii) a number
cited for each --- the end values for direction, the absence of a turning point for form,
the residual range for strength --- and (iii) a rejection of ``strong'' that cites the
spread rather than the slope. The commonest wrong answer agrees with the claim because
Plot E's trend line rises steeply. Slope is not strength: both plots have slope $5$.\par
Open synthesis --- $\ans{\text{open response}}$.}
