\problem{$f(x) = 3x - 1$, $g(x) = x^2 + 2$. (a) $f(g(2))$. (b) $g(f(2))$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Inner rule first: $g(2) = 2^2 + 2 = 6$, then $f(6) = 3(6) - 1$. As a
single rule, $f(g(x)) = 3(x^2 + 2) - 1 = 3x^2 + 5$. \ans{17}

\textbf{Solution (b):} Now $f$ is the inner rule: $f(2) = 3(2) - 1 = 5$, then
$g(5) = 5^2 + 2$. As a single rule, $g(f(x)) = (3x - 1)^2 + 2$. \ans{27}

\vspace{0.1cm}
\noindent\emph{The point of the pair:} same two rules, same input, two different answers.
Composition is not commutative, and this is the fact every later problem leans on.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{A student gave $-2$ for $(f\circ g)(6)$ with $f(x) = \frac{x}{2} + 7$, $g(x) = 8 - x$. (a) Correct value. (b) Which composition did the student do?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The inner rule is $g$, so it acts on $6$ first.
\begin{align*}
  g(6) &= 8 - 6 = 2 \\
  f(2) &= \frac{2}{2} + 7 = 1 + 7
\end{align*}
So the correct value is \ans{8}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The student computed $g(f(6))$:
$f(6) = 3 + 7 = 10$, then $g(10) = 8 - 10 = -2$. A correct response says the rules were
applied in the wrong order --- $g$ must act on the input first, $f$ second --- or
equivalently identifies $-2$ as $g(f(6))$. Naming which rule was used first is required;
``arithmetic slip'' is not a diagnosis and earns nothing.

\problem{$f(x) = 3x + 12$; a student claims the inverse is $\frac{x}{3} + 12$. (a) Round-trip $5$. (b) Correct $f^{-1}(x)$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $f(5) = 3(5) + 12 = 27$. Feeding $27$ into the claimed rule:
\begin{align*}
  \frac{27}{3} + 12 &= 9 + 12
\end{align*}
The round trip returns \ans{21} --- not the $5$ it started from, so the claim is false.

\textbf{Solution (b):} Write $x = 3y + 12$ and solve for $y$.
\begin{align*}
  x - 12 &= 3y \\
  y &= \frac{x - 12}{3}
\end{align*}
The $12$ must be removed \emph{before} dividing, because it was added last. \ans{f^{-1}(x) = \dfrac{x - 12}{3}}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{A student claims $\sqrt{x-1}$ inverts $h(x) = x^2 + 1$ for every input. (a) Round-trip $-3$. (b) What does that show?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $h(-3) = 9 + 1 = 10$, then $\sqrt{10 - 1} = \sqrt{9}$. The output is
\ans{3}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The round trip returns $3$, not the $-3$ it
started from, so the claimed rule is not an inverse on all inputs. The reason: squaring
destroys the sign, so $-3$ and $3$ share the output $10$, and no single rule can send $10$
back to both. Full credit needs the failed round trip \emph{and} the two-inputs-one-output
(not one-to-one) reason. A student who adds that the claim becomes true once the inputs of
$h$ are restricted to $x \ge 0$ has the complete picture.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{A student wrote $(g\circ f)(x) = x^2 - 4$ for $f(x) = x - 4$, $g(x) = x^2$. (a) Correct rule. (b) Its value at $7$. (c) The error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $f$ is the inner rule, so subtract first and square the result.
\begin{align*}
  (g \circ f)(x) &= g(x - 4) = (x - 4)^2 \\
                 &= x^2 - 8x + 16
\end{align*}
\ans{(g\circ f)(x) = x^2 - 8x + 16}

\textbf{Solution (b):} $(7 - 4)^2 = 3^2$, giving \ans{9}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} The student squared first and subtracted
afterwards, which is $(f \circ g)(x) = x^2 - 4$ --- the other composition. At $x = 7$ that
rule gives $45$ instead of $9$. A correct response names the \emph{order}: the inner rule
$f$ subtracts, and only then does $g$ square. Reporting the two different numbers without
naming the order is not a diagnosis.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$k(x) = \sqrt{x} + 3$. (a) Find $k^{-1}(x)$. (b) State and justify the restriction it must carry.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Write $x = \sqrt{y} + 3$ and undo the $+3$ before squaring.
\begin{align*}
  x - 3 &= \sqrt{y} \\
  y &= (x - 3)^2
\end{align*}
\ans{k^{-1}(x) = (x - 3)^2}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} Required: inputs of the inverse must satisfy
$x \ge 3$. The justification: $\sqrt{x}$ is never negative, so $k$ never outputs anything
below $3$; the inverse's inputs are exactly the outputs of $k$. Without the restriction the
squaring rule would happily accept $x = 1$ and return $4$, but $k(4) = 5$, not $1$ --- the
round trip fails. Full credit needs the restriction \emph{and} its tie to the range of $k$;
the counterexample is the strongest form.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{A student claims the inverse of $f(x) = \frac{x+5}{x-2}$ is $\frac{x-2}{x+5}$. (a) Correct $f^{-1}(x)$. (b) Its value at $3$. (c) Why upside down is wrong.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Write $x = \dfrac{y + 5}{y - 2}$, clear the fraction, collect $y$.
\begin{align*}
  x(y - 2) &= y + 5 \\
  xy - 2x &= y + 5 \\
  xy - y &= 2x + 5 \\
  y(x - 1) &= 2x + 5
\end{align*}
\ans{f^{-1}(x) = \dfrac{2x + 5}{x - 1}}

\textbf{Solution (b):} $\dfrac{2(3) + 5}{3 - 1} = \dfrac{11}{2}$, so \ans{f^{-1}(3) = \dfrac{11}{2}}
The student's upside-down rule gives $\frac{3-2}{3+5} = \frac{1}{8}$ instead --- nowhere near.

\vspace{0.15cm}
\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} Turning a rule upside down produces its
\emph{reciprocal}, $1/f(x)$, which undoes multiplication only. An inverse undoes the entire
rule and is found by solving $x = f(y)$ for $y$. A correct response draws that distinction,
or demonstrates it: $f\bigl(\frac{x-2}{x+5}\bigr)$ does not simplify to $x$.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$f(x) = x + 3$, $g(x) = x^2$; $(f\circ g)(x) = x^2+3$. (a) $(g\circ f)(x)$. (b) The one input where they agree. (c) Why only one.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $g(f(x)) = (x + 3)^2$, expanded:
\[ (x + 3)^2 = x^2 + 6x + 9 \]
\ans{(g\circ f)(x) = x^2 + 6x + 9}

\textbf{Solution (b):} Set the two composed rules equal.
\begin{align*}
  x^2 + 3 &= x^2 + 6x + 9 \\
  0 &= 6x + 6 \\
  x &= -1
\end{align*}
Check: $(-1)^2 + 3 = 4$ and $(-1 + 3)^2 = 4$ $\checkmark$ \ans{x = -1}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} When the two rules are set equal the $x^2$
terms cancel, leaving the linear equation $6x + 6 = 0$, and a linear equation with a nonzero
slope has exactly one solution. A correct response names that cancellation (or argues that
the difference of the two rules, $6x + 6$, is linear and not constant), so the graphs cross
once. Asserting only ``they are different functions'' does not answer the question.
