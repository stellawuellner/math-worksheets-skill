\noindent\small\textbf{What this sheet is teaching, and what to listen for.} A younger
child reads a graph by counting: one picture, one thing. A \emph{scaled} graph breaks that
habit on purpose --- one picture is worth four books, one grid line is worth two bikes ---
and the child has to look at the key or the scale \emph{before} counting anything. Almost
every wrong answer on this sheet comes from skipping that look. When you hear a number
that is much too small, do not correct it: point at the key and ask ``what is one picture
worth?''\normalsize

\problem{\textbf{How many books did Ana read?}

\par\smallskip
Ana's row has $5$ book pictures. The key says one picture stands for $4$ books, so the
count goes in fours: $4, 8, 12, 16, 20$.
\par
Pictures: $\ans{5}$. \quad Books: $5 \times 4 = \ans{20}$.
\par
\textbf{If they say 5:} they have counted pictures instead of books. Cover the row with
your hand except the first picture and ask ``how many books is just that one?'' Then let
them count again.}

\problem{\textbf{How many books did Ben read?}

\par\smallskip
Ben's row has $3$ book pictures. Skip-counting by fours: $4, 8, 12$.
\par
Books Ben read: $3 \times 4 = \ans{12}$.
\par
\textbf{The skip-count is the answer they should write down}, not just the total. A child
who can say ``four, eight, twelve'' while touching each picture has the idea; a child who
writes 12 with no working may have copied it from a neighbour's sheet or guessed.}

\problem{\textbf{How many more books did Cara read than Ana?}

\par\smallskip
Two steps, and the first one is easy to skip.
\par
Cara has $6$ pictures: $6 \times 4 = \ans{24}$ books. \quad
Ana has $5$ pictures: $5 \times 4 = \ans{20}$ books.
\par
Now compare the \emph{books}, not the pictures: $24 - 20 = \ans{4}$.
\par
\textbf{Watch for the answer 1.} A child who compares the pictures ($6 - 5 = 1$) has
compared the wrong quantity. The question asks about books, and one extra picture is four
extra books. This is the most useful conversation on the whole sheet --- worth having even
if they got it right.}

\problem{\textbf{How many bikes were in the rack on Wednesday?}

\par\smallskip
Wednesday's bar is the tallest. Its top sits exactly on a grid line. The scale is labelled
$0, 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14$, so the line the bar reaches is the one labelled $12$.
\par
Bikes on Wednesday: $\ans{12}$.
\par
\textbf{If they say 6:} they have counted grid lines ($1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$) instead of
reading the number printed beside the line. Both are ``counting'', which is why it is such
a natural mistake. Ask them to read the number written at the top of the bar's line out
loud.}

\problem{\textbf{How many bikes were in the rack in the whole week?}

\par\smallskip
Read all five bars first and write the numbers down --- the addition is the easy part.
\par
Mon $\ans{8}$, \quad Tue $\ans{7}$, \quad Wed $\ans{12}$, \quad Thu $\ans{10}$, \quad
Fri $\ans{4}$.
\par
Adding: $8 + 7 = 15$, \ $15 + 12 = 27$, \ $27 + 10 = 37$, \ $37 + 4 = 41$.
\par
Bikes altogether: $\ans{41}$.
\par
\textbf{A friendlier order.} Pairs that make tens first: $8 + 12 = 20$, then
$10 + 7 + 4 = 21$, and $20 + 21 = 41$. If the child finds a shortcut like that, it is
worth more than the answer.}

\problem{\textbf{Tuesday is tricky.}

\par\smallskip
Tuesday's bar stops between the grid line labelled $\ans{6}$ and the grid line labelled
$\ans{8}$ --- exactly halfway.
\par
Each gap on this scale is worth $2$ bikes, so half a gap is worth $1$ bike. Halfway
between $6$ and $8$ is $6 + 1$.
\par
Bikes on Tuesday: $\ans{7}$.
\par
\textbf{If they say 8 (or 6):} they have rounded the bar up to the nearest labelled line
rather than reading where it actually stops. Lay a ruler across the top of the bar to the
scale and let them see that it lands between two numbers. On a scale of twos, ``between''
always means the odd number in the middle.}

\problem{\textbf{Dev's half picture.}

\par\smallskip
A whole picture is worth $\ans{4}$ books, so a half picture is worth half of four, which
is $\ans{2}$ books.
\par
Dev's row: two whole pictures and one half. $4 + 4 + 2 = \ans{10}$.
\par
\textbf{If they say 12:} they have counted the half picture as a whole one --- three
pictures, three fours. It is the commonest slip on scaled picture graphs, and it is worth
asking ``is that last one the same size as the others?'' rather than saying it is wrong.
\par
\textbf{If they say 3 or 2.5:} they are still counting pictures rather than books. Go back
to the key.}

\problem{\textbf{Talk it out.} (Open explanation --- listen to the reason, not the number.)

\par\smallskip
Jo counted the pictures and stopped there. What Jo forgot is the \textbf{key} at the
bottom of the graph: one whole book picture stands for $4$ books, not $1$ book. So five
pictures means five groups of four, and $5 \times 4 = 20$. Ana read $20$ books.
\par
\textbf{Answers that earn full credit} name the key, or describe the counting: ``you have
to go 4, 8, 12, 16, 20'', ``each picture is 4 books so you times it by 4'', ``the little
writing at the bottom says one picture is 4 books''.
\par
\textbf{Answers to push on gently:} ``Jo counted wrong'' (Jo counted the pictures
perfectly --- the pictures are not what the question asked about), and ``Jo should have
looked at the graph better'' (which part of it?). Ask ``what would Ana's row look like if
one picture meant one book?'' --- twenty pictures --- and let them see why the key exists.
\par
\textbf{A good sign:} a child who says the graph would be too long to fit without a key
has understood what scaling is \emph{for}, which is more than the sheet asked.
\par
Open explanation --- $\ans{\text{open response}}$.}
