\problem{\textbf{$y = (x-2)^2 - 1$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} This is vertex form $y = a(x-h)^2 + k$ with $a = 1$, $h = 2$,
$k = -1$. The subtraction inside the parentheses is what makes $h$ positive.

\textbf{Step 2.} Check by substituting the axis value: $y(2) = (2-2)^2 - 1 =
-1$, which is the lowest point because $a = 1 > 0$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Vertex $\ans{(2,\,-1)}$, opening upward.

\textbf{Sketch.} Axis $x = 2$; $y$-intercept $y(0) = 3$; by symmetry the curve
also passes through $(4,3)$, and the $x$-intercepts are $1$ and $3$.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} a vertex $y$-value of $15$ --- the sign of $h$ was
read straight off the parentheses, putting the vertex at $x = -2$.}

\problem{\textbf{$y = -(x+3)^2 + 4$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Rewrite the inside as $(x - (-3))$, so $h = -3$ and $k = 4$.
The leading coefficient is $-1$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $y(-3) = -(0)^2 + 4 = 4$, and because $a = -1 < 0$ this point
is a maximum: the parabola opens downward.

\textbf{Step 3.} Vertex $\ans{(-3,\,4)}$, opening downward.

\textbf{Sketch.} Axis $x = -3$; $y$-intercept $y(0) = -9 + 4 = -5$; symmetric
point $(-6,-5)$; $x$-intercepts at $-5$ and $-1$.}

\problem{\textbf{$y = x^2 - 6x + 5$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Standard form with $a = 1$, $b = -6$, $c = 5$. The axis of
symmetry is $x = -\dfrac{b}{2a} = -\dfrac{-6}{2} = 3$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Substitute to get the vertex $y$-value:
$y(3) = 9 - 18 + 5 = -4$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Vertex $\ans{(3,\,-4)}$; the $y$-intercept is $c$, that is
$y(0) = \ans{5}$

\textbf{Sketch.} Opens up; $x$-intercepts from $(x-1)(x-5)$ are $1$ and $5$,
and they straddle $x = 3$ as they must.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $32$ --- $x = \frac{b}{2a}$ was used instead of
$-\frac{b}{2a}$, putting the axis at $x = -3$.}

\problem{\textbf{$y = x^2 - 4x - 5$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The $x$-intercepts are where $y = 0$, so factor: two numbers
multiplying to $-5$ and adding to $-4$ are $-5$ and $1$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $x^2 - 4x - 5 = (x-5)(x+1)$, so $x - 5 = 0$ or $x + 1 = 0$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $x$-intercepts $\ans{x = -1 \text{ and } x = 5}$

\textbf{Sketch.} The axis of symmetry is midway between them, at $x = 2$, and
$y(2) = -9$, so the vertex is $(2,-9)$. The $y$-intercept is $-5$.}

\problem{\textbf{$y = 2(x-1)^2 - 8$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Vertex form with $h = 1$, $k = -8$, $a = 2$, so
$y(1) = \ans{-8}$ and the parabola opens up, narrower than $y = x^2$.

\textbf{Step 2.} For the $x$-intercepts set $y = 0$: $2(x-1)^2 = 8$, so
$(x-1)^2 = 4$ and $x - 1 = \pm 2$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $x$-intercepts $\ans{x = -1 \text{ and } x = 3}$

\textbf{Sketch.} Vertex $(1,-8)$, axis $x = 1$, $y$-intercept $y(0) = -6$.

\textit{Note:} vertex form hands you the intercepts in two moves --- no
factoring and no quadratic formula.}

\problem{\textbf{$y = -x^2 + 4x + 5$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Here $a = -1$, $b = 4$, $c = 5$. Axis of symmetry:
$x = -\dfrac{4}{2(-1)} = 2$. Keeping the sign of $a$ is the whole difficulty.

\textbf{Step 2.} $y(2) = -4 + 8 + 5 = 9$. Because $a < 0$, this is a
\emph{maximum}.

\textbf{Step 3.} Vertex $\ans{(2,\,9)}$, a maximum; $y$-intercept
$y(0) = \ans{5}$

\textbf{Sketch.} Opens down; $-x^2+4x+5 = -(x-5)(x+1)$, so the $x$-intercepts
are $-1$ and $5$.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $-7$ --- the minus sign on $a$ was dropped in
$-\frac{b}{2a}$, putting the axis at $x = -2$.}

\problem{\textbf{$y = x^2 - 9$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} There is no $x$ term, so $b = 0$ and the axis of symmetry is
$x = -\frac{0}{2} = 0$ --- the $y$-axis itself.

\textbf{Step 2.} Set $y = 0$: $x^2 = 9$, a difference of squares
$(x-3)(x+3) = 0$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $x$-intercepts $\ans{x = -3 \text{ and } x = 3}$

\textbf{Sketch.} Vertex $(0,-9)$ on the $y$-axis, opening up. The two
intercepts are mirror images precisely because the axis is $x = 0$.}

\problem{\textbf{$y = \frac{1}{2}(x+2)^2 - 2$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Vertex form with $h = -2$, $k = -2$, $a = \frac{1}{2}$. Since
$|a| < 1$, the parabola is \emph{wider} than $y = x^2$: at one unit from the
axis it rises only $\frac{1}{2}$ instead of $1$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $y(-2) = \ans{-2}$, the minimum. For the intercepts set
$y = 0$: $\frac{1}{2}(x+2)^2 = 2$, so $(x+2)^2 = 4$ and $x + 2 = \pm 2$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $x$-intercepts $\ans{x = -4 \text{ and } x = 0}$

\textbf{Sketch.} Vertex $(-2,-2)$, axis $x = -2$; the curve passes through the
origin, which is one of the two intercepts.}

\problem{\textbf{$y = x^2 + 2x - 3$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} $a = 1$, $b = 2$: the axis is $x = -\dfrac{2}{2} = -1$, and
$y(-1) = 1 - 2 - 3 = -4$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Factor for the intercepts: two numbers with product $-3$ and
sum $2$ are $3$ and $-1$, giving $(x+3)(x-1)$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Vertex $\ans{(-1,\,-4)}$; $x$-intercepts
$\ans{x = -3 \text{ and } x = 1}$

\textbf{Sketch.} Opens up, $y$-intercept $-3$. Notice the vertex $x$-value
$-1$ is the average of $-3$ and $1$ --- the two routes agree, which is a free
check.}

\problem{\textbf{$y = 2x^2 - 8x + 6$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Factor the leading coefficient out first:
$2(x^2 - 4x + 3) = 2(x-1)(x-3)$. A common factor left in place is where sign
errors breed.

\textbf{Step 2.} Setting each factor to zero gives the $x$-intercepts
$\ans{x = 1 \text{ and } x = 3}$

\textbf{Step 3.} The axis of symmetry is midway between the intercepts, at
$x = 2$, and $y(2) = 8 - 16 + 6 = -2$, so the vertex is at
$\ans{(2,\,-2)}$

\textbf{Sketch.} Opens up and is narrow ($a = 2$); the $y$-intercept is $6$.}

\problem{\textbf{$y = x^2 - 6x + 4$ --- complete the square.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Take half of $b = -6$, giving $-3$, and square it: $9$. Add
and subtract that inside the expression:
$x^2 - 6x + 9 - 9 + 4$.

\textbf{Step 2.} The first three terms are a perfect square:
$(x-3)^2 - 5$. Expanding back gives $x^2 - 6x + 9 - 5 = x^2 - 6x + 4$, so the
two forms are the same function.

\textbf{Step 3.} Vertex form: $\ans{y = (x-3)^2 - 5}$, so the vertex is
$(3,\,\ans{-5})$

\textbf{Sketch.} Axis $x = 3$, opens up, $y$-intercept $4$; the
$x$-intercepts are $3 \pm \sqrt{5}$, about $0.8$ and $5.2$.}

\problem{\textbf{Vertex $(3,-4)$, through $(5,0)$.} \emph{The sketch and the
explanation are open work, flagged for manual review; the model answer below is
what full credit contains.}

\textbf{Step 1.} A vertex of $(3,-4)$ fixes $h$ and $k$, so the equation must
look like $y = a(x-3)^2 - 4$. Only $a$ is still unknown, and one more point is
exactly enough to find it.

\textbf{Step 2.} Substitute $(5,0)$: $0 = a(5-3)^2 - 4 = 4a - 4$, so $a = 1$
and the equation is $y = (x-3)^2 - 4$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Check the given point in the finished equation:
$(5-3)^2 - 4 = 4 - 4$, so $y = \ans{0}$ at $x = 5$, as required.

\textbf{Step 4 (the sketch).} Vertex $(3,-4)$, axis $x = 3$, opens up.
$x$-intercepts: $(x-3)^2 = 4$ gives $x = 1$ and $x = 5$. $y$-intercept
$y(0) = 5$.

\textit{Full credit:} the equation $y = (x-3)^2 - 4$, a sketch labelled with
the vertex, the axis $x = 3$ and the intercepts $1$ and $5$, and a sentence
saying that the vertex fixes $h$ and $k$ while the extra point is the only
thing that can determine the stretch factor $a$.}
