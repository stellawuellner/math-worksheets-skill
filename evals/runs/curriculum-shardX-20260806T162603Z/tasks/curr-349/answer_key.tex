\problem{Translation maps $A(3, -2)$ to $A'(7, 1)$. Ana writes $(x, y) \to (x - 4,\ y - 3)$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The vector runs \emph{from} the original \emph{to} the image, so each component is image minus original.
\[ 7 - 3 \]
So the horizontal component is \ans{4}

\textbf{Solution (b):} $1 - (-2) = 1 + 2$, so the vertical component is \ans{3}

The correct rule is $(x, y) \to (x + 4,\ y + 3)$.

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} A model response: Ana subtracted in the wrong order, taking original minus image instead of image minus original. That flips the sign of both components, so her rule points the opposite way: applied to $A(3, -2)$ it gives $(-1, -5)$, which is nowhere near $A'(7, 1)$.

\emph{Full credit:} states the image-minus-original order and says the reversal reverses the direction. \emph{Half credit:} gives the correct order only. \emph{No credit:} fixes the numbers with no account of the error.

\problem{Dilation, centre the origin, factor $3$, applied to $B(-2, 5)$. Ben reports $B'(1, 8)$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} A dilation about the origin \emph{multiplies} each coordinate by the scale factor.
\[ x' = 3(-2) = -6, \qquad y' = 3(5) = 15 \]
So the coordinates are \ans{-6} and \ans{15}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} A model response: Ben added $3$ to each coordinate instead of multiplying by $3$. Adding a fixed amount to both coordinates is a \emph{translation}, so what he actually performed was a slide of $3$ right and $3$ up. A slide leaves the figure exactly the same size, which is the opposite of what a dilation with factor $3$ is meant to do.

\emph{Full credit:} states the multiplying rule and names the move he actually made as a translation. \emph{Half credit:} gives the rule without naming his move. \emph{No credit:} redoes the arithmetic with no diagnosis.

\problem{Similar triangles with sides $9$ cm and $15$ cm. Cara says the scale factor is $6$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} A scale factor is a ratio of matching sides.
\[ k = \frac{15}{9} = \frac{5}{3} = 1.6666\ldots \]
So \ans{k \approx 1.67}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} A model response: a scale factor answers "how many times as long", which is a division, not a subtraction. Cara's $6$ is a difference of two lengths, so it is measured in centimetres --- a scale factor has no unit at all, because the units cancel in the ratio. Her method would also give the same "factor" for a $1$ cm side becoming $7$ cm, which is clearly a much bigger enlargement than $9$ cm to $15$ cm.

\emph{Full credit:} names the quotient rule and explains why a difference cannot be a scale factor. \emph{Half credit:} gives the division without the reason. \emph{No credit:} states the value alone.

\problem{Dilation, centre $C(1, 4)$, factor $2$, applied to $P(5, 6)$. A student reports $P'(10, 12)$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Measure the offset from the centre, scale it, and add it back to the centre.
\begin{align*}
  x' &= 1 + 2(5 - 1) = 1 + 8 = 9 \\
  y' &= 4 + 2(6 - 4) = 4 + 4 = 8
\end{align*}
So the coordinates are \ans{9} and \ans{8}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} A model response: the centre of a dilation is the one point that does not move, and every other point moves directly away from it by the scale factor. Using the origin scales each point's offset \emph{from the origin} instead of from $C(1, 4)$, so the image comes out the right size but in the wrong place. Here the student's $P'(10, 12)$ is twice as far from the origin as $P$; the correct $P'(9, 8)$ is twice as far from $C$.

\emph{Full credit:} says the centre is fixed and explains what using the origin scales instead. \emph{Half credit:} states the centre form of the rule only. \emph{No credit:} recomputes with no diagnosis.

\problem{Similar rectangles of area $32$ cm$^2$ and $72$ cm$^2$. A student says the linear scale factor is $2.25$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Areas of similar figures scale by $k^2$, so the area ratio \emph{is} $k^2$.
\[ k^2 = \frac{72}{32} = 2.25 \qquad k = \sqrt{2.25} \]
So \ans{k = 1.5}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} A model response: when every length is multiplied by $k$, both dimensions of a rectangle are stretched, so the area is multiplied by $k^2$. The ratio $72 \div 32$ is therefore already the square of the factor, and a square root is needed before it can be used as a length ratio. The student's $2.25$ would only be the linear factor if area scaled the same way length does, which it does not. Checking: a $4 \times 8$ rectangle scaled by $1.5$ becomes $6 \times 12$, whose area is $72$ $\checkmark$

\emph{Full credit:} explains why the given ratio is $k^2$ and names the square-root step. \emph{Half credit:} names the square root without the reason. \emph{No credit:} states the value alone.

\problem{$\overline{MN}$ with $M(2, 3)$, $N(6, 3)$ dilated by $3$ about $M$. A student used the origin and got $M'(6, 9)$, $N'(18, 9)$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} With $M$ as the centre, $M$ stays put and $N$'s offset from $M$ is tripled.
\[ x' = 2 + 3(6 - 2) = 2 + 12 \]
So the correct $x$-coordinate of $N'$ is \ans{14}

\textbf{Solution (b):} The correct image runs from $M'(2, 3)$ to $N'(14, 3)$, so its length is $|14 - 2|$. \ans{12}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} A model response: a dilation multiplies every length by the scale factor no matter which centre is chosen, so both images are $3 \times 4 = 12$ units long --- the student's segment from $(6, 9)$ to $(18, 9)$ measures $12$ as well. What the centre decides is \emph{where} the image lands. Dilating about the origin moved $M$ to $(6, 9)$, but the problem specified $M$ as the centre, so $M$ was supposed to stay exactly where it was.

\emph{Full credit:} says lengths are preserved under any centre and identifies position as what the centre controls. \emph{Half credit:} notes the lengths agree with no account of the centre's role. \emph{No credit:} calls the student's answer wholly right or wholly wrong.
