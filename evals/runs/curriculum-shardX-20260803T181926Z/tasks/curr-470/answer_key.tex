\problem{\textbf{Differentiate the cubic.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Every term is a power of $x$, so the power rule alone is
enough --- no product or chain rule is hiding here.

\textbf{Step 2.} Term by term: $3x^2$ from $x^3$, $-12x$ from $-6x^2$, $9$ from
$9x$, and $0$ from the constant $2$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $\ans{f'(x) = 3x^2 - 12x + 9}$.}

\problem{\textbf{Critical numbers and increasing intervals.}

\textbf{Step 1.} $f$ is a polynomial, so $f'$ exists everywhere and the only
critical numbers are the zeros of $f'$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $3x^2 - 12x + 9 = 3(x^2 - 4x + 3) = 3(x - 1)(x - 3) = 0$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The critical numbers are $\ans{x = 1 \text{ and } x = 3}$.

\textbf{Step 4.} Sign of $f' = 3(x-1)(x-3)$: both factors negative for $x < 1$
(product positive), signs opposite on $1 < x < 3$ (negative), both positive for
$x > 3$. So $f$ increases on $(-\infty, 1)$ and on $(3, \infty)$ and decreases
on $(1, 3)$.

\textit{Grading note:} the sign chart, or an explicit test value in each
interval, is the justification. ``$f'$ is a parabola opening upward'' with no
values shown is not.}

\problem{\textbf{Expanding balloon (related rates).}

\textbf{Step 1.} Radius and volume both change with time, so differentiate the
relationship with respect to $t$ first and substitute afterwards.

\textbf{Step 2.} From $V = \dfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$, the chain rule gives
$\dfrac{dV}{dt} = 4\pi r^2 \dfrac{dr}{dt}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} At the required instant $r = 5$ and $\dfrac{dr}{dt} = 2$, so
$\dfrac{dV}{dt} = 4\pi(25)(2) = \ans{200\pi}$ cubic centimetres per second.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $314.16$ --- the $\dfrac{dr}{dt}$ factor was
dropped, which computes the surface area rather than a rate.}

\problem{\textbf{Fenced garden (optimization).}

\textbf{(a) Step 1.} $A(x) = x(60 - 2x) = 60x - 2x^2$ is already a function of
one variable on the physical domain $0 < x < 30$, so differentiate it directly.

\textbf{Step 2.} $A'(x) = 60 - 4x = 0$ gives $4x = 60$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $\ans{x = 15}$ metres.

\textbf{Step 4.} Justification: $A''(x) = -4 < 0$ everywhere, so the graph of
$A$ is concave down and this single critical point is the absolute maximum on
the interval.

\textbf{(b) Step 5.} The other side measures $60 - 2(15) = 30$ metres, so
$A(15) = 15 \cdot 30$.

\textbf{Step 6.} The maximum area is $\ans{450}$ square metres.}

\problem{\textbf{Where $f'' = 0$, and which of those are inflection points.}

\textbf{Step 1.} An inflection point needs a change of concavity, so start from
the second derivative and then test it for a sign change --- $f'' = 0$ alone is
not enough.

\textbf{Step 2.} $f(x) = x^4 - 4x^3$ gives $f'(x) = 4x^3 - 12x^2$ and
$f''(x) = 12x^2 - 24x = 12x(x - 2)$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $f''(x) = 0$ at $\ans{x = 0 \text{ and } x = 2}$.

\textbf{Step 4.} Sign of $f''$: positive for $x < 0$, negative on $0 < x < 2$,
positive again for $x > 2$. The concavity changes at both, so both are
inflection points, at $(0, 0)$ and $(2, -16)$.

\textit{Teaching note:} contrast this with $g(x) = x^4$, where $g''(0) = 0$ but
$g''$ never changes sign, so the origin is not an inflection point. That is why
Step 4 exists.}

\problem{\textbf{Sliding ladder (related rates).}

\textbf{Step 1.} Let $x$ be the distance from wall to foot and $y$ the height
of the top. The ladder length is constant, so $x^2 + y^2 = 169$ holds at every
instant and can be differentiated in $t$.

\textbf{Step 2.} At the instant asked about, $y = \sqrt{169 - 25} = 12$.
Compute $y$ from the relation --- it is not a given.

\textbf{Step 3.} Differentiating: $2x\dfrac{dx}{dt} + 2y\dfrac{dy}{dt} = 0$, so
$\dfrac{dy}{dt} = -\dfrac{x}{y}\cdot\dfrac{dx}{dt}
= -\dfrac{5}{12}(4)$.

\textbf{Step 4.} $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = \ans{-\frac{5}{3} \text{ ft/s}}$.

\textit{Reading the sign:} negative means $y$ is decreasing --- the top is
sliding \emph{down} at $\frac{5}{3}$ feet per second. A positive answer here is
a sign error, not a different convention.}

\problem{\textbf{Open box (optimization with a domain check).}

\textbf{(a) Step 1.} Expand before differentiating:
$V(x) = x(12 - 2x)^2 = 4x^3 - 48x^2 + 144x$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $V'(x) = 12x^2 - 96x + 144 = 12(x - 2)(x - 6) = 0$, so the
roots are $\ans{x = 2 \text{ and } x = 6}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The domain rejects $x = 6$: cutting $6$ inches from each
corner of a $12$-inch sheet removes the whole width, and $V(6) = 0$ is a
degenerate box, not a maximum. Only $x = 2$ lies in $0 < x < 6$.

\textbf{(b) Step 4.} $V(2) = 2(12 - 4)^2 = 2 \cdot 64$.

\textbf{Step 5.} The maximum volume is $\ans{128}$ cubic inches.

\textbf{Step 6.} Justification: $V' = 12(x-2)(x-6)$ is positive on $(0, 2)$ and
negative on $(2, 6)$, so $V$ rises then falls and $x = 2$ is the absolute
maximum on the physical domain.

\textit{Grading note:} an answer of $x = 6$ is the whole point of the problem
--- the algebra is right and the model is not.}

\problem{\textbf{Absolute extrema on a closed interval.}

\textbf{(a) Step 1.} $f'(x) = 3x^2 - 6x - 9 = 3(x^2 - 2x - 3) = 3(x - 3)(x + 1)$.

\textbf{Step 2.} The critical numbers are $\ans{x = -1 \text{ and } x = 3}$.
Only $x = 3$ lies in $[0, 4]$; $x = -1$ is outside and plays no part.

\textbf{(b) Step 3.} On a closed interval the candidates are the interior
critical numbers and both endpoints --- the Extreme Value Theorem guarantees
the extrema exist and that this short list contains them.

\textbf{Step 4.} $f(0) = 5$; \quad $f(3) = 27 - 27 - 27 + 5 = -22$; \quad
$f(4) = 64 - 48 - 36 + 5 = -15$.

\textbf{Step 5.} The absolute maximum is $\ans{5}$, at $x = 0$, and the
absolute minimum is $\ans{-22}$, at $x = 3$.

\textit{Listen for:} a student who stops at the critical number and reports
$-22$ as ``the'' answer. On a closed interval the endpoints are candidates
every time, and here the maximum is at one of them.}

\problem{\textbf{Filling cone (related rates).}

\textbf{Step 1.} The volume formula has two changing variables, $r$ and $h$.
The similar-triangles relation $r = h/2$ removes one \emph{before}
differentiating, which is what makes the problem solvable.

\textbf{Step 2.} $V = \dfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h
= \dfrac{1}{3}\pi \left(\dfrac{h}{2}\right)^2 h = \dfrac{\pi h^3}{12}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Differentiate in $t$: $\dfrac{dV}{dt}
= \dfrac{\pi h^2}{4}\cdot\dfrac{dh}{dt}$, so
$\dfrac{dh}{dt} = \dfrac{4}{\pi h^2}\cdot\dfrac{dV}{dt}$.

\textbf{Step 4.} With $\dfrac{dV}{dt} = 9$ and $h = 6$:
$\dfrac{dh}{dt} = \dfrac{4(9)}{\pi(36)} = \ans{\frac{1}{\pi} \text{ ft/min}}$
(about $0.32$ feet per minute).

\textit{Common wrong answer:} about $0.08$ --- the substitution $r = h/2$ was
skipped, so the cross-section was taken four times too large.}

\problem{\textbf{Synthesis: inscribed rectangle.}

\textbf{(a) Step 1.} $A(x) = 2x(9 - x^2) = 18x - 2x^3$, so
$A'(x) = 18 - 6x^2$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $18 - 6x^2 = 0$ gives $x^2 = 3$, so the roots are
$\ans{x = \sqrt{3}}$ and $\ans{x = -\sqrt{3}}$. Only the positive root lies in
$[0, 3]$; the negative root describes the same rectangle reflected and is
outside the stated domain.

\textbf{(b) Step 3.} \textit{(Open response --- flagged for manual review; read
the student's argument.)} A full-credit justification does all of this:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2cm, itemsep=2pt, topsep=3pt]
\item names $[0, 3]$ as a closed, bounded interval and $A$ as continuous on it,
so the Extreme Value Theorem guarantees an absolute maximum exists;
\item compares the only three candidates --- $A(0) = 0$, $A(3) = 0$, and
$A(\sqrt{3}) = 2\sqrt{3}(9 - 3) = 12\sqrt{3} \approx 20.8$ --- or else gives
the sign of $A'$ on each side of $\sqrt{3}$ (positive then negative);
\item explains that a table of sampled values can only report the largest value
\emph{it happened to sample}. Between any two sampled inputs the function is
unconstrained, so a table can suggest a maximum but never establish one; the
derivative argument rules out every unsampled input at once.
\end{itemize}

\textit{Grading note:} accept either the candidate-comparison or the
first-derivative-sign argument, but require the closed-interval or sign-change
reasoning to be stated. ``$A(\sqrt{3})$ is bigger than the values I tried'' is
exactly the answer part (b) is asking the student to improve on.}
