\wstitleblock{Factors, Multiples and Patterns --- Diagnosing a Rule That Was Guessed From Too Few Terms}{Grades 4--5}{}

\noindent\small A rule that fits the first two or three numbers is only a
\textbf{guess}. A guess that is then used everywhere is an
\textbf{overgeneralized rule}.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=0.8cm, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item In each problem, someone states a rule and the evidence they checked.
  \item Test the rule on a term they did \emph{not} check, or find one
        \textbf{counterexample} --- a single case where the rule fails.
  \item Then answer the question with the \emph{repaired} rule. Show your testing.
\end{itemize}
\normalsize

\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem[5cm]{\textbf{Pattern A:} \quad $3$, \ $6$, \ $9$, \ $12$, \ \ldots\par
Kip checks only the first two terms and writes the rule
``\emph{double the term before}'' --- and $3$ doubled really is $6$.\par
Test Kip's rule on the third term. Does doubling $6$ give the third term of
Pattern A? Then use the pattern's real rule to find the $7$th term.}

\problem[5cm]{\textbf{Pattern B:} \quad $1$, \ $4$, \ $9$, \ $16$, \ \ldots\par
Dee writes the rule ``\emph{add $3$}'' because $1 + 3 = 4$.\par
Show that Dee's rule fails at the third term. The real pattern is the square
numbers ($1 \times 1$, $2 \times 2$, $3 \times 3$, \ldots). Find the $7$th term.}

\problem[5cm]{\textbf{Find and fix.} Nia claims: ``\emph{Every multiple of $4$ is
also a multiple of $8$}.'' Her evidence is $8$, $16$ and $24$ --- all multiples of
$8$.\par
Ravi answers that $12$ is a counterexample. Divide $12$ by $8$ and write the
\textbf{remainder}. A remainder that is not zero proves Nia's rule is
overgeneralized.}

\problem[5cm]{\textbf{Find and fix.} \textbf{Pattern C:} \quad $5$, \ $10$, \ $15$,
\ $20$, \ \ldots\par
Tam writes: ``\emph{The terms are the multiples of $5$, so every term ends in
$5$}.'' The first half of Tam's rule is right; the ending-in-$5$ part is
overgeneralized from the first term only.\par
Cross out the wrong half of Tam's rule. Then use the correct half to find the
$12$th term.}

\problem[5cm]{\textbf{Pattern D} starts at $4$ and adds $4$ each time. Bea says the
rule is ``\emph{double the term before}'' --- which gives the same first two terms.\par
Work out the $6$th term of Pattern D, and the $6$th term Bea's doubling rule would
give. Then write $<$, $>$ or $=$ between them:\par
\vspace{0.2cm}
\hspace{1cm}Pattern D's $6$th term \ansblank\ Bea's $6$th term}

\problem[5cm]{\textbf{Find and fix.} Ollie claims: ``\emph{Every odd number is
prime}.'' His evidence is $3$, $5$ and $7$.\par
Find the smallest odd number greater than $7$ that is \emph{not} prime, and prove
it is not prime by writing it as a product of two smaller whole numbers, both
greater than $1$. Write the product's value on the answer line.}

\problem[6cm]{\textbf{Pattern E:} \quad $1$, \ $2$, \ $4$, \ $7$, \ $11$,
\ \ldots\par
Ines writes the rule ``\emph{double the term before}'' because $1$ doubles to $2$
and $2$ doubles to $4$.\par
Show the exact term where Ines's rule first breaks. The real pattern adds $1$, then
$2$, then $3$, and so on --- one more each step. Find the $8$th term.}

\problem[7cm]{\textbf{Explain.} Rosa claims: ``\emph{Every number that ends in $0$
is a multiple of $20$}.'' Her evidence is $20$, $40$ and $60$.\par
\textbf{(a)} Explain, in your own words, why three examples that work do not prove
Rosa's rule.\par
\textbf{(b)} Give one counterexample to Rosa's rule and show why it is a
counterexample.\par
\textbf{(c)} Write one sentence a classmate could use as a habit for testing any
new pattern rule.\par
\noansline}
