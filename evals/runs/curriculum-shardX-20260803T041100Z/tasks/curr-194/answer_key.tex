\problem{\textbf{Circumference of a badge with radius $5$ cm.}\\
Step 1: the question asks for the distance around the circle, so the tool is
$C = 2\pi r$ --- and the given measurement is already a radius, so nothing needs
halving or doubling.\\
Step 2: substitute: $C = 2\pi(5) = 10\pi$.\\
Step 3: $10\pi = 31.4159\ldots$, so $C \approx \ans{31.42\ \text{cm}}$.\\
\emph{Watch for:} $15.71$. That is $\pi r$ --- the $\pi d$ form with a radius
pushed into the diameter's slot. It is exactly half the right answer, which is
the signature of this swap.}

\problem{\textbf{Area of a tabletop whose diameter is $14$ cm.}\\
Step 1: the question asks how much surface the top covers, so the tool is
$A = \pi r^2$. That formula takes a \emph{radius}, and $14$ cm is a diameter, so
convert before substituting.\\
Step 2: $r = 14 \div 2 = 7$ cm.\\
Step 3: $A = \pi(7)^2 = 49\pi = 153.938\ldots$, so
$A \approx \ans{153.94\ \text{cm}^2}$.\\
\emph{Watch for:} $615.75$. That is $\pi(14)^2$ --- the diameter squared. Because
the radius is squared, forgetting to halve multiplies the answer by four, not by
two.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Rae's circle of radius $6$ cm.}\\
Step 1: name what Rae used. $2\pi r$ is the \textbf{circumference} formula, so her
$37.70$ is a length in cm --- the distance once around the circle. Her own unit
label, cm$^2$, contradicts the formula she wrote, which is the clue.\\
Step 2: the question asked for area, so use $A = \pi r^2$ with the radius she was
already given.\\
Step 3: $A = \pi(6)^2 = 36\pi = 113.097\ldots$, so
$A \approx \ans{113.10\ \text{cm}^2}$.\\
\emph{Full-credit answer says:} ``Rae used the circumference formula, which
measures the distance around, not the surface covered,'' and then shows
$\pi r^2$.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Marco's $9$ m by $4$ m garden bed.}\\
Step 1: name what Marco computed. Length times width is the \textbf{area} of the
rectangle, so his $36$ is a number of square metres of soil --- not a length of
edging.\\
Step 2: edging runs around the outside, so the question wants the perimeter:
$P = 2(9) + 2(4) = 18 + 8$.\\
Step 3: the bed needs $\ans{26\ \text{m}}$ of edging.\\
\emph{Full-credit answer says:} ``Marco found the area instead of the
perimeter.'' Note that his mistake would have cost him $10$ extra metres of
edging, and that a length can never be measured in square metres.}

\problem{\textbf{Area of the L-shaped plot
$(0,0), (10,0), (10,4), (4,4), (4,7), (0,7)$.}\\
Step 1: the landscaper wants the ground covered, so this is area. The plot is not
a single rectangle, so split it into two rectangles rather than reaching for one
formula.\\
Step 2: cut along the line $y = 4$. The bottom piece runs from $x = 0$ to
$x = 10$ and from $y = 0$ to $y = 4$: area $10 \times 4 = 40$. The top piece runs
from $x = 0$ to $x = 4$ and from $y = 4$ to $y = 7$: area
$4 \times 3 = 12$.\\
Step 3: add the pieces: $40 + 12$, giving
$\ans{52\ \text{square units}}$.\\
Step 4: check another way --- the full $10$ by $7$ rectangle is $70$, minus the
missing $6$ by $3$ corner, which is $18$: $70 - 18 = 52$ $\checkmark$.\\
\emph{Watch for:} $34$. That is the sum of the six side lengths, i.e.\ the
perimeter of the plot. A fence and a lawn are different orders (metres versus
square metres).}

\problem{\textbf{Trim around a rug measuring $1.8$ m edge-to-edge through the
centre.}\\
Step 1: classify the given number first. A measurement taken \emph{through the
centre} from edge to edge is a \textbf{diameter}, not a radius.\\
Step 2: trim runs around the rug, so use the circumference. With a diameter in
hand the direct form is $C = \pi d$, no halving and no doubling:
$C = \pi(1.8)$.\\
Step 3: $\pi(1.8) = 5.6548\ldots$, so $C \approx \ans{5.65\ \text{m}}$.\\
\emph{Watch for:} $11.31$. That is $2\pi(1.8)$ --- the $2\pi r$ form fed a
diameter, which doubles the answer.}

\problem{\textbf{Area of a pool whose railing is $44$ m around.}\\
Step 1: the given number is a length around the circle, so it is the
circumference. The area formula needs a radius, so recover $r$ from $C$ before
doing anything else.\\
Step 2: from $C = 2\pi r$, $\; r = \dfrac{44}{2\pi} = 7.0028\ldots$ m.\\
Step 3: $A = \pi r^2 = \pi\left(\dfrac{44}{2\pi}\right)^2 = 154.0619\ldots$,
so $A \approx \ans{154.06\ \text{m}^2}$.\\
Step 4: sanity check --- a radius of about $7$ m gives an area near
$\pi(7)^2 \approx 154$ $\checkmark$.\\
\emph{Watch for:} $6082.12$. That is $\pi(44)^2$: the circumference dropped
straight into the radius slot. An answer forty times too large is the tell.}

\problem{\textbf{Explain: circle B has twice the radius of circle A.}\\
\emph{Open response --- flagged for manual review. Model answer below; accept any
equivalent reasoning.}\\
\textbf{(a)} Write both quantities for a radius of $r$ and then for $2r$.
Circumference: $2\pi r$ becomes $2\pi(2r) = 2 \times (2\pi r)$ --- the radius
appears to the first power, so doubling it doubles the length. Area:
$\pi r^2$ becomes $\pi(2r)^2 = \pi \cdot 4r^2 = 4 \times (\pi r^2)$ --- the
radius is squared, so the scale factor is squared too. Doubling a radius doubles
a length and quadruples a covering.\\
\textbf{(b)} Model habit: ``\emph{First} ask whether the question wants a trip
around the shape (length: use $2\pi r$ or add the sides) or the surface it covers
(covering: use $\pi r^2$ or multiply). \emph{Then} check that my answer's unit
matches --- m for a length, m$^2$ for a covering.''\\
\textbf{(c)} Rae wrote a circumference formula but labelled her answer cm$^2$.
Part two of the habit compares the formula with the unit, and those two disagree,
so the check fires before the answer is ever written down.\\
\emph{What a full-credit answer must contain:} the substitution $r \rightarrow
2r$ in both formulas, the observation that squaring the radius squares the scale
factor, a habit with both a decide-first part and a unit-check part, and an
explicit link back to the mismatch in problem 3.}
