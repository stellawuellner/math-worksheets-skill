\sstitleblock{Matching Division Stories, Drawings, and Equations}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Every division has the same three parts.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=0.9cm, itemsep=1pt, topsep=1pt]
\item the \textbf{total} --- how many there are in all
\item the \textbf{number of groups}
\item the \textbf{size of each group} --- how many are in one group
\end{itemize}
A story, a drawing, and an equation all tell those same three parts. Two of them
are always given; the third is what you find.\\[2pt]
total $\div$ number of groups $=$ size of each group \qquad
total $\div$ size of each group $=$ number of groups
\end{formulabox}

\skillheading{1. From a story to an equation}

\begin{formulabox}
Find the \textbf{total} first --- it is the number you divide. Then ask what the
story gives you next: the number of groups, or how many go in each group. The
part it never tells you is the answer.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} 15 grapes are shared equally among 3 children. How many does
each child get?
\step{The total is 15 and the story names 3 groups, so the missing part is the
size of one group --- that is the sharing shape of division.}
\step{Write total $\div$ groups and share them out: $15 \div 3$ puts 5 grapes
in each pile.}
\[ 15 \div 3 = \ans{5} \]
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} 21 marbles are shared equally among 3 jars. How many marbles
are in each jar?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{7}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{2. From a drawing to an equation}

\begin{formulabox}
A drawing hands you two parts for free: \textbf{count the dots} for the total,
and \textbf{count the groups} (boxes, circles, or rows). Then write
total $\div$ groups. An array works the same way --- rows are the groups.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Write the division equation this picture shows.
\begin{center}
\fbox{$\bullet\ \bullet\ \bullet$}\quad
\fbox{$\bullet\ \bullet\ \bullet$}\quad
\fbox{$\bullet\ \bullet\ \bullet$}\quad
\fbox{$\bullet\ \bullet\ \bullet$}
\end{center}
\step{The picture is already grouped, so read the two given parts off it rather
than counting one dot at a time.}
\step{There are 12 dots in all and 4 boxes, so the equation is
$12 \div 4$, and one box holds 3.}
\[ 12 \div 4 = \ans{3} \]
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} A picture shows 16 dots sorted into 2 equal boxes. Write the
division equation and answer it.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{8}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{3. From an equation back to a story}

\begin{formulabox}
Say the equation out loud: ``\emph{this many} in all, split into \emph{this
many} equal parts.'' The number in front of the $\div$ sign must be the
\textbf{total} in the story. If a story uses that number as ``how many in
each,'' it does not match.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Which story matches $20 \div 5$? \;
\textbf{A.} 20 books put on 5 equal shelves. \;
\textbf{B.} 20 books on each of 5 shelves.
\step{The number written first must be the total, so check which story uses 20
as the whole amount.}
\step{Story A uses 20 as the total and 5 as the number of groups, so A matches;
in B the total would be much larger than 20. Answering A gives 4 books on a
shelf.}
\[ 20 \div 5 = \ans{4} \]
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Write a sharing story for $30 \div 5$, then answer it.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{6}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{4. Fact families: multiply to check}

\begin{formulabox}
Three numbers make four sentences. From $4 \times 6 = 24$ you also get
$6 \times 4 = 24$, $24 \div 4 = 6$ and $24 \div 6 = 4$.\\[2pt]
So a missing number in a division sentence can always be found by turning the
sentence into multiplication and skip-counting.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Find the missing number: $4 \times \square = 24$.
\step{A missing factor is what division finds, so turn the sentence around
instead of guessing numbers.}
\step{The partner sentence is $24 \div 4$; skip-count by fours --- 4, 8, 12, 16,
20, 24 --- which takes 6 steps.}
\[ \square = \ans{6} \]
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Find the missing number: $8 \times \square = 56$.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{7}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} the total is the number you divide, and it is never the
answer to a sharing question --- if your answer is bigger than the total, you
multiplied. And read what the question asks for: in an array, the number of rows
and the number in each row are different answers.
\end{watchoutbox}
