\problem{Test the shortcut: (a) $\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16}$, (b) $\sqrt{9 + 16}$,
(c) compare them.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Each radicand is a perfect square, so evaluate the roots
first and then add: $3 + 4$. \ans{7}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad Here the addition happens \emph{under} the radical first:
$9 + 16 = 25$, and the root of that is a whole number. \ans{5}

\textbf{Solution (c):} \quad Seven is larger than five, so the two sides are not equal ---
the radical did not distribute across the plus sign. \ans{>}

\problem{Priya writes $\sqrt{36} + \sqrt{64} = \sqrt{36 + 64} = 10$. (a) Correct value?
(b) What did she do wrong?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Evaluate each root separately, then add: $6 + 8$. \ans{14}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad Priya added the two numbers underneath
the radical signs, as though the radical distributed across the addition. It does not: the
root of a sum is not the sum of the roots, and part (a) is the proof --- the correct total
is four larger than the value she wrote. A full-credit answer states that rule in some form
and points at the numerical difference.

\problem{Combine into a single radical term: $3\sqrt{2} + 5\sqrt{2}$.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Both terms carry the same radical part, so they are like terms.
Add the counts in front and carry the radical along unchanged. \ans{8\sqrt{2}}

\problem{Combine into a single radical term: $7\sqrt{5} - 2\sqrt{5}$.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Like terms again, so subtract the counts in front and leave the
radical part exactly as it was. \ans{5\sqrt{5}}

\problem{Test the shortcut with decimals: (a) $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{3}$, (b) $\sqrt{5}$,
(c) compare.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Neither root is exact, so round each to two places and add:
$1.41 + 1.73$, which to the nearest hundredth from the unrounded values gives \ans{3.15}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad Squeezing between $2.23^2 = 4.9729$ and $2.24^2 = 5.0176$
traps the radicand. \ans{2.24}

\textbf{Solution (c):} \quad The sum of the two roots is about nine tenths larger, so once
again the shortcut fails --- this time with radicands that are not perfect squares at all.
\ans{>}

\problem{Simplify $\sqrt{8} + \sqrt{18}$ into a single radical term.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Pull out the largest perfect-square factor of each radicand.
\begin{align*}
  \sqrt{8} &= \sqrt{4}\cdot\sqrt{2} = 2\sqrt{2} \\
  \sqrt{18} &= \sqrt{9}\cdot\sqrt{2} = 3\sqrt{2}
\end{align*}
Both terms now carry the same radical part, so the counts in front add. \ans{5\sqrt{2}}

\problem{Marco writes $4\sqrt{3} + 2\sqrt{3} = 6\sqrt{6}$. (a) Correct value?
(b) What did he do wrong?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad The radical parts already match, so add only the counts in
front: four of them plus two of them. \ans{6\sqrt{3}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad Marco added the numbers under the
radicals as well as the counts in front. The radical part is the shared \emph{name} of the
two terms; it is what makes them like terms, and it must come through unchanged. Only the
counts in front may be added. A full-credit answer says explicitly that the radical part
must not change.

\problem{\textbf{Challenge.} Simplify $\sqrt{50} + \sqrt{32} - \sqrt{8}$, and justify why
the terms combine.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Every radicand hides a perfect-square factor.
\begin{align*}
  \sqrt{50} &= \sqrt{25}\cdot\sqrt{2} = 5\sqrt{2} \\
  \sqrt{32} &= \sqrt{16}\cdot\sqrt{2} = 4\sqrt{2} \\
  \sqrt{8} &= \sqrt{4}\cdot\sqrt{2} = 2\sqrt{2}
\end{align*}
Now the counts in front combine: five plus four minus two. \ans{7\sqrt{2}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The three radicands looked different but
each is a perfect square times the same small number, so after simplifying all three terms
carry the identical radical part and become like terms. Only then may the counts be added
and subtracted. An answer that jumps straight to combining, or that adds the radicands,
does not earn credit.
