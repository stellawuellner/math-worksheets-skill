\problem{Three attempted definitions of the midpoint of $\overline{AB}$. Which is precise, and what do the others leave out?}

\textbf{Attempt B is the precise definition.} It names a testable location (a point \emph{of} the segment) and a testable condition (the two pieces are congruent).

\textbf{Attempt A} leaves out ``on the segment''. Every point of the perpendicular bisector of $\overline{AB}$ is the same distance from $A$ and from $B$, and all but one of them lie off the segment --- so the words are satisfied by infinitely many non-midpoints.

\textbf{Attempt C} uses ``look equal'', which is not a condition anyone can check. Two pieces that look equal in a sketch may differ in length; congruence is the measurable version of the same idea.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$M$ is the midpoint of $\overline{AB}$, $AM = 3x - 4$, $MB = x + 6$. Find $x$.}

\textbf{Solution:} the definition of midpoint says $\overline{AM} \cong \overline{MB}$, which means the two lengths are equal --- that equation is the whole content of the definition.
\begin{align*}
  3x - 4 &= x + 6 \\
  2x - 4 &= 6 \\
  2x &= 10
\end{align*}
So $x = 5$. Check: $AM = 3(5) - 4 = 11$ and $MB = 5 + 6 = 11$. \ans{x = 5}

\problem{Counterexample to ``if two angle measures add to $180^\circ$, the angles form a linear pair''.}

\textbf{Model answer:} draw a $100^\circ$ angle in one corner of the page and an $80^\circ$ angle in the opposite corner. Their measures add to $180^\circ$, so the ``if'' part holds. They are not a linear pair: a linear pair must be \emph{adjacent} (sharing a vertex and a side) and the two non-shared sides must be \emph{opposite rays}. These two angles share nothing at all, so both conditions fail.

\textbf{Grading:} full credit needs (i) two measures that really add to $180^\circ$, and (ii) a reason naming the adjacency or opposite-ray condition. Two right angles drawn apart, or any supplementary pair drawn separately, is equally correct. Saying only ``they are not next to each other'' without naming the definition's condition is partial credit.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\overrightarrow{BD}$ bisects $\angle ABC$; $m\angle ABD = 5x + 2$, $m\angle DBC = 7x - 14$. (a) Find $x$. (b) Find $m\angle ABC$.}

\textbf{(a)} An angle bisector divides the angle into two congruent angles, so the two measures are equal:
\begin{align*}
  5x + 2 &= 7x - 14 \\
  16 &= 2x
\end{align*}
So $x = 8$. \ans{x = 8}

\textbf{(b)} Each half measures $5(8) + 2 = 42$ degrees, and $7(8) - 14 = 42$ agrees. The whole angle is twice one half: $2(42)$. \ans{m\angle ABC = 84^\circ}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{``Perpendicular lines are two lines that intersect.'' (a) Repair the definition. (b) One of the four angles is $(4x+10)^\circ$; find $x$.}

\textbf{(a)} Model answer: any two lines that cross at, say, $30^\circ$ fit the words exactly and are not perpendicular --- intersecting is necessary but not sufficient. The missing condition is the angle: \emph{perpendicular lines are two lines that intersect to form a right angle}, that is, an angle of $90^\circ$. (Grading: the repair must name a right angle or $90^\circ$. ``They make a square corner'' with no angle condition is partial credit.)

\textbf{(b)} By the repaired definition the marked angle is a right angle, so its measure is $90$:
\begin{align*}
  4x + 10 &= 90 \\
  4x &= 80
\end{align*}
So $x = 20$. \ans{x = 20}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Counterexamples. (a) ``Congruent angles are vertical angles.'' (b) ``A point the same distance from $A$ and $B$ is the midpoint of $\overline{AB}$.''}

\textbf{(a) Model answer:} the two base angles of an isosceles triangle are congruent, and they are not vertical angles --- vertical angles are the opposite pair formed by \emph{two intersecting lines}, and a triangle's base angles are not such a pair. Two separately drawn $40^\circ$ angles work just as well.

\textbf{(b) Model answer:} take $\overline{AB}$ of length $10$ and a point $P$ two units directly above its midpoint. Then $PA = PB$, so the ``if'' part holds, but $P$ is not on $\overline{AB}$ at all, so it cannot be the midpoint. Every point of the perpendicular bisector except the midpoint itself is a counterexample.

\textbf{Grading:} each part needs a case that satisfies the hypothesis \emph{and} an explicit reason for failing the conclusion. In (b) the reason must be that the point is off the segment.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\overline{PQ} \cong \overline{RS}$, $PQ = 2x + 1$, $RS = 5x - 14$. (a) Find $x$. (b) Find $PQ$.}

\textbf{(a)} Congruent segments are segments of equal length, so
\begin{align*}
  2x + 1 &= 5x - 14 \\
  15 &= 3x
\end{align*}
So $x = 5$. \ans{x = 5}

\textbf{(b)} Substitute into either expression: $PQ = 2(5) + 1$, and $RS = 5(5) - 14 = 11$ agrees, which is the check that the congruence really holds. \ans{PQ = 11 \, \text{cm}}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{``The midpoint of a segment is a point on the segment that divides it into two segments.'' (a) Counterexample. (b) Rewrite it.}

\textbf{(a) Model answer:} let $\overline{AB}$ have length $12$ and let $P$ sit $3$ units from $A$. Then $P$ is on the segment and it does divide $\overline{AB}$ into two segments, of lengths $3$ and $9$ --- every word of the definition is satisfied. But $3 \neq 9$, so $P$ is not the midpoint. Any interior point other than the midpoint works.

\textbf{(b) Model answer:} ``The midpoint of $\overline{AB}$ is the point $M$ on $\overline{AB}$, between $A$ and $B$, such that $\overline{AM} \cong \overline{MB}$.'' The three conditions are: on the segment; between the endpoints; the two pieces congruent. The counterexample in (a) fails the third.

\textbf{Grading:} the rewrite must carry the congruence condition --- that is the one the counterexample exposed. Full credit also states the on-the-segment and betweenness conditions.
