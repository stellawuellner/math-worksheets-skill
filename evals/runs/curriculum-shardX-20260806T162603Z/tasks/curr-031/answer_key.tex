\problem{The number is $13$. Fill in: $13$ is one ten and \underline{\hspace{1.2cm}} ones.}

\textbf{Solution:} Take the ten out first. Fill the ten-frame: that uses up ten of the thirteen counters. Count what is left over: thirteen counters take away the ten already in the frame leaves three. So $13 - 10$ is the number of loose ones. \ans{3}

\problem{The number is $16$. Fill in: $16$ is one ten and \underline{\hspace{1.2cm}} ones.}

\textbf{Solution:} Same move: the full frame takes ten of the sixteen counters, and $16 - 10$ counters are left outside it. Counting on from ten to sixteen --- eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, sixteen --- takes six steps, which is the same six. \ans{6}

\problem{Kai built one full ten-frame and put $4$ loose counters beside it. What number did Kai build?}

\textbf{Solution:} Going the other way now: the frame is worth ten, so start at ten and count on the loose counters. Ten, then eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen. In notation that is $10 + 4$. \ans{14}

\problem{Mia built one full ten-frame and put $8$ loose counters beside it. What number did Mia build?}

\textbf{Solution:} Start at ten and count on eight: eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, sixteen, seventeen, eighteen. In notation, $10 + 8$. Note the pattern --- the ones counter you added is the digit you can hear in the number word. \ans{18}

\problem{Read the model (one full ten-frame plus loose dots) and write the number it shows.}

\textbf{Solution:} The full frame is one ten; there are five loose dots beside it. Do not recount the frame dot by dot --- that is what the frame is for. Start at ten and count on the five: $10 + 5$. \ans{15}

\problem{Read the model (one full ten-frame plus loose dots) and write the number it shows.}

\textbf{Solution:} The full frame is one ten and nine dots sit outside it. Start at ten and count on nine: $10 + 9$. This is the largest teen number --- one more counter would fill a second frame and make two tens. \ans{19}

\problem{Both $14$ and $12$ are built with one full ten-frame and some loose counters. (a) Write $<$, $>$, or $=$ between them. (b) Explain why the loose counters, and not the frames, decide which is greater.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Each number has exactly one full frame, so the frames match. What is left is four loose counters against two loose counters, and four is more. \ans{14 > 12}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} A correct response says the tens are equal, so only the ones can decide. Full credit for the idea in any wording: both numbers hold one ten, so the frames cancel out and $4$ ones beats $2$ ones; or, shown with the models, both have one full frame, and the loose dots settle it; or ``they both have a ten so I just looked at the $4$ and the $2$''. Saying only that $4$ is more than $2$, without noting that the tens match, earns half credit. Repeating the symbol with no reason earns no credit.

\problem{Nia says: ``$17$ is one ten and seventeen ones.'' (a) Write the correct number of ones. (b) Explain what Nia mixed up.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The ten inside the frame has already been counted, so it must come out of the total before the ones are counted: $17 - 10$. \ans{7}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} A correct response explains the double count. Full credit for the idea that the ten was already counted once inside the frame, so calling all seventeen counters ``ones'' as well counts those same ten a second time; one ten and seven ones is all seventeen. Accept ``she forgot to take the ten out first'', or a drawing of a full frame plus seven loose dots labelled as the whole amount. Saying the answer should be seven ones without saying why earns half credit. Restating the number earns no credit.
