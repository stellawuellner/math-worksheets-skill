\problem{$y = (x-2)^2 - 9$. (a) $x$-intercepts. (b) $y$-intercept.}

\textbf{(a)} The squared bracket is alone once $9$ is added to both sides, so take square roots: $x - 2 = \pm 3$, giving $x = 5$ and $x = -1$.
\[ \boxed{x = -1 \quad \text{and} \quad x = 5} \]
\textbf{(b)} The $y$-intercept is the value at $x = 0$: $(0-2)^2 - 9 = 4 - 9$. \ans{-5}

\problem{$y = -(x+1)^2 + 4$. (a) $x$-intercepts. (b) Opens up or down; maximum or minimum?}

\textbf{(a)} $-(x+1)^2 + 4 = 0$ gives $(x+1)^2 = 4$, so $x + 1 = \pm 2$ and $x = 1$ or $x = -3$.
\[ \boxed{x = -3 \quad \text{and} \quad x = 1} \]
\textbf{(b) --- instructor-judged.} The parabola opens \emph{downward}, so the vertex $(-1, 4)$ is a \emph{maximum}. Full credit requires the reason read off the equation: the number multiplying the squared bracket is $-1$, and a negative multiplier flips the parent graph over. A correct direction with no reason, or a reason taken from a sketch rather than the equation, is partial credit.

\problem{$y = 2(x-1)^2 - 8$. (a) $x$-intercepts. (b) $y$-intercept.}

\textbf{(a)} $2(x-1)^2 = 8$, so $(x-1)^2 = 4$ and $x - 1 = \pm 2$.
\[ \boxed{x = -1 \quad \text{and} \quad x = 3} \]
\textbf{(b)} At $x = 0$: $2(0-1)^2 - 8 = 2 - 8$. \ans{-6}

\problem{$y = x^2 - 6x + 5$. (a) Axis of symmetry. (b) $y$-value of the vertex.}

\textbf{(a)} Standard form with $a = 1$ and $b = -6$, so the axis is $x = -\dfrac{b}{2a} = -\dfrac{-6}{2}$. \ans{x = 3}

\textbf{(b)} The vertex sits on the axis, so substitute $x = 3$: $3^2 - 6(3) + 5 = 9 - 18 + 5$. The vertex is $(3, -4)$. \ans{-4}

\problem{$y = x^2 + 4x - 5$. (a) $x$-intercepts. (b) Axis of symmetry.}

\textbf{(a)} Factor: product $-5$, sum $4$ gives $+5$ and $-1$, so $(x+5)(x-1) = 0$.
\[ \boxed{x = -5 \quad \text{and} \quad x = 1} \]
\textbf{(b)} $a = 1$, $b = 4$, so $x = -\dfrac{4}{2}$. Notice it is exactly halfway between the two intercepts. \ans{x = -2}

\problem{$y = 2x^2 - 8x + 6$. (a) $x$-intercepts. (b) $y$-value of the vertex.}

\textbf{(a)} Take out the common factor $2$ first: $2(x^2 - 4x + 3) = 2(x-1)(x-3) = 0$.
\[ \boxed{x = 1 \quad \text{and} \quad x = 3} \]
\textbf{(b)} The axis is $x = -(-8)/(2 \cdot 2) = 2$, halfway between $1$ and $3$. At $x = 2$: $2(4) - 16 + 6$. \ans{-2}

\problem{$y = x^2$ shifted $3$ left and $2$ down. (a) New equation in vertex form. (b) $y$-intercept.}

\textbf{(a) --- instructor-judged.} The equation must be $y = (x+3)^2 - 2$. Full credit needs both signs right: a shift to the \emph{left} puts a \emph{plus} inside the bracket, and a shift down puts a minus outside. Writing $(x-3)^2 + 2$ reverses both and earns no credit for (a), though (b) may still be marked against the student's own equation.

\textbf{(b)} At $x = 0$: $(0+3)^2 - 2 = 9 - 2$. \ans{7}

\problem{$y = x^2$ reflected in the $x$-axis, then shifted $9$ up. (a) New equation. (b) $x$-intercepts.}

\textbf{(a) --- instructor-judged.} The equation must be $y = -x^2 + 9$. The reflection puts the minus in front of the squared term only; the shift then adds $9$ to the whole thing. $y = -(x^2 + 9)$ is a different graph --- it reflects the shifted parabola instead --- and is not full credit.

\textbf{(b)} $-x^2 + 9 = 0$ gives $x^2 = 9$.
\[ \boxed{x = -3 \quad \text{and} \quad x = 3} \]

\problem{$y = -2(x-1)^2 + 8$. (a) The three transformations from $y = x^2$, in order. (b) $x$-intercepts.}

\textbf{(a) --- instructor-judged.} Three moves, and all three are needed for full credit: shift $1$ unit to the \emph{right} (the bracket is $x - 1$); stretch vertically by a factor of $2$ and reflect in the $x$-axis (the $-2$ in front); then shift $8$ units up. The horizontal shift belongs inside the bracket and is applied before the vertical move. Naming only the shifts, or reading $(x-1)$ as a shift to the left, is not full credit.

\textbf{(b)} $-2(x-1)^2 + 8 = 0$ gives $(x-1)^2 = 4$, so $x - 1 = \pm 2$.
\[ \boxed{x = -1 \quad \text{and} \quad x = 3} \]

\problem{$y = (x-1)^2 - 4$. (a) $x$-intercepts. (b) Sketch on the grid.}

\textbf{(a)} $(x-1)^2 = 4$, so $x - 1 = \pm 2$.
\[ \boxed{x = -1 \quad \text{and} \quad x = 3} \]
\textbf{(b) --- instructor-judged.} The sketch must open upward, with the lowest point plotted at $(1, -4)$ --- read straight off the vertex form --- and cross the horizontal axis at $-1$ and $3$. It must be symmetric about the vertical line through the lowest point. A curve through the two crossings whose lowest point is somewhere else, or a V-shape with a corner, is not full credit.

\problem{$y = -x^2 + 2x + 3$. (a) $x$-intercepts. (b) $y$-intercept. (c) Sketch on the grid.}

\textbf{(a)} Multiply by $-1$: $x^2 - 2x - 3 = 0$, so $(x-3)(x+1) = 0$.
\[ \boxed{x = -1 \quad \text{and} \quad x = 3} \]
\textbf{(b)} At $x = 0$ the value is the constant term. \ans{3}

\textbf{(c) --- instructor-judged.} The sketch must open downward (the coefficient of $x^2$ is negative), cross the horizontal axis at $-1$ and $3$, cross the vertical axis at $3$, and have its highest point plotted at $(1, 4)$ --- the axis of symmetry is midway between the two crossings, and $-(1)^2 + 2(1) + 3 = 4$. An upward-opening curve, or a peak that is not on that middle line, is not full credit.

\problem{\textbf{Synthesis challenge.} $y = x^2 - 8x + 12$. (a) Axis of symmetry. (b) $y$-value of the vertex. (c) $x$-intercepts. (d) Sketch, and explain the symmetry check.}

\textbf{(a)} $a = 1$, $b = -8$, so $x = -\dfrac{-8}{2}$. \ans{x = 4}

\textbf{(b)} At $x = 4$: $16 - 32 + 12$, so the vertex is $(4, -4)$. \ans{-4}

\textbf{(c)} Factor: product $12$, sum $-8$ gives $-2$ and $-6$, so $(x-2)(x-6) = 0$.
\[ \boxed{x = 2 \quad \text{and} \quad x = 6} \]
\textbf{(d) --- instructor-judged.} The sketch must open upward, sit lowest at $(4, -4)$, and cross the horizontal axis at $2$ and $6$. The written check must use symmetry: the two crossings are equally far from the line of symmetry --- $2$ is two units to its left, $6$ is two units to its right --- so their average has to be $4$. A student who sketches correctly but writes no symmetry argument has answered only half of (d).
