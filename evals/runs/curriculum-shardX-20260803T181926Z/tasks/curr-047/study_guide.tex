\sstitleblock{Sorting Shapes by What Makes Them That Shape}

% =================== SKILL 1 ===================
\skillheading{Skill 1 --- Counting Sides and Corners}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{A side} is a straight edge. \textbf{A corner} is the point where two sides meet.\par
\textbf{In every flat shape with straight sides, the number of sides equals the number of corners.}
Triangle: three sides, three corners. Square and rectangle: four and four. Hexagon: six and six. Circle: no straight sides and no corners --- one curved edge all the way round.\par
\textbf{How to count without losing your place:} start at one corner, touch each side once, and stop when you get back to where you started.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad Which has more straight sides, a rectangle or a triangle?
\step{The question asks about sides, so count the straight edges of each shape and then compare the two numbers.}
\step{Rectangle: top, bottom, left, right --- that is four. Triangle: three.}

$\Rightarrow\quad \ans{4 > 3}$, so the rectangle has more.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad Which has more straight sides, a hexagon or a triangle?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{6 > 3}$, the hexagon}
\end{tryitbox}

% =================== SKILL 2 ===================
\skillheading{Skill 2 --- Colour and Size Do Not Matter}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Defining attributes} are the things you can count or check: number of straight sides, number of corners, whether the shape is closed, whether the edges are straight or curved. These decide what the shape IS.\par
\textbf{Non-defining attributes} are colour, size, and which way the shape is turned. A shape keeps its name when you make it bigger, paint it another colour, or stand it on a point.\par
\textbf{Test to use:} if you are not sure, count the sides. If the counts match, the shapes have the same name.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad A tiny triangle and a huge triangle --- are they both triangles?
\step{Size is not something you count on the shape itself, so ignore it and count the sides on each shape instead.}
\step{Tiny one: three sides. Huge one: three sides.}

$\Rightarrow\quad \ans{3 = 3}$, so yes --- both are triangles.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad A red square and a blue square --- count the corners of each. Are they both squares?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{4 = 4}$, yes}
\end{tryitbox}

% =================== SKILL 3 ===================
\skillheading{Skill 3 --- Sorting Shapes into Groups}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Sorting means picking one rule and using only that rule.} Good rules to sort by: ``has four straight sides'', ``has three corners'', ``has no straight sides''.\par
\textbf{Bad rules:} ``is red'', ``is big'', ``looks like a box''. Those change from shape to shape without changing what the shape is.\par
\textbf{After sorting, count each group} --- then you can say which group has more.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad Sort a square, a rectangle, a trapezoid, a triangle and a circle by ``has four straight sides''. Which group is bigger?
\step{Pick the one rule and test each shape against it, then count how many landed in each group.}
\step{Four straight sides: square, rectangle, trapezoid --- that is three. Not four sides: triangle, circle --- that is two.}

$\Rightarrow\quad \ans{3 > 2}$, so the four-sided group is bigger.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad Out of six shapes, two have three corners and the rest do not. Which group is bigger?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{2 < 4}$, the ``not three corners'' group}
\end{tryitbox}

% =================== SKILL 4 ===================
\skillheading{Skill 4 --- Putting Shapes in Order}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{To put shapes in order, turn each shape into a number first} --- how many sides, or how many corners --- and then order the numbers.\par
\textbf{Fewest to most} means smallest number first. \textbf{Most to fewest} means largest number first.\par
\textbf{Remember the circle:} it has zero straight sides, so it comes first in a fewest-to-most list and last in a most-to-fewest list.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad Put a square, a triangle and a hexagon in order from the fewest corners to the most.
\step{Order words like ``fewest'' are about numbers, so count each shape first and then sort the numbers, not the pictures.}
\step{Square: four. Triangle: three. Hexagon: six. Smallest first: three, then four, then six.}

$\Rightarrow\quad \ans{3,\ 4,\ 6}$
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad Put a pentagon, a triangle and a square in order from the fewest corners to the most.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{3,\ 4,\ 5}$}
\end{tryitbox}
