# Factor Structure Puzzles — Grades 4–5

Three PDFs: the student worksheet, a full step-by-step answer key, and a two-page study
guide.

**Worksheet (12 problems).** This is an interleaved review sheet, so the design point is
that your child has to *pick* the tool, not repeat one. Problems 1–4 are a short warm-up
on factor pairs while the idea settles. From problem 5 on, the four kinds rotate and no
two neighbouring problems use the same one:

- **factor pairs** — the missing factor in $7 \times \_ = 91$, the second side of an
  84-square rectangle, the closest-together pair of 54, and two "which factor fits this
  window" puzzles (108 stickers, 96 tiles);
- **divisibility tests** — is 4236 divisible by 6 (both tests needed, not one), and
  which digit completes $4\,?\,7$ so it is divisible by 9;
- **common multiples** — two lamps flashing on 6- and 10-second cycles, two ferris
  wheels on 8- and 20-minute cycles;
- **prime factor structure** — the largest prime factor of 126, and a mystery number
  given only as $2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 5 \times 5$.

Problem 12 is the synthesis: the smallest cookie total that fills whole boxes of 12
*and* whole trays of 18, how many of those fit in 540, and why the prime factors settle
it without trial division.

Two misconception traps are declared and machine-checked, both the same error: reaching
for $m \times n$ instead of the *least* common multiple (60 instead of 30 on the lamps,
160 instead of 40 on the wheels). Both appear in the key's "Common wrong answers" block.

**Answer key.** Every solution lists the factors or multiples actually used — the full
factor list of 108 and of 96, both multiple lists for each common-multiple problem — so
a parent can see the reasoning and not just the number. Units (units, seconds, minutes)
are printed inside the boxed answers and were bound back to the verify data by the
build.

**Study guide (2 pages).** Four sections matching the four tools: finding the partner in
a factor pair, the digit-sum tests for 3/9 (and how 2 and 3 combine into 6), when two
repeating events meet again, and reading a number from its prime factors. Each has a
rule box, a worked example whose first step says why that tool is chosen, and a try-it
with the answer upside down inside the box.

## What is verified, honestly

The sheet asks for 16 separate responses across the 12 problems.

- **13 are machine-checked** with SymPy: every factor, every digit, both least common
  multiples, the mystery number 300, and both numeric parts of problem 12. The printed
  key was bound back to those verified values, so a boxed answer cannot drift from what
  was checked.
- **3 are instructor-judged** and print as `---` in the Quick Answers bank: problem 5(b)
  (is 4236 divisible by 6 — explain with both tests), problem 11(b) (why the prime-factor
  list shows divisibility by 15 without dividing), and problem 12(c) (the prime-factor
  argument for 540). The key states what each full-credit response must contain — for
  5(b), both tests rather than only the digit sum; for 11(b), pointing at the 3 and the 5
  inside the list rather than dividing.

Nine of the twelve problems are fully machine-checked end to end; the three with a
written half are the ones worth reading yourself.
