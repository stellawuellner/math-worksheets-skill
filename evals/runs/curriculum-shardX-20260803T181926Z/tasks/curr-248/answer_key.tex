\begin{tabular}{|l|c|c|c|}
\hline
 & \textbf{8 or more hours} & \textbf{Under 8 hours} & \textbf{Row total} \\
\hline
\textbf{Plays a school sport} & 30 & 20 & 50 \\
\hline
\textbf{Does not play a sport} & 45 & 105 & 150 \\
\hline
\textbf{Column total} & 75 & 125 & 200 \\
\hline
\end{tabular}

\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{\textbf{Read one cell.}
\par\smallskip
Go across the row labelled ``Plays a school sport'' and down the column labelled
``8 or more hours.'' The cell where they meet holds the students who are in both
categories at once.
\ans{30 \text{ students}}

That cell is the one every later problem builds on: it is counted once in the
row total 50 and once in the column total 75, which is why the two relative
frequencies in problems 3 and 6 come out differently.}

\problem{\textbf{Grand total from the four inner cells.}
\par\smallskip
The four inner cells are disjoint --- every surveyed student lands in exactly
one of them --- so adding all four must reproduce the grand total:
$30 + 20 + 45 + 105$.
\ans{200 \text{ students}}

Two other routes give the same number and are worth checking with the student:
the row totals $50 + 150$, and the column totals $75 + 125$.}

\problem{\textbf{Row relative frequency: sport players with 8 or more hours.}
\par\smallskip
The question asks about the sport players only, so the base is that row's total,
$50$ --- not the whole survey.
\[
\frac{30}{50} = 0.60 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 100 \times \frac{30}{50} = 60
\]
So 60 percent of the students who play a school sport get 8 or more hours of
sleep. \ans{60}

\emph{Common wrong turn:} dividing by 200 gives 15, which answers a different
question (what percent of \emph{all} students both play a sport and sleep well).}

\problem{\textbf{Difference between two cells.}
\par\smallskip
Both counts sit in the ``Under 8 hours'' column: $105$ non-players and $20$
players. Subtract the smaller from the larger: $105 - 20$.
\ans{85 \text{ students}}

This is a raw-count comparison, and problem 10 asks why a comparison like this
one can mislead: the two rows do not have the same number of students in them.}

\problem{\textbf{Row relative frequency: non-players with 8 or more hours.}
\par\smallskip
Same move as problem 3, but on the second row, whose total is $150$.
\[
100 \times \frac{45}{150} = 100 \times 0.30 = 30
\]
So 30 percent of the students who do not play a school sport get 8 or more hours
of sleep. \ans{30}

Notice the reversal: the non-players have the \emph{larger} raw count (45 against
30) and the \emph{smaller} percentage.}

\problem{\textbf{Column relative frequency: well-rested students who play a
sport.}
\par\smallskip
This question starts from the sleep variable, so the base is the column total
for ``8 or more hours,'' which is $75$.
\[
100 \times \frac{30}{75} = 100 \times 0.40 = 40
\]
So 40 percent of the students who get 8 or more hours of sleep play a school
sport, and the total divided by is the column total 75. \ans{40}

\emph{Common wrong turn:} using the row total 50 gives 60, which is the answer
to problem 3 --- the same cell, the wrong base.}

\problem{\textbf{Column relative frequency: short-sleep students who play a
sport.}
\par\smallskip
The base is the other column total, $125$.
\[
100 \times \frac{20}{125} = 100 \times 0.16 = 16
\]
So 16 percent of the students who get under 8 hours of sleep play a school
sport, and the total divided by is the column total 125. \ans{16}

Compare with problem 6: among well-rested students 40 percent are athletes,
among short-sleep students only 16 percent are. Reading the table by columns
tells the same story as reading it by rows.}

\problem{\textbf{Gap between the two row relative frequencies.}
\par\smallskip
Problem 3 gave 60 percent and problem 5 gave 30 percent, both measured on their
own row. Subtract: $60 - 30$.
\ans{30}

The units matter: this is 30 \emph{percentage points}, not 30 percent. A gap
this large on a survey of 200 students is strong evidence of an association.}

\problem{\textbf{Median of five class percentages.}
\par\smallskip
Order the five values first: $45, 55, 60, 60, 70$. With five values the median
is the third one.
\ans{60}

The class in the table reported 60 percent (problem 3), which sits exactly at
the median of the five other classes --- so this class is typical, not unusual.}

\problem{\textbf{Is there an association?} \emph{(Open response --- flagged for
manual review.)}
\par\smallskip
\textbf{Model answer.} Yes. Among the students who play a school sport,
60 percent get 8 or more hours of sleep; among the students who do not,
only 30 percent do. Those two row relative frequencies are far apart, so knowing
whether a student plays a sport changes what you would predict about their
sleep. That is what an association means.
\par\smallskip
The raw counts 30 and 45 point the other way and are misleading, because the two
rows are not the same size. There are only 50 sport players but 150 non-players,
so the non-players have more students in \emph{every} cell simply because there
are three times as many of them. Dividing each count by its own row total puts
both groups on the same footing --- out of 100 --- and only then can the two
groups be compared fairly.
\par\smallskip
\emph{Grading note:} full credit needs (i) the answer yes, (ii) both row
percentages quoted, and (iii) an explanation that names the unequal row totals
as the reason raw counts mislead. A student who says only ``60 is bigger than
30'' has compared the percentages correctly but has not explained why the counts
fail; give partial credit and ask the follow-up question aloud.}
