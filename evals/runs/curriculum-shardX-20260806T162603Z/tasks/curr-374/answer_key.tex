\aktitleblock{Radicals Do Not Split Across a Sum}{Algebra 2}{}

\problem{With $9$ and $16$: (a) $\sqrt{9+16}$. (b) $\sqrt{9}+\sqrt{16}$.
(c) What the comparison shows.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $\sqrt{9+16} = \sqrt{25}$. \ans{5}

\textbf{Solution (b):} $\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = 3 + 4$. \ans{7}

\textbf{Solution (c):} \emph{Instructor-judged.} The two results are different,
$5$ against $7$, so $\sqrt{a+b}$ and $\sqrt{a}+\sqrt{b}$ are not the same
expression. One counterexample is enough to kill a proposed identity: taking a
square root term by term across a sum is never valid. Full credit needs the
general conclusion, not just the two numbers.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Simplest form: (a) $\sqrt{50}$. (b) $\sqrt{72}$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Split off the largest perfect-square \emph{factor} ---
this is a product, so the root does come apart:
$\sqrt{50} = \sqrt{25 \cdot 2} = \sqrt{25}\sqrt{2}$. \ans{5\sqrt{2}}

\textbf{Solution (b):} $\sqrt{72} = \sqrt{36 \cdot 2} = \sqrt{36}\sqrt{2}$.
\ans{6\sqrt{2}}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Student claim $\sqrt{x^2+9} = x+3$. (a) Left side at $x=4$.
(b) Right side at $x=4$. (c) The error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $\sqrt{4^2+9} = \sqrt{16+9} = \sqrt{25}$. \ans{5}

\textbf{Solution (b):} $4 + 3$. \ans{7}

\textbf{Solution (c):} \emph{Instructor-judged.} The student applied the product
rule to a sum. $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\sqrt{b}$ holds, but there is no rule that
takes $\sqrt{a+b}$ apart, and the single test at $x=4$ ($5 \ne 7$) settles it.
$x^2+9$ is a sum of squares and does not factor over the real numbers, so
$\sqrt{x^2+9}$ is already in simplest form --- nothing can be brought out.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{With $36$ and $64$: (a) $(36+64)^{1/2}$. (b) $36^{1/2}+64^{1/2}$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $(36+64)^{1/2} = 100^{1/2} = \sqrt{100}$. \ans{10}

\textbf{Solution (b):} $36^{1/2} + 64^{1/2} = 6 + 8$. \ans{14}

The exponent $\tfrac{1}{2}$ obeys the same rules as the radical sign: it
distributes over a product, never over a sum.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Simplest form: (a) $\sqrt{75}$. (b) $\sqrt{4/9}$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $\sqrt{75} = \sqrt{25 \cdot 3} = \sqrt{25}\sqrt{3}$.
\ans{5\sqrt{3}}

\textbf{Solution (b):} A quotient does split:
$\sqrt{\dfrac{4}{9}} = \dfrac{\sqrt{4}}{\sqrt{9}} = \dfrac{2}{3}$.
\ans{\tfrac{2}{3}}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Student claim $\sqrt{4+x^2} = 2+x$. (a) Correct value at $x=3$.
(b) Student's value at $x=3$. (c) The error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $\sqrt{4+3^2} = \sqrt{4+9} = \sqrt{13} = 3.6055\ldots$,
which rounds to \ans{3.61}

\textbf{Solution (b):} $2 + 3$. \ans{5}

\textbf{Solution (c):} \emph{Instructor-judged.} The student took the square root
of each term separately, which the comparison $3.61 \ne 5$ rules out. Since
$4 + x^2$ is a sum of two squares it has no real factorisation, so no factor can
come out of the radical: $\sqrt{4+x^2}$ is already simplest. Any alternative
form a student offers should be tested at a number before it is accepted.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Student solved $\sqrt{x}+\sqrt{9} = 5$ as $\sqrt{x+9}=5$, $x=16$.
(a) Correct solution. (b) The error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Evaluate the known radical first, then isolate:
\begin{align*}
  \sqrt{x} + 3 &= 5 \\
  \sqrt{x} &= 2 \\
  x &= 4 .
\end{align*}
Check: $\sqrt{4} + \sqrt{9} = 2 + 3 = 5$ $\checkmark$ \ans{x = 4}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \emph{Instructor-judged.} The first line replaces a sum of
two radicals with the radical of the sum, which is the same invalid move as
before. Testing the student's answer settles it: $\sqrt{16}+\sqrt{9} = 4+3 = 7$,
not $5$. A full-credit answer names that step and says the two radicals must be
handled separately.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Challenge: $\sqrt{12}+\sqrt{27}$. (a) Exact simplified form.
(b) As a decimal. (c) $\sqrt{12+27}$ as a decimal.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Simplify each radical separately, then collect like
radicals:
\[ \sqrt{12} + \sqrt{27} = 2\sqrt{3} + 3\sqrt{3} . \]
\ans{5\sqrt{3}}

\textbf{Solution (b):} $5\sqrt{3} = 5(1.73205\ldots) = 8.6602\ldots$
\ans{8.66}

\textbf{Solution (c):} $\sqrt{12+27} = \sqrt{39} = 6.2449\ldots$ \ans{6.24}

The two decimals differ, which is the same lesson as problem 1: adding under the
radical and adding the radicals give different numbers.
