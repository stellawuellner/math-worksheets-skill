\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle\int x e^{x}\,dx$.}
LIATE puts the Algebraic factor ahead of the Exponential, so $u = x$ and
$dv = e^{x}\,dx$. That choice is the whole point: differentiating $x$ kills it,
while $e^{x}$ integrates to itself.
\begin{align*}
u = x, \quad du &= dx, \qquad dv = e^{x}\,dx, \quad v = e^{x} \\
\int x e^{x}\,dx &= x e^{x} - \int e^{x}\,dx
\end{align*}
The remaining integral is elementary.
$\ans{x e^{x} - e^{x} + C}$}

\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle\int x \cos x\,dx$.}
Same reasoning: $u = x$ (Algebraic) and $dv = \cos x\,dx$ (Trigonometric).
\begin{align*}
u = x, \quad du &= dx, \qquad dv = \cos x\,dx, \quad v = \sin x \\
\int x \cos x\,dx &= x \sin x - \int \sin x\,dx = x \sin x + \cos x + C
\end{align*}
Check by differentiating: $\sin x + x\cos x - \sin x = x \cos x$.
$\ans{x \sin x + \cos x + C}$}

\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle\int \ln x\,dx$.}
There is only one factor, so write the integrand as $\ln x \cdot 1$. The
Logarithm is first in LIATE, which forces $u = \ln x$ and $dv = dx$ --- and
that is the only workable split, since $\ln x$ has no elementary
antiderivative to serve as $v$.
\begin{align*}
u = \ln x, \quad du &= \frac{1}{x}\,dx, \qquad dv = dx, \quad v = x \\
\int \ln x \,dx &= x\ln x - \int x \cdot \frac{1}{x}\,dx = x \ln x - \int dx
\end{align*}
$\ans{x \ln x - x + C}$}

\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle\int x^{2} e^{x}\,dx$.}
With $u = x^{2}$, one round of parts leaves $\int x e^{x}\,dx$ --- not finished,
but strictly simpler, because the power dropped by one. That is the signal to
apply parts again rather than to change method.
\begin{align*}
\int x^{2} e^{x}\,dx &= x^{2}e^{x} - 2\int x e^{x}\,dx \\
\intertext{and problem 1 already evaluated the inner integral:}
&= x^{2}e^{x} - 2\left(x e^{x} - e^{x}\right)
\end{align*}
$\ans{e^{x}\left(x^{2} - 2x + 2\right) + C}$}

\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle\int_{0}^{1} x e^{2x}\,dx$.}
Parts first, limits last. With $u = x$ and $dv = e^{2x}\,dx$, the chain rule
runs backwards: $v = \tfrac{1}{2}e^{2x}$, and forgetting that $\tfrac{1}{2}$ is
the most common slip here.
\begin{align*}
\int x e^{2x}\,dx &= \frac{x}{2}e^{2x} - \frac{1}{2}\int e^{2x}\,dx
 = \frac{x}{2}e^{2x} - \frac{1}{4}e^{2x} \\
\Big[\;\Big]_{0}^{1} &= \left(\frac{e^{2}}{2} - \frac{e^{2}}{4}\right)
 - \left(0 - \frac{1}{4}\right)
\end{align*}
$\ans{\dfrac{e^{2} + 1}{4}}$}

\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle\int x \ln x\,dx$.}
Why $u = \ln x$ and not $u = x$: taking $u = x$ would need $v = \int \ln x\,dx$,
which is itself a parts problem, so the integral would get harder. Taking
$u = \ln x$ replaces the logarithm by $\tfrac{1}{x}$ and leaves a power to
integrate.
\begin{align*}
u = \ln x, \quad du &= \frac{1}{x}\,dx, \qquad dv = x\,dx, \quad v = \frac{x^{2}}{2} \\
\int x \ln x\,dx &= \frac{x^{2}}{2}\ln x - \int \frac{x^{2}}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{x}\,dx
 = \frac{x^{2}}{2}\ln x - \int \frac{x}{2}\,dx
\end{align*}
$\ans{\dfrac{x^{2}}{2}\ln x - \dfrac{x^{2}}{4} + C}$}

\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle\int x \sec^{2} x\,dx$.}
The strategic move is to notice that $\sec^{2}x$ is the factor whose
antiderivative you already know, so it must be $dv$; $u = x$ then differentiates
away.
\begin{align*}
u = x, \quad du &= dx, \qquad dv = \sec^{2}x\,dx, \quad v = \tan x \\
\int x \sec^{2}x\,dx &= x\tan x - \int \tan x\,dx
\end{align*}
The leftover integral is a standard one: $\int \tan x\,dx = -\ln|\cos x|$, so
subtracting it adds the logarithm back.
$\ans{x \tan x + \ln|\cos x| + C}$}

\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle\int e^{x} \sin x\,dx$.}
Neither factor simplifies under differentiation, so parts cannot terminate ---
it cycles. Call the integral $I$, apply parts twice keeping the same kind of
choice both times, and an equation in $I$ appears.
\begin{align*}
I &= \int e^{x}\sin x\,dx = e^{x}\sin x - \int e^{x}\cos x\,dx \\
\int e^{x}\cos x\,dx &= e^{x}\cos x + \int e^{x}\sin x\,dx = e^{x}\cos x + I \\
I &= e^{x}\sin x - e^{x}\cos x - I
\end{align*}
Solving the equation, $2I = e^{x}(\sin x - \cos x)$.
$\ans{\dfrac{e^{x}\left(\sin x - \cos x\right)}{2} + C}$}

\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi} x^{2} \cos x\,dx$.}
The power is $2$, so expect two rounds of parts, each with $u$ the polynomial.
\begin{align*}
\int x^{2}\cos x\,dx &= x^{2}\sin x - 2\int x \sin x\,dx \\
\int x \sin x\,dx &= -x\cos x + \sin x \\
\Rightarrow\quad \int x^{2}\cos x\,dx &= x^{2}\sin x + 2x\cos x - 2\sin x
\end{align*}
At $x = \pi$: $\sin\pi = 0$ and $\cos\pi = -1$, giving $0 + 2\pi(-1) - 0$. At
$x = 0$ every term is zero, so the lower limit contributes nothing.
$\ans{-2\pi}$
\par\smallskip
The sign is the thing to check: a positive answer means the minus sign in the
second round of parts was dropped.}

\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle\int_{1}^{\infty} \frac{\ln x}{x^{2}}\,dx$.}
Never substitute $\infty$ into an antiderivative --- integrate to a finite $t$,
then take a limit. Parts with $u = \ln x$, $dv = x^{-2}dx$, $v = -\dfrac{1}{x}$:
\begin{align*}
\int_{1}^{t} \frac{\ln x}{x^{2}}\,dx
 &= \left[-\frac{\ln x}{x}\right]_{1}^{t} + \int_{1}^{t} \frac{1}{x^{2}}\,dx
 = -\frac{\ln t}{t} + 0 + \left[-\frac{1}{x}\right]_{1}^{t} \\
 &= -\frac{\ln t}{t} - \frac{1}{t} + 1 \;=\; -\frac{\ln t + 1}{t} + 1
\end{align*}
\textbf{(a)} As $t \to \infty$ the denominator grows linearly while the
numerator grows logarithmically, so the quotient collapses; L'H\^opital's rule
on $\dfrac{\ln t + 1}{t}$ gives $\dfrac{1/t}{1} \to 0$. Polynomial growth beats
logarithmic growth.
$\ans{0}$
\par\smallskip
\textbf{(b)} Only the constant term survives the limit, so the integral
converges.
$\ans{1}$}
