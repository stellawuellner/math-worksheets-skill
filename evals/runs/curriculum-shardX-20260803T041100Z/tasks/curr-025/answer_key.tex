\problem{\textbf{$7 + 2$ --- best way: count on.}\\
\textbf{Step 1.} One part ($2$) is tiny and the other ($7$) is large, so
counting on beats counting everything: hold $7$.\\
\textbf{Step 2.} Say the \emph{next} two numbers: ``$8$, $9$.'' Two counts, so
$7 + 2 = \ans{9}$.\\
\textit{Accept also:} a student who makes ten first ($7 + 2$ is one less than
$7 + 3$) still gets $9$; the count-on is the shortest route.}

\problem{\textbf{$4 + 4$ --- best way: double.}\\
\textbf{Step 1.} Both parts are the same number, so this is a known double ---
no counting needed.\\
\textbf{Step 2.} Double $4$ is $\ans{8}$.}

\problem{\textbf{$6 + 4$ --- best way: make ten.}\\
\textbf{Step 1.} $6$ and $4$ are ten-partners: on a ten-frame, $6$ counters
leave exactly $4$ empty spaces, so the frame fills up.\\
\textbf{Step 2.} A full frame is ten, so $6 + 4 = \ans{10}$.}

\problem{\textbf{$3 + 6$ --- best way: count on from the larger part.}\\
\textbf{Step 1.} Order does not change a sum, so start from the \emph{larger}
part, $6$, and count on the smaller part, $3$. (Starting at $3$ would take six
counts instead of three.)\\
\textbf{Step 2.} Say ``$7$, $8$, $9$'' --- three counts --- so
$3 + 6 = \ans{9}$.}

\problem{\textbf{$4 + 5$ --- best way: near double.}\\
\textbf{Step 1.} The parts are neighbours, so lean on the double you already
know: $4 + 4 = 8$.\\
\textbf{Step 2.} $5$ is one more than $4$, so the sum is one more than $8$:
$4 + 5 = \ans{9}$.}

\problem{\textbf{$7 + \underline{\hspace{0.8cm}} = 10$ --- best way: ten-partners.}\\
\textbf{Step 1.} The whole is $10$ and one part is $7$, so the missing part is
what is left of the ten-frame after $7$ counters.\\
\textbf{Step 2.} Count the empty spaces: ``$8$, $9$, $10$'' --- three of them.
The missing part is $\ans{3}$.}

\problem{\textbf{A number added to itself gives $8$ --- best way: doubles.}\\
\textbf{Step 1.} ``Added to itself'' means both parts are the same, so ask
which double makes $8$.\\
\textbf{Step 2.} Run the doubles: $3 + 3 = 6$, $4 + 4 = 8$. Kim added
$\ans{4}$.}

\problem{\textbf{$\underline{\hspace{0.8cm}} + 2 = 10$ --- best way: ten-partners.}\\
\textbf{Step 1.} The whole is $10$ and the known part is $2$, so the missing
part is the ten-partner of $2$.\\
\textbf{Step 2.} Ten-partners come in pairs, and $2$ pairs with $8$ because
$8 + 2 = 10$. The missing part is $\ans{8}$.}

\problem{\textbf{Sam's error on $2 + 6$.}\\
\textbf{Step 1.} Sam picked the right strategy: hold the larger part, $6$, and
count on the smaller part, $2$.\\
\textbf{Step 2.} His mistake is \emph{where he started counting}. He said the
$6$ itself as his first count, so he only moved one step forward and landed on
$7$. The first thing you say must be the \emph{next} number after the one you
are holding.\\
\textbf{Step 3.} Counting correctly: ``$7$, $8$'' --- two counts, one for each
of the $2$. So $2 + 6 = \ans{8}$.}

\problem{\textbf{Challenge: $2 + 3 + 5$.}\\
\textbf{Step 1.} Three parts may be added in any order, so scan for a pair that
makes a friendly number before adding left to right.\\
\textbf{Step 2.} $2$ and $3$ make $5$, and $5$ is the ten-partner of the
remaining $5$ --- so group them as $(2 + 3) + 5$.\\
\textbf{Step 3.} $(2 + 3) + 5 = 5 + 5$, a double that is also a ten-partner
pair, so the total is $\ans{10}$.\\
\textit{Also correct:} $2 + (3 + 5) = 2 + 8$, counted on, gives the same $10$;
credit any order with correct work.}
