\noindent\small\textbf{How to read this key.} Three different questions are mixed on the
sheet on purpose, so the child has to decide what a problem is asking before answering.
\emph{How many pieces} questions are counted group by group. \emph{Draw and name}
questions are checked by looking at the picture: the pieces must touch along whole sides
with no gaps and no overlaps. \emph{Which uses more} questions are answered by comparing
two counts, never by comparing how big one piece looks.\normalsize

\problem{\textbf{A rectangle covered by square tiles: 2 rows of 4.}\par
Each row is one group of the same size, so count by rows rather than one tile at a time.
There are 2 rows, and each row holds 4 tiles:
\[ 4 + 4 = 8 \qquad \text{or} \qquad 2 \times 4 = 8 \]
The rectangle is covered by $\ans{8}$ square tiles.\par
\textit{Ask the child:} ``How do you know you did not count a tile twice?'' Counting row
by row is the answer.}

\problem{\textbf{A hexagon covered by rhombuses, and each rhombus by triangles.}\par
This is a two-layer cover. Work from the small piece up: each of the 3 rhombuses is made
of 2 green triangles, so there are 3 equal groups of 2.
\[ 2 + 2 + 2 = 6 \qquad \text{or} \qquad 3 \times 2 = 6 \]
The hexagon takes $\ans{6}$ green triangles.\par
This is the fact worth remembering: six green triangles fill one yellow hexagon.}

\problem{\textbf{Two identical squares, one whole side touching.}\par
Slide the two squares together along a full side. The two touching sides disappear inside
the build, and the outside edge is now four straight sides with four square corners ---
but one pair of sides is twice as long as the other.\par
The new shape is a $\ans{\text{rectangle}}$ (twice as long as it is tall).\par
\textit{Check the drawing, not just the word:} the squares must share a whole side. If a
child slides one square only halfway along the other, the outline is not a rectangle, and
that is a useful thing to notice out loud.}

\problem{\textbf{Ben's triangles versus Mia's rhombuses.}\par
Both children covered the same hexagon, so the question is only about \emph{how many
pieces} each one used --- not about how big a piece is.\par
Ben used 6 pieces. Mia used 3 pieces. Six is more than three, so Ben used more pieces:
$\ans{6 > 3}$.\par
\textit{Watch for:} answering ``Mia, because her pieces are bigger.'' Bigger pieces cover
the same hexagon with \emph{fewer} of them --- the opposite of more.}

\problem{\textbf{Four trapezoids, each made of three triangles.}\par
Four equal groups of three. Count the groups, then count by threes:
\[ 3 + 3 + 3 + 3 = 12 \qquad \text{or} \qquad 3 \times 4 = 12 \]
Nina uses $\ans{12}$ green triangles.\par
\textit{Watch for:} the answer 7, which comes from adding the two numbers in the problem
instead of making four groups of three.}

\problem{\textbf{Two identical right triangles, longest sides touching.}\par
The longest side of a right triangle is the side across from the square corner. When the
two longest sides are laid exactly on top of one another, the two square corners land on
opposite ends and the outline closes into four sides with four square corners.\par
The shape is a $\ans{\text{rectangle}}$.\par
For the second drawing, any other correct build counts. The two most common are:
\begin{itemize}[label={$\bullet$}, leftmargin=1.1cm, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item a \emph{larger triangle}, made by joining the two shorter legs of equal length;
  \item a \emph{parallelogram}, made by joining two equal sides but flipping one triangle
        so the corners point the same way.
\end{itemize}
The point of part two is that the same two pieces make more than one shape, so the pieces
alone do not decide the answer --- how they are joined does.}

\problem{\textbf{Eight square tiles or sixteen triangle tiles.}\par
Same rectangle, two ways to cover it. Compare the two counts:\par
8 square tiles against 16 triangle tiles. Eight is less than sixteen, so the square-tile
covering uses fewer pieces: $\ans{8 < 16}$.\par
\textit{Why it comes out that way:} it takes two triangle tiles to cover one square tile,
so the triangle covering always needs twice as many pieces. Smaller pieces always means
more of them.}

\problem{\textbf{Two hexagons, each built from trapezoids, each trapezoid from
triangles.}\par
Three layers, so build up one layer at a time.\par
One hexagon holds 2 red trapezoids, and each trapezoid holds 3 green triangles, so one
hexagon takes $2 \times 3 = 6$ green triangles. Ari builds 2 hexagons the same way, so
double that count:
\[ 6 + 6 = 12 \qquad \text{or} \qquad 2 \times 3 \times 2 = 12 \]
Ari uses $\ans{12}$ green triangles.\par
\textit{Watch for:} the answer 6, which is one hexagon only --- the second hexagon was
forgotten.}

\problem{\textbf{Four green triangles making one larger triangle.}\par
The standard build: put one triangle point-up, put a second and a third beside it also
point-up in a bottom row of three, then turn the fourth triangle point-down and drop it
into the gap between the first two. The result is one big triangle whose side is twice
as long as a small triangle's side.\par
A correct explanation says something like: \emph{``The new shape has three straight sides
and three corners, so it is a triangle. Each big side is two small sides in a row.''}\par
Accept any wording that names three straight sides and three corners. This is an open
build --- $\ans{\text{open response, check the drawing and the reasoning}}$.\par
\textit{Common wrong build:} four triangles all pointing the same way, which leaves gaps
and is not a covering at all.}

\problem{\textbf{Challenge: order the three coverings.}\par
Read the count for each way of covering the one hexagon:
\begin{itemize}[label={$\bullet$}, leftmargin=1.1cm, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item red trapezoids: 2 pieces
  \item blue rhombuses: 3 pieces
  \item green triangles: 6 pieces
\end{itemize}
Now put the counts in order from fewest to most: $\ans{2, 3, 6}$ --- trapezoids, then
rhombuses, then triangles.\par
\textit{The big idea:} the pieces get smaller as you go down that list, and the count gets
bigger. Covering one shape always takes more pieces when the pieces are smaller.}
