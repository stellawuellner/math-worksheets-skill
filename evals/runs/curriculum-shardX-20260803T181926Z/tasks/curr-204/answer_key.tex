\problem{\textbf{Simplify} $\sqrt{72}$.

\textbf{Solution:} Pull out the largest perfect-square factor. $72 = 36 \cdot 2$,
and $\sqrt{36} = 6$:
\[ \sqrt{72} = \sqrt{36 \cdot 2} = \sqrt{36}\,\sqrt{2} \]
\ans{6\sqrt{2}}

\noindent The step that is legal here is splitting a root over a
\emph{product}. That is the rule students later over-apply to sums, which is
what the rest of this sheet is about.}

\problem{\textbf{Simplify} $5\sqrt{3} + 2\sqrt{3} - \sqrt{3}$.

\textbf{Solution:} All three terms carry the same radicand, so they are like
terms and only the coefficients move: $5 + 2 - 1 = 6$. \ans{6\sqrt{3}}

\noindent Treat $\sqrt{3}$ exactly as you would treat $x$: $5x + 2x - x = 6x$.
The radicand never changes when like radicals are combined.}

\problem{\textbf{Simplify} $\sqrt{8} + \sqrt{18}$.

\textbf{Solution:} Neither radicand is a perfect square, but both contain one.
Simplify each term first:
\[ \sqrt{8} = \sqrt{4 \cdot 2} = 2\sqrt{2}, \qquad
   \sqrt{18} = \sqrt{9 \cdot 2} = 3\sqrt{2} \]
Now the radicands match, so the terms combine: $2 + 3 = 5$.
\ans{5\sqrt{2} \approx 7.07}

\noindent\textit{Why not} $\sqrt{26}$? Because $\sqrt{26} \approx 5.10$, and the
sum is about $7.07$. Adding the radicands is not a slower route to the same
answer; it is a different number.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix:} $\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = \sqrt{25}$?

\textbf{The mistake:} the student used a rule that does not exist ---
$\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b} = \sqrt{a + b}$. The real rule splits a root over a
product, $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt{a}\,\sqrt{b}$, and there is no matching rule for
sums.

\textbf{Correct work:} take each root first, then add:
\[ \sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} = 3 + 4 \]
\ans{7 > 5}

\noindent This is the cleanest counterexample in the whole topic, because both
radicands are perfect squares and no decimals are needed: the true value is
$7$, the invented rule gives $5$, and the gap is $2$. A student who believes
the rule can be asked to explain where the missing $2$ went.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix:} $4\sqrt{5} + 3\sqrt{2} = 7\sqrt{7}$?

\textbf{The mistake:} two additions happened at once. The coefficients were
added ($4 + 3 = 7$), which is legal only for like radicals, and the radicands
were added ($5 + 2 = 7$), which is never legal.

\textbf{Correct work:} check whether the radicands match after simplifying.
Both $5$ and $2$ are already square-free and they are different, so the two
terms are unlike and \emph{nothing combines}. The expression is already in
simplest form, and its decimal value is
$4(2.2360\ldots) + 3(1.4142\ldots)$. \ans{13.19}

\noindent\textit{Watch for:} $18.52$, the decimal value of $7\sqrt{7}$. "Cannot
be simplified" is a complete and correct answer here --- students often assume
an expression must reduce to a single term.}

\problem{\textbf{Simplify} $\sqrt{12} + \sqrt{27} - \sqrt{3}$.

\textbf{Solution:} Simplify each radical before deciding anything:
\[ \sqrt{12} = \sqrt{4 \cdot 3} = 2\sqrt{3}, \qquad
   \sqrt{27} = \sqrt{9 \cdot 3} = 3\sqrt{3}, \qquad
   \sqrt{3} = \sqrt{3} \]
All three share the radicand $3$, so the coefficients combine:
$2 + 3 - 1 = 4$. \ans{4\sqrt{3} \approx 6.93}

\noindent\textit{Watch for:} $6$, which is $\sqrt{36}$ --- what you get by
adding and subtracting the radicands $12 + 27 - 3$. The near miss is
instructive: $6$ and $6.93$ are close enough that a student who only estimates
may not notice, which is why the simplified radical form is the answer of
record.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix:} $\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{8} = \sqrt{10}$?

\textbf{The mistake:} the radicands were collected under one root again. Here
the two terms genuinely do combine --- but through simplification, not
addition of radicands.

\textbf{Correct work:} the decimal test first, since it settles the claim in one
line: $1.4142\ldots + 2.8284\ldots$ gives \ans{4.24}

while $\sqrt{10} \approx 3.16$. So
\[ \boxed{\sqrt{2} + \sqrt{8} > \sqrt{10}} \]
For the exact form, $\sqrt{8} = 2\sqrt{2}$, so the sum is
$\sqrt{2} + 2\sqrt{2} = 3\sqrt{2}$, and $3\sqrt{2} = \sqrt{18}$ --- not
$\sqrt{10}$.

\noindent The last line is the one worth showing: the sum of two radicals can
be written as a single radical sometimes, and when it can, the number under the
root is $18$, not the sum $10$ of the two radicands.}

\problem{\textbf{Explain: why roots do not add.}
\textit{(Open response --- flagged for manual review; a model answer follows.)}

\textbf{Model answer:} A square root asks which number, multiplied by itself,
gives the radicand, and that question does not distribute over addition.
Squaring $\sqrt{a} + \sqrt{b}$ gives $a + b + 2\sqrt{ab}$, not $a + b$, so the
two expressions differ by that middle term $2\sqrt{ab}$ --- and it vanishes only
when $a$ or $b$ is zero. That is the whole reason the invented rule fails, and
it also shows the failure is not about "ugly" numbers: $\sqrt{9} + \sqrt{16} =
7$ while $\sqrt{25} = 5$, and every radicand there is a perfect square.

\noindent Two radical terms may be combined only when their radicands are
identical after each has been simplified. Then they behave like like terms:
$2\sqrt{2} + 3\sqrt{2} = 5\sqrt{2}$, exactly as $2x + 3x = 5x$. If the radicands
still differ once simplified, as in $4\sqrt{5} + 3\sqrt{2}$, the expression is
already in simplest form and there is nothing to do.

\noindent\textit{What a reviewer should look for:} identical radicands
\emph{after simplifying} named as the condition; a counterexample whose
arithmetic is actually carried out; and no claim that the rule works for perfect
squares and fails only for irrational roots --- the perfect-square case is the
counterexample.}
