\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 2^{+}} \frac{|x-2|}{x-2}$}
\par
Absolute value has to be unwrapped with a known sign, and the $+$ superscript
supplies it: for $x$ just above $2$, $x - 2 > 0$, so $|x-2| = x-2$.
\par
The quotient is then $\dfrac{x-2}{x-2} = 1$ for every $x > 2$, so the limit is
the constant value of that expression.
\par
$\ans{1}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Grading note:} the left-hand limit is $-1$. A student who answers
``does not exist'' has answered the two-sided question that was not asked.}

\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3x^2+5x-1}{x^2-4}$}
\par
Numerator and denominator have the same degree, so divide both by $x^2$:
\[ \frac{3 + \dfrac{5}{x} - \dfrac{1}{x^2}}{1 - \dfrac{4}{x^2}} \]
Every term with an $x$ in the denominator goes to $0$, leaving the ratio of
the leading coefficients.
\par
$\ans{3}$}

\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 5^{-}} \frac{1}{x-5}$}
\par
The numerator is the nonzero constant $1$ and the denominator goes to $0$, so
the limit is infinite; the only question is the sign.
\par
For $x$ just below $5$ (say $4.99$), $x - 5$ is a small \emph{negative}
number, so $\dfrac{1}{x-5}$ is a large negative number.
\par
$\ans{-\infty}$}

\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to -\infty} \frac{4x^3-x}{2x^3+7}$}
\par
Same degree top and bottom, so the end behavior is the ratio of leading
coefficients --- and it is the same at $-\infty$ as at $+\infty$ because the
odd powers appear in both numerator and denominator and their signs cancel in
the quotient.
\[ \frac{4x^3 - x}{2x^3 + 7} = \frac{4 - \dfrac{1}{x^2}}{2 + \dfrac{7}{x^3}}
   \longrightarrow \frac{4}{2} \]
\par
$\ans{2}$}

\problem{\textbf{Piecewise $f$ at $x = 2$.}
\par
(a) From the left, $x < 2$, so the rule in force is $x^2 + 1$, which is a
polynomial and therefore continuous: substitute.
$2^2 + 1 = 5$.
\par
$\ans{5}$
\par
(b) From the right, $x \ge 2$, so the rule is $5x - 3$: $5(2) - 3 = 7$.
\par
$\ans{7}$
\par
(c) No. A two-sided limit exists only when both one-sided limits exist
\emph{and} agree; here they are $5$ and $7$, so
$\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 2} f(x)$ does not exist and $f$ has a jump
discontinuity at $x = 2$.
\par\smallskip
\emph{Manual review:} part (c) is a written justification, read by a teacher;
parts (a) and (b) are machine-checked.}

\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to -4^{+}} \frac{2x}{x+4}$}
\par
Numerator: $2x \to -8$, a nonzero negative number. Denominator: $x + 4 \to 0$,
and for $x$ just above $-4$ (say $-3.99$) it is a small \emph{positive}
number.
\par
Negative over small positive gives a large negative quotient.
\par
$\ans{-\infty}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Grading note:} the answer flips to $+\infty$ on the left side. Whenever a
sheet asks for one side only, the sign of the denominator on that side is the
whole problem.}

\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{\sqrt{9x^2+4}}{2x+1}$}
\par
Divide numerator and denominator by $x$. Inside a square root, dividing by $x$
means dividing by $x^2$ --- and since $x \to \infty$ is positive,
$\sqrt{x^2} = x$ with no sign flip.
\[ \frac{\sqrt{9x^2+4}}{2x+1}
   = \frac{\sqrt{9 + \dfrac{4}{x^2}}}{2 + \dfrac{1}{x}}
   \longrightarrow \frac{\sqrt{9}}{2} \]
\par
$\ans{\dfrac{3}{2}}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Grading note:} for $x \to -\infty$ the same work gives $-\dfrac{3}{2}$,
because there $\sqrt{x^2} = -x$. Getting $\dfrac{3}{2}$ on both sides means the
sign step was skipped.}

\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 0^{+}}
\left( \frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{x^2} \right)$}
\par
Separately the two terms give $\infty - \infty$, which decides nothing.
Combine them over the common denominator $x^2$ first:
\[ \frac{1}{x} - \frac{1}{x^2} = \frac{x - 1}{x^2} \]
Now read the pieces: the numerator $\to -1$, a nonzero negative number, and
$x^2 \to 0$ through \emph{positive} values (a square is positive on both
sides).
\par
$\ans{-\infty}$}

\problem{\textbf{Choose $k$ so that $g$ is continuous at $x = 3$.}
\par
Continuity at $x = 3$ requires the two one-sided limits to exist and to equal
each other (and the function value).
\par
From the left: $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 3^{-}} (x^2 + k) = 9 + k$.
\par
From the right: $\displaystyle\lim_{x \to 3^{+}} (4x - 1) = 11$, which is also
$g(3)$.
\par
Set them equal: $9 + k = 11$.
\par
$\ans{k = 2}$
\par\smallskip
Check: with $k = 2$ the left rule gives $x^2 + 2 \to 11$, matching the right
rule exactly.}

\problem{\textbf{Synthesis: $\displaystyle h(x) = \frac{2x^2-3x}{x^2-x-6}$.}
\par
Factor first, because factoring decides whether a zero of the denominator is a
hole or an asymptote:
\[ h(x) = \frac{x(2x - 3)}{(x-3)(x+2)} \]
Neither $x - 3$ nor $x + 2$ divides the numerator, so both are genuine
vertical asymptotes --- nothing cancels.
\par
(a) Same degree top and bottom, so the end behavior is the ratio of leading
coefficients, $\dfrac{2}{1}$.
\par
$\ans{2}$
\par
(b) As $x \to 3^{-}$: the numerator $\to 3(3) = 9 > 0$; the factor
$x + 2 \to 5 > 0$; the factor $x - 3$ is a small \emph{negative} number. A
positive over a small negative is a large negative quotient.
\par
$\ans{-\infty}$
\par
(c) As $x \to 3^{+}$ everything is the same except that $x - 3$ is now a small
positive number.
\par
$\ans{+\infty}$
\par
(d) The graph has vertical asymptotes at $x = 3$ and at $x = -2$ (the two
zeros of the denominator, neither of which cancels), and a horizontal
asymptote at $y = 2$, approached at both ends by part (a). Near $x = 3$ the
curve plunges to $-\infty$ on the left and climbs to $+\infty$ on the right.
\par\smallskip
\emph{Manual review:} part (d) is a written description, read by a teacher;
parts (a)--(c) are machine-checked.}
