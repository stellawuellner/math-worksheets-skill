\noindent\small\textbf{What this sheet is teaching, and what to listen for.} One move ---
completing the square --- does all twelve problems, and the sheet varies what surrounds it: two
variables instead of one, a coefficient in front of the squared term, a square completed in $y$
rather than $x$, and finally a right-hand side that turns out negative. When a student's answer
is wrong, ask which number they halved and what they did to the other side. Almost every error
here is a balance error or a sign read backwards off $(x+h)^2$.\normalsize

\problem{\textbf{The completing move on its own.} $x^2 - 10x + \underline{\phantom{00}} =
(x - \underline{\phantom{0}})^2$

\par\smallskip
Half of $-10$ is $-5$, and $(-5)^2 = 25$, so the number to add is \ans{25}.
\par
That makes $x^2 - 10x + 25 = \ans{(x-5)^2}$.
\par
\textbf{The pattern to carry through the sheet.} $x^2 + bx$ needs $\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2$
added, and the result is always $\left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^2$ --- the number inside the
bracket is half of $b$, sign and all, never $b$ itself.}

\problem{\textbf{A circle from general form.} $x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 4y - 12 = 0$

\par\smallskip
Group and move the constant: $(x^2 - 6x) + (y^2 + 4y) = 12$.
\par
Half of $-6$ is $-3$, so add $9$; half of $4$ is $2$, so add $4$. Both must go on the right too:
\[ (x^2 - 6x + 9) + (y^2 + 4y + 4) = 12 + 9 + 4 \]
\[ \ans{(x-3)^2 + (y+2)^2 = 25} \]
\par
Centre $(3, -2)$, and the radius is $\sqrt{25} = \ans{5}$.
\par
\textbf{Read the centre with the signs flipped.} $(y+2)^2$ is $(y - (-2))^2$, so $k = -2$. A
student who reports $(3, 2)$ has read the printed sign rather than the form.
\par
\textbf{If they answered $25$ for the radius:} the number on the right of a circle equation is
$r^2$. Ask them what the radius of $x^2 + y^2 = 9$ is; almost everyone says $3$, and then the
general rule lands.}

\problem{\textbf{Watch the signs.} $x^2 + y^2 + 8x - 2y + 8 = 0$

\par\smallskip
Group and move the constant: $(x^2 + 8x) + (y^2 - 2y) = -8$.
\par
Half of $8$ is $4$, so add $16$; half of $-2$ is $-1$, so add $1$:
\[ (x^2 + 8x + 16) + (y^2 - 2y + 1) = -8 + 16 + 1 \]
\[ \ans{(x+4)^2 + (y-1)^2 = 9} \]
\par
Centre $(-4, 1)$, radius $\sqrt{9} = \ans{3}$.
\par
\textbf{A negative constant is not a problem.} Here the right-hand side starts at $-8$ and the
two added squares carry it up to $9$. Students often assume they have made a mistake when the
right side is briefly negative; problem 12 is the case where it stays negative, which is a
different matter entirely.}

\problem{\textbf{A parabola into vertex form.} $y = x^2 - 8x + 11$

\par\smallskip
Half of $-8$ is $-4$, and $(-4)^2 = 16$. Add and subtract it in the same expression so nothing
changes:
\[ y = (x^2 - 8x + 16) - 16 + 11 \]
\[ \ans{y = (x-4)^2 - 5} \]
\par
Vertex $(4, \ans{-5})$.
\par
\textbf{This balances differently from a circle.} A circle equation has two sides, so the added
$16$ goes on the right as well. Here there is only one expression, so the $16$ has to be
subtracted again immediately. Mixing the two habits is the commonest error on this sheet ---
watch for a student who adds $16$ and never takes it away.}

\problem{\textbf{Vertex form and the $x$-intercepts.} $y = x^2 + 6x + 5$

\par\smallskip
Half of $6$ is $3$, and $3^2 = 9$:
\[ y = (x^2 + 6x + 9) - 9 + 5 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \ans{y = (x+3)^2 - 4} \]
\par
Vertex $(-3, -4)$.
\par
For the intercepts set $y = 0$: $(x+3)^2 = 4$, so $x + 3 = \pm 2$, giving $x = \ans{-1}$ and
$x = \ans{-5}$.
\par
\textbf{Two routes, one answer.} Factoring $x^2 + 6x + 5 = (x+5)(x+1)$ gives the same two
intercepts in one line. Vertex form earns its keep when the quadratic does not factor --- and it
also hands you the vertex, which factoring does not.
\par
\textbf{A check worth doing.} The vertex sits halfway between the intercepts: halfway between
$-5$ and $-1$ is $-3$, which matches $h = -3$. That symmetry is free and catches sign slips.}

\problem{\textbf{A circle with a coefficient in front.} $2x^2 + 2y^2 - 8x + 12y - 24 = 0$

\par\smallskip
Divide everything by $2$ first --- the standard form has no coefficient in front of the squares:
\[ x^2 + y^2 - 4x + 6y - 12 = 0 \]
Then group and complete: half of $-4$ is $-2$ (add $4$), half of $6$ is $3$ (add $9$):
\[ (x^2 - 4x + 4) + (y^2 + 6y + 9) = 12 + 4 + 9 \]
\[ \ans{(x-2)^2 + (y+3)^2 = 25} \]
\par
Centre $(2, -3)$, radius $\sqrt{25} = \ans{5}$.
\par
\textbf{Divide first, always.} Completing the square inside $2x^2 - 8x$ without factoring the
$2$ out leads to a right-hand side that is twice what it should be, and the radius comes out
$\sqrt{2}$ times too big. Dividing the whole equation by $2$ at the start makes the coefficient
disappear for good; there is nothing to remember later.}

\problem{\textbf{A parabola with a coefficient in front.} $y = 2x^2 - 12x + 13$

\par\smallskip
Here the $2$ cannot simply be divided away --- it belongs to the function. Factor it out of the
$x$ terms only:
\[ y = 2(x^2 - 6x) + 13 \]
Half of $-6$ is $-3$, so add $9$ inside the bracket. Because the bracket is multiplied by $2$,
that adds $18$ overall, and $18$ must come back off:
\[ y = 2(x^2 - 6x + 9) - 18 + 13 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \ans{y = 2(x-3)^2 - 5} \]
\par
Vertex $(\ans{3}, -5)$.
\par
\textbf{If they answered $6$ for the $x$-coordinate:} they halved $-12$ instead of $-6$, which
means they completed the square before factoring the $2$ out. Once the bracket reads
$x^2 - 6x$, the number to halve is $-6$.
\par
\textbf{The $-18$ is where marks are lost.} Adding $9$ inside a bracket multiplied by $2$ does
not add $9$ to the function, it adds $18$. Expanding the final answer back out is a five-second
check that catches this every time.}

\problem{\textbf{Inside, on, or outside?} $x^2 + y^2 + 10x - 6y + 18 = 0$, and the point
$(-2, 1)$.

\par\smallskip
Half of $10$ is $5$ (add $25$); half of $-6$ is $-3$ (add $9$):
\[ (x^2 + 10x + 25) + (y^2 - 6y + 9) = -18 + 25 + 9 \]
\[ \ans{(x+5)^2 + (y-3)^2 = 16} \]
Centre $(-5, 3)$, radius $4$.
\par
Distance from the centre to $(-2,1)$:
\[ \sqrt{(-2 - (-5))^2 + (1 - 3)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 4} = \sqrt{13} \]
\par
$\ans{\sqrt{13} \approx 3.61 < 4}$, so the point is nearer the centre than the radius: it lies
\textbf{inside} the circle.
\par
\textbf{Standard form is the whole point of the question.} From the general form there is no way
to see where the centre is, so the distance cannot be computed at all. Rewriting is not a
presentational step here --- it is what makes the question answerable.}

\problem{\textbf{Back the other way.} $(x+1)^2 + (y-4)^2 = 20$

\par\smallskip
Expand both squares and collect:
\[ x^2 + 2x + 1 + y^2 - 8y + 16 = 20 \]
\[ \ans{x^2 + y^2 + 2x - 8y - 3 = 0} \]
\par
\textbf{Why this direction is worth practising.} It is the check on every other problem on the
sheet: expanding a standard form must give back the general form you started from. A student who
runs this check on problems 2, 3 and 6 never has to wonder whether they balanced correctly.}

\problem{\textbf{A parabola on its side.} $x = y^2 - 4y + 7$

\par\smallskip
The square is completed in $y$, because that is the variable being squared. Half of $-4$ is
$-2$, and $(-2)^2 = 4$:
\[ x = (y^2 - 4y + 4) - 4 + 7 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \ans{x = (y-2)^2 + 3} \]
\par
The form says $x$ is smallest when $y = 2$, and then $x = 3$. Written as an ordered pair the
vertex is $\ans{(3,\, 2)}$, and the parabola opens to the right.
\par
\textbf{If they answered $(2, 3)$:} they read the numbers off in the order they appear in the
form, as they would for $y = (x-h)^2 + k$. Here the completed square is in $y$, so the number
inside the bracket is the $y$-coordinate. Have them substitute $y = 2$ and check that $x$ really
does come out as $3$ --- a point that fails substitution is not on the curve.}

\problem{\textbf{Where the line meets the circle.} $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ and $y = x + 1$

\par\smallskip
Substitute the line into the circle:
\[ x^2 + (x+1)^2 = 25 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2x^2 + 2x - 24 = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad
   x^2 + x - 12 = 0 \]
Factor: $(x - 3)(x + 4) = 0$, so $x = 3$ or $x = -4$.
\par
Back into $y = x + 1$: when $x = 3$, $y = 4$; when $x = -4$, $y = -3$.
\par
The intersection points are $\ans{(3,\, 4)}$ and $\ans{(-4,\, -3)}$.
\par
\textbf{Substitute both values back before writing them down.} $3^2 + 4^2 = 25$ and
$(-4)^2 + (-3)^2 = 25$, so both points really are on the circle. Solving only for $x$ and
stopping is the commonest omission here --- a point needs two coordinates.}

\problem{\textbf{A circle that isn't there.} $x^2 + y^2 - 4x + 6y + 20 = 0$

\par\smallskip
\textbf{(a)} Half of $-4$ is $-2$ (add $4$); half of $6$ is $3$ (add $9$):
\[ (x^2 - 4x + 4) + (y^2 + 6y + 9) = -20 + 4 + 9 \]
\[ \ans{(x-2)^2 + (y+3)^2 = -7} \]
\par\smallskip
\textbf{(b)} (Open explanation --- listen to the reason.)
\par
\textbf{A model answer.} ``There is no graph. The left-hand side is a sum of two squares of real
numbers, and a square is never negative, so the smallest value the left side can take is $0$ ---
it can never equal $-7$. That means no point $(x, y)$ in the plane satisfies the equation, so the
equation has an empty graph. It looks like a circle equation in general form, but completing the
square shows that $r^2$ would have to be negative, and no circle has an imaginary radius.''
\par
\textbf{Marking guide.} Full credit for saying the graph is empty (no real points, no circle)
\emph{and} giving the reason --- a sum of squares cannot be negative, or equivalently $r^2 < 0$
is impossible. Partial credit for noticing that $-7$ is impossible for $r^2$ without saying what
that means for the graph. Not sufficient: ``the radius is $\sqrt{-7}$'' offered as the whole
answer, and definitely not ``it is a circle of radius $-7$''.
\par
\textbf{Why this problem is here.} Every other problem on the sheet ends with a picture, which
trains students to believe that any equation of this shape is a circle. It is not: completing
the square is what decides the question, and the three outcomes are a circle ($r^2 > 0$), a
single point ($r^2 = 0$), and nothing at all ($r^2 < 0$). Asking for the $r^2 = 0$ case is a
good follow-up --- $x^2 + y^2 - 4x + 6y + 13 = 0$ is the same problem with the one point
$(2, -3)$ as its entire graph.}
