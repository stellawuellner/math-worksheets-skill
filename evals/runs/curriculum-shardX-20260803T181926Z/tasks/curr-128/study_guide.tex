\sstitleblock{Adding and Subtracting Mixed Numbers}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{A mixed number is a whole plus a fraction,} so work the two parts in
columns: wholes with wholes, fractions with fractions, then put them back
together.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Same-size pieces rule:} fractions can only be added or subtracted when
the denominators match. The denominator names the size of the piece and never
changes when you add or subtract.\\[3pt]
\textbf{One whole is always the fraction with equal top and bottom} --- three
thirds, four fourths, eight eighths. That is what makes trading possible in
both directions.
\end{formulabox}

\skillheading{1. Adding with the same denominator}

\begin{formulabox}
Add the wholes, add the fractions, and then look at the fraction part. If it is
one whole or more, trade it: carry the whole across and keep what is left.
Finish in simplest form, with the unit from the story.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} A hike is $3\tfrac{2}{5}$ km, then $1\tfrac{4}{5}$ km. How
far in all?
\step{The fifths match, so the parts can be added as they stand --- but two
fifths plus four fifths passes one whole, so expect a trade.}
\step{Wholes: $3 + 1 = 4$. Fifths: $\tfrac{2}{5} + \tfrac{4}{5} = \tfrac{6}{5}
= 1\tfrac{1}{5}$, and $4 + 1\tfrac{1}{5}$ is the total.}

The hike is $\ans{5\,\tfrac{1}{5}}$ km.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} A ribbon of $2\tfrac{3}{8}$ m is joined to one of
$4\tfrac{6}{8}$ m. How long is the joined ribbon?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{7\,\tfrac{1}{8}}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{2. Subtracting when you have to regroup}

\begin{formulabox}
If the fraction you are taking away is bigger than the one you have, borrow one
whole and change it into a fraction with the same denominator, then add it to
the fraction you already had.\\[3pt]
Never turn the subtraction around. Reversing the fraction column answers a
different question and always gives an answer that is too big.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} A jug holds $5\tfrac{1}{4}$ L. Pour out $1\tfrac{3}{4}$ L.
How much is left?
\step{Three quarters cannot come out of one quarter, so borrow a whole before
subtracting anything.}
\step{$5\tfrac{1}{4} = 4\tfrac{5}{4}$. Wholes: $4 - 1 = 3$. Quarters:
$\tfrac{5}{4} - \tfrac{3}{4} = \tfrac{2}{4} = \tfrac{1}{2}$.}

There is $\ans{3\,\tfrac{1}{2}}$ L left.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} A board is $6\tfrac{1}{5}$ ft long. A piece of
$2\tfrac{3}{5}$ ft is cut off. How much board is left?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{3\,\tfrac{3}{5}}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{3. When the denominators are different}

\begin{formulabox}
Rewrite both fractions with a common denominator first --- the smallest number
both denominators divide is easiest --- and only then add or subtract in
columns.\\[3pt]
After rewriting, check the fraction column again: a subtraction may still need
regrouping, and a sum may still need a trade.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} A cook mixes $2\tfrac{1}{2}$ cups of stock with
$1\tfrac{1}{3}$ cups of cream. How much liquid is that?
\step{Halves and thirds are different-sized pieces, so nothing can be added
until both are written over one denominator, and sixths is the smallest that
works.}
\step{$\tfrac{1}{2} = \tfrac{3}{6}$ and $\tfrac{1}{3} = \tfrac{2}{6}$. Wholes:
$2 + 1 = 3$. Sixths: $\tfrac{3}{6} + \tfrac{2}{6} = \tfrac{5}{6}$.}

The mixture is $\ans{3\,\tfrac{5}{6}}$ cups.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} A tank holds $4\tfrac{2}{3}$ gal and $1\tfrac{1}{4}$ gal is
drained. How much is left?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{3\,\tfrac{5}{12}}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} adding or subtracting straight across the denominators
is the most common slip. Estimate with the nearest whole numbers before you
start, and check that your answer lands near the estimate.
\end{watchoutbox}
