Here are the three PDFs for a 10-problem precalculus/statistics set on **interpreting
correlation and least-squares regression**.

**Worksheet — `ws_regression_curr448.pdf` (4 pages).** All ten problems work one small,
honest data set — six students' study time in hours against their test score in points —
printed at the top with the fitted line $\hat{y} = 50 + 6x$ and the three sums the
correlation formula needs. The data was built so that $50 + 6x$ *is* the exact least-squares
line, so nothing the student computes fights the numbers they were given. Units are stated
on every variable and there is no story detail that does not carry information.

The ten items move from reading a value, through prediction and residuals, to the two
interpretations that this topic is really about: what the slope means in the study's own
units, and what $r^2$ means as a share of variation. Problems 7 and 9 are a matched pair on
extrapolation (below the data and above it), problem 8 asks why a correlation of 0.97 does
not establish causation, and the synthesis problem adds a seventh student whose residual is
$-12$ — four times any residual in the original six — and asks what that does to the
correlation and the line.

**Answer key — `ak_regression_curr448.pdf` (3 pages).** Every problem restated and worked,
with the data and the three sums repeated at the top so the key stands alone. It includes a
quick-answer bank, a curriculum block, and a "common wrong answers" line from the declared
trap: computing predicted minus actual and reversing the residual's sign.

**Study guide — `ss_regression_curr448.pdf` (2 pages).** Four sections — reading the data,
predicting and residuals, the meaning of slope and intercept, and correlation with $r^2$ —
each with a rule box, a worked example that opens by naming why that move applies, and a
try-it.

**What was verified, honestly.** The sheet asks for **16 separate responses**. **10 are
machine-checked** with SymPy: the table reading, all three predictions, both residuals, the
slope-times-change computation, the probability, and both $r$ and $r^2$ recomputed from the
printed sums to four decimal places. The other **6 are instructor-judged** and marked `---`
in the quick-answer bank: interpreting the slope (3b), interpreting $r^2$ (6b), judging the
intercept (7b), the causation argument (8b), the extrapolation caution (9b), and the residual
discussion (10b). That is the honest split for this topic — the interpretations *are* the
learning objective, and every one of them is prose no computer algebra system can grade. For
each, the key prints a model answer and states exactly what a response must contain for full
credit, including that restating "$0.9459$" is not an interpretation of $r^2$. The eight
study-guide examples and try-its are machine-checked.

No response is marked `[unchecked]`.
