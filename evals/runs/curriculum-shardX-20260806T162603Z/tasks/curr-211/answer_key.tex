\problem{Balance scale: $3$ bags and $4$ blocks on the left, one bag and $12$ blocks on
the right. Write and solve the equation.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Let $x$ be the number of blocks in one bag. The pans balance, so
$3x + 4 = x + 12$. Take one bag off \emph{both} pans (the balance is kept), leaving
$2x + 4 = 12$; take four blocks off both pans to get $2x = 8$; then halve both pans.
\ans{x = 4}

\problem{Solve $5x = 2x + 9$.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Subtract $2x$ from both sides to leave $3x = 9$, then divide both
sides by three. \ans{x = 3}

\problem{Solve $7x - 5 = 4x + 10$.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Subtract $4x$ from both sides: $3x - 5 = 10$. Add five to both
sides (the inverse of subtracting): $3x = 15$. Divide by three. \ans{x = 5}

\problem{Gym A: $20$ dollars plus $3$ dollars a class. Gym B: $8$ dollars plus $5$ dollars
a class. When is the cost equal?}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Let $n$ be the number of classes. The two totals are equal when
$20 + 3n = 8 + 5n$. Subtract $3n$ from both sides: $20 = 8 + 2n$. Subtract eight:
$12 = 2n$. Halve both sides. Six classes is a sensible answer in the story --- a whole
number of classes. \ans{n = 6}

\problem{Solve $4(x - 2) = 2x + 6$.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Clear the bracket first: $4x - 8 = 2x + 6$. Subtract $2x$:
$2x - 8 = 6$. Add eight: $2x = 14$. Halve. \ans{x = 7}

\problem{$2(x + 3) = 2x + 6$: (a) clear the bracket, (b) how many solutions?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Distributing on the left multiplies both terms inside the
bracket, and the two sides turn out identical. \ans{2x + 6 = 2x + 6}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad Because the two sides are the same
expression, the equation is true whatever number is substituted, so every real number is a
solution --- infinitely many. Full credit requires that identity observation, not just the
words "infinitely many" on their own.

\problem{Solve $3(2x + 1) = 5x - 4$.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Clear the bracket: $6x + 3 = 5x - 4$. Subtract $5x$ from both
sides: $x + 3 = -4$. Subtract three from both sides. A negative solution is perfectly
legitimate here; substituting it back gives $-39$ on both sides. \ans{x = -7}

\problem{Candles: $24$ cm burning $3$ cm an hour, and $16$ cm burning one cm an hour. When
are they equal?}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Let $t$ be the number of hours. Each height is its start minus its
burn rate times the time, so $24 - 3t = 16 - t$. Add $3t$ to both sides:
$24 = 16 + 2t$. Subtract sixteen: $8 = 2t$. Halve. \ans{t = 4\ \text{hours}}

\problem{$5x + 3 = 5x - 2$: (a) collect the variable terms, (b) how many solutions?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Subtracting $5x$ from both sides removes the variable
altogether and leaves the numerical claim $3 = -2$. \ans{3 = -2}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad What is left is false: three is not
negative two. No number was involved in that statement, so no value of the variable can
rescue it, and the equation has \ans{\text{no solution}}. Full credit requires the leftover
false statement to be named, not just the words "no solution".

\problem{\textbf{Challenge.} Elena's work on $7x - 6 = 2x + 9$. (a) Solve correctly.
(b) What went wrong?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Subtract $2x$ from both sides: $5x - 6 = 9$. Now \emph{add}
six to both sides --- adding is the inverse of subtracting --- to get $5x = 15$, then
divide by five. \ans{x = 3}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad Elena's first line is right. Her second
line subtracts six from both sides when the six had to be added: she repeated the operation
she saw instead of applying its inverse. The substitution check makes it plain --- her value
does not make the two sides of the original equation equal, while the correct one gives
fifteen on both sides. Full credit requires naming the inverse-operation rule.
