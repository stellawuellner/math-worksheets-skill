\problem{\textbf{Evaluate $\dbinom{7}{3}$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} $\dbinom{7}{3} = \dfrac{7!}{3!\,4!}$. Cancel the $4!$ against
the tail of $7!$ before multiplying anything out.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\dfrac{7 \cdot 6 \cdot 5}{3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1}
= \dfrac{210}{6} = 35$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Sanity check with the symmetry $\binom{7}{3} = \binom{7}{4}$:
choosing which $3$ to take is the same as choosing which $4$ to leave.
\ans{35}}

\problem{\textbf{Expand $(x + 2)^3$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Row $3$ of Pascal's triangle is $1, 3, 3, 1$, so
$(x+2)^3 = x^3 + 3x^2(2) + 3x(2)^2 + (2)^3$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Each term needs its power of $2$ as well as its coefficient:
$3 \cdot 2 = 6$, $3 \cdot 4 = 12$, $2^3 = 8$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Collecting: $x^3 + 6x^2 + 12x + 8$. Check by substituting
$x = 1$: the expansion gives $27$, and $(1+2)^3 = 27$ $\checkmark$
\ans{x^3 + 6x^2 + 12x + 8}}

\problem{\textbf{Committee of $3$ from $9$ members, $4$ of them seniors.}

\textbf{Step 1 (part a).} Order does not matter on a committee, so the total is
$\dbinom{9}{3} = \dfrac{9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7}{3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1} = 84$.
\ans{84}

\textbf{Step 2 (part b).} An all-senior committee uses $3$ of the $4$ seniors:
$\dbinom{4}{3} = 4$. \ans{4}

\textbf{Step 3 (part c).} All $84$ committees are equally likely, so the
probability is $\dfrac{4}{84} = \dfrac{1}{21}$. \ans{\dfrac{1}{21}}}

\problem{\textbf{Pascal's rule for $\dbinom{9}{3}$, $\dbinom{9}{4}$,
$\dbinom{10}{4}$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} $\dbinom{9}{3} = \dfrac{9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7}{6} = 84$ and
$\dbinom{9}{4} = \dfrac{9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6}{24} = 126$. \ans{84 \quad 126}

\textbf{Step 2.} $\dbinom{10}{4}
= \dfrac{10 \cdot 9 \cdot 8 \cdot 7}{24} = 210$. \ans{210}

\textbf{Step 3.} $84 + 126 = 210$, so
$\dbinom{9}{3} + \dbinom{9}{4} = \dbinom{10}{4}$ --- Pascal's rule. In the
triangle, two neighbouring entries of row $9$ add to the entry sitting between
them in row $10$. The reason is a counting one: a group of $4$ from $10$ people
either contains the tenth person (choose $3$ more from $9$) or does not (choose
all $4$ from $9$).}

\problem{\textbf{Expand $(2x - 1)^4$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Row $4$ is $1, 4, 6, 4, 1$, and here $a = 2x$, $b = -1$, so
the terms are $\binom{4}{k}(2x)^{4-k}(-1)^k$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Term by term: $(2x)^4 = 16x^4$; $4(2x)^3(-1) = -32x^3$;
$6(2x)^2(1) = 24x^2$; $4(2x)(-1) = -8x$; $(-1)^4 = 1$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The signs alternate because $b$ is negative, and the powers of
$2$ shrink as the power of $x$ does:
$16x^4 - 32x^3 + 24x^2 - 8x + 1$. Check at $x = 1$: the expansion gives $1$, and
$(2 - 1)^4 = 1$ $\checkmark$ \ans{16x^4 - 32x^3 + 24x^2 - 8x + 1}}

\problem{\textbf{Exactly $4$ heads in $6$ flips of a fair coin.}

\textbf{Step 1 (part a).} An outcome is fixed by saying \emph{which} flips were
heads, so the number of outcomes with exactly $4$ heads is
$\dbinom{6}{4} = \dfrac{6 \cdot 5}{2} = 15$. \ans{15}

\textbf{Step 2 (part b).} All $2^6 = 64$ sequences are equally likely, so the
probability is $\dfrac{15}{64}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Note where the coefficient came from: $15$ is the entry in row
$6$ of Pascal's triangle, and the whole row sums to $64$. That is why the
probabilities of $0, 1, \ldots, 6$ heads add to $1$. \ans{\dfrac{15}{64}}}

\problem{\textbf{Coefficient of $x^5$ in $(x + 3)^8$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The general term is $\dbinom{8}{k}x^k 3^{\,8-k}$, so the
$x^5$ term is the one with $k = 5$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\dbinom{8}{5} = \dbinom{8}{3}
= \dfrac{8 \cdot 7 \cdot 6}{6} = 56$, and the remaining factor is
$3^{3} = 27$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The coefficient is $56 \cdot 27 = 1512$. Answering $56$ is the
standard slip: the binomial coefficient counts the terms, but the constant
raised to its power belongs to the coefficient too. \ans{1512}}

\problem{\textbf{Exactly $2$ red from $5$ red and $7$ blue, drawing $3$.}

\textbf{Step 1 (part a).} Choose which reds and which blue independently:
$\dbinom{5}{2} = 10$ and $\dbinom{7}{1} = 7$, giving
$10 \cdot 7 = 70$ favourable draws. \ans{10 \quad 7}

\textbf{Step 2 (part b).} Total draws of $3$ from $12$ marbles:
$\dbinom{12}{3} = \dfrac{12 \cdot 11 \cdot 10}{6} = 220$. \ans{220}

\textbf{Step 3 (part c).} $\dfrac{70}{220} = \dfrac{7}{22}$. Note the shape:
because the marbles are drawn without replacement, the coefficients multiply on
top and divide by a single coefficient below --- this is not the
$\binom{n}{k}p^k q^{\,n-k}$ formula, which needs independent trials.
\ans{\dfrac{7}{22}}}

\problem{\textbf{Exactly $3$ made free throws out of $5$, with $p = 0.7$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The throws are independent with a fixed success probability, so
this is the binomial formula
$\dbinom{5}{3}p^3(1-p)^2$ with $p = \frac{7}{10}$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\dbinom{5}{3} = 10$; one particular sequence of three makes
and two misses has probability
$\left(\frac{7}{10}\right)^3\left(\frac{3}{10}\right)^2
= \frac{343}{1000}\cdot\frac{9}{100} = \frac{3087}{100000}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} There are $10$ such sequences, so multiply:
$10 \cdot \frac{3087}{100000} = \frac{3087}{10000} = 0.3087$. The coefficient
counts the arrangements; the powers price one arrangement.
\ans{\dfrac{3087}{10000}}}

\problem{\textbf{Constant term of $\left(2x + \frac{1}{x}\right)^6$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} General term:
$\dbinom{6}{k}(2x)^{6-k}\left(\frac{1}{x}\right)^{k}
= \dbinom{6}{k}2^{\,6-k}x^{6-2k}$.

\textbf{Step 2.} The term is constant when $6 - 2k = 0$, that is $k = 3$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Evaluate at $k = 3$: $\dbinom{6}{3} = 20$ and
$2^{3} = 8$, so the constant term is $20 \cdot 8 = 160$. \ans{160}}

\problem{\textbf{Error analysis: Jo's $\frac{2}{5}$.}

\textbf{Step 1 (part a).} Outcomes with exactly $2$ heads in $5$ flips:
$\dbinom{5}{2} = \dfrac{5 \cdot 4}{2} = 10$. \ans{10}

\textbf{Step 2 (part b).} There are $2^5 = 32$ equally likely sequences, so the
probability is $\dfrac{10}{32} = \dfrac{5}{16}$, about $0.3125$ --- not $0.4$.

\textbf{Step 3 (part c).} Jo compared a count of \emph{flips} with a count of
flips, but a probability compares a count of \emph{outcomes} with a count of
outcomes. The fraction $\frac{2}{5}$ answers ``what share of the flips are
heads'', which is a different question; the binomial coefficient $\binom{5}{2}$
is what converts ``$2$ of the $5$ flips'' into the number of sequences that
achieve it.

\textit{Manual review (part c):} accept any sentence saying that Jo used the
ratio of heads to flips instead of the ratio of favourable outcomes to total
outcomes, or that the arrangement count $\binom{5}{2}$ was omitted.
\ans{\dfrac{5}{16}}}

\problem{\textbf{Synthesis: $8$ components, each defective with probability
$\frac{1}{4}$.}

\textbf{Step 1 (part a).} With $p = \frac14$ and $q = \frac34$, the expansion of
$(q + p)^8$ has terms $\dbinom{8}{k}q^{\,8-k}p^{\,k}$, and $k$ counts the
defective components. Exactly $2$ defective is the $k = 2$ term,
$\dbinom{8}{2}\left(\frac34\right)^{6}\left(\frac14\right)^{2}$, whose binomial
coefficient is $\dbinom{8}{2} = 28$. \ans{28}

\textbf{Step 2 (part b).} $\left(\frac14\right)^2 = \frac{1}{16}$ and
$\left(\frac34\right)^6 = \frac{729}{4096}$, so the term is
$28 \cdot \frac{1}{16} \cdot \frac{729}{4096} = \frac{5103}{16384}$, about
$0.3115$. \ans{\dfrac{5103}{16384}}

\textbf{Step 3 (part c).} Expanding $(q+p)^8$ means choosing $q$ or $p$ from
each of the eight factors, one factor per component. A product with exactly two
$p$'s corresponds to one specific set of two defective components, and
$\binom{8}{2}$ is precisely the number of ways to choose that set --- so the
binomial coefficient is not an algebraic accident, it is the count of which
components failed. Since $q + p = 1$, the whole expansion sums to $1$, which is
why the nine terms form a complete probability distribution.

\textit{Manual review (part c):} accept any explanation linking the choice of
$p$ from $k$ of the $8$ factors to the choice of which $k$ components are
defective.}
