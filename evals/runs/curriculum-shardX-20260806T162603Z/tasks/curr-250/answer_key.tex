\problem{Display A: how many students are plotted?}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Count the plotted points, not the tick marks: they sit at study times of
$10$, $20$, $30$, $35$, $40$, $50$, $55$, $60$, $70$ and $80$ minutes. Ten points means ten
students, and that is the whole evidence base for every prediction made from this display.
\ans{10}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Display A: the actual score of the student who studied $35$ minutes.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Find $35$ on the horizontal axis --- it is the minor gridline halfway
between $30$ and $40$ --- go up to the point, and read across to the vertical axis. \ans{70}

\noindent Note for later: the trend line at $x = 35$ gives $0.6(35) + 48 = 69$, so this student sits
one mark above the line.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Display A: predict the score at $45$ minutes.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Substitute $x = 45$ into $y = 0.6x + 48$:
$0.6 \times 45 = 27$, and $27 + 48 = 75$. Because $45$ lies inside the range of study times that
were actually measured ($10$ to $80$ minutes), this is an interpolation and is the kind of
prediction the display supports. \ans{75}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Display B: predict sleep at $5.5$ hours of television.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Substitute $x = 5.5$ into $y = 9.5 - 0.5x$:
$0.5 \times 5.5 = 2.75$, and $9.5 - 2.75 = 6.75$. Again this is inside the measured range of $0$ to
$7$ hours. \ans{6.75\ \text{hours}}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Display A: (a) what the line gives for $200$ minutes; (b) two reasons not to trust it.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $0.6 \times 200 = 120$, and $120 + 48 = 168$. \ans{168}

\noindent\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} First, $200$ minutes is far outside the range
of study times anybody in the sample actually did, which stops at $80$ minutes; the line has no
evidence behind it out there, and nothing says the pattern continues. Second, the number itself is
impossible --- a quiz marked out of $100$ cannot return $168$, which is the display telling you the
straight-line model has to bend somewhere. Full credit names the out-of-range extrapolation; naming
only the impossible score earns half credit.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Display B: (a) sleep at $6$ hours of television; (b) direction of the association.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Read up from $6$ on the horizontal axis to the point, then across.
\ans{6.5\ \text{hours}}

\noindent\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The association is negative: as television
time increases, sleep time decreases, and the points fall from upper left to lower right. For
prediction that means any student with more television time should be given a \emph{smaller}
predicted sleep time --- a prediction that went up with television time would contradict the
display. Full credit names the direction and says what it implies for a prediction.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Display A: (a) predicted score at $60$ minutes; (b) actual minus predicted.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $0.6 \times 60 = 36$, and $36 + 48 = 84$. \ans{84}

\noindent\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad The student actually scored $85$, so the gap is
$85 - 84 = 1$ mark. A point one mark from the line is a close fit; the size of these gaps across the
whole display is what tells you how much to trust a prediction. \ans{1}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Display B: mean sleep time of the eight students.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Add the eight sleep times and divide by eight:
$9 + 9.5 + 8 + 8.5 + 8 + 6.5 + 6.5 + 6 = 62$, and $62 \div 8 = 7.75$.
\ans{7.75\ \text{hours}}

\noindent A prediction near the middle of the television range should land close to this value; one
that does not is worth a second look.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Display C: (a) lowest spelling score; (b) can shoe size predict it?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad The lowest plotted point sits at shoe size $5$. \ans{60}

\noindent\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} No. The points show no pattern at all: at
shoe size $5$ one student scored $60$ and another $90$, and the same wide spread appears at sizes
$6$, $7$, $8$ and $9$. Knowing a student's shoe size narrows the possible spelling score hardly at
all, so no trend line through these points would carry any predictive weight. Full credit refers to
the absence of a trend in the plotted points.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Display A: study time the model calls for to reach a score of $96$.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad This runs the model backwards: solve $0.6x + 48 = 96$.
Subtract $48$ from both sides to get $0.6x = 48$, then divide both sides by $0.6$:
$x = 48 \div 0.6 = 80$. Eighty minutes is the largest study time anyone in the sample actually did,
so this sits right at the edge of the evidence --- reliable as a rough target, and the last point at
which the model can be used at all without extrapolating. \ans{80\ \text{min}}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Display B: (a) what the line predicts at $20$ hours of television; (b) why it is not a prediction.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $0.5 \times 20 = 10$, and $9.5 - 10 = -0.5$.
\ans{-0.5\ \text{hours}}

\noindent\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} A negative sleep time is impossible, which is
the giveaway. The cause is extrapolation: television times of $20$ hours are nowhere near the
measured range of $0$ to $7$ hours, so the straight line is being read in territory where no data
was collected and where the real relationship must flatten out. Full credit names the extrapolation
beyond the data as the reason the number cannot be trusted.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Display A and the headline ``Studying causes higher quiz scores''.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $0.6 \times 25 = 15$, and $15 + 48 = 63$. \ans{63}

\noindent\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad $70 - 65 = 5$ marks. \ans{5}

\noindent\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} No. The display shows an association --- in
this sample of ten students, longer study times came with higher scores --- and an association
cannot by itself establish a cause. Another explanation fits the same ten points just as well: for
instance, students who already understand the material may find studying easier and so keep at it
longer, or a generally motivated student may both study more and score higher, with motivation
driving both. Ten students is also a small sample from one class. Full credit offers a specific
alternative explanation; naming the association-is-not-causation rule with no alternative earns
half credit.
