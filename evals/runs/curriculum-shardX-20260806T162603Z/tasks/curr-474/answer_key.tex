\problem{Evaluate $\int_0^3 (2x - 4)\,dx$.}

\textbf{Solution.} $\int (2x-4)\,dx = x^2 - 4x$, so the value is
$(9 - 12) - (0 - 0)$.
\[ \boxed{-3} \]
The integrand is negative on $[0,2)$ and positive on $(2,3]$, and the negative
part wins. A student who reports $5$ has added the two regions as areas instead
of letting the signs cancel.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\int_5^2 2x\,dx$; the student answered $21$. (a) Name the error.
(b) Corrected value.}

\textbf{Solution (a) --- instructor-judged.} The limits were read in the wrong
order. The integral runs \emph{from} $5$ \emph{to} $2$, and
$\int_b^a = -\int_a^b$. Credit any wording that names the reversed limits or
states the answer must be the negative of the student's.

\textbf{Solution (b).} $\left[x^2\right]_5^2 = 4 - 25$.
\[ \boxed{-21} \]

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\frac{d}{dx}\int_1^x (3t^2+1)\,dt$; the student answered $3t^2+1$ and
then $4$. (a) Name the error. (b) Corrected value at $x = 2$.}

\textbf{Solution (a) --- instructor-judged.} The variable $t$ is a dummy: it
exists only inside the integral and cannot appear in the answer. The
Fundamental Theorem substitutes the \emph{upper limit} into the integrand,
giving $3x^2 + 1$. The student instead substituted the lower limit, $t = 1$.
Credit any wording that identifies $t$ as a dummy variable.

\textbf{Solution (b).} $3(2)^2 + 1 = 12 + 1$.
\[ \boxed{13} \]

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\int_0^2 x(x^2+1)^3dx$ by $u = x^2+1$; the student answered $2$.
(a) Name the error. (b) Corrected value.}

\textbf{Solution (a) --- instructor-judged.} The limits were never converted.
When $x$ runs from $0$ to $2$, $u = x^2 + 1$ runs from $1$ to $5$; the student
integrated in $u$ but evaluated at the old $x$-limits. Converting the limits, or
converting back to $x$ before evaluating, both fix it.

\textbf{Solution (b).}
\[ \frac{1}{2}\int_1^{5} u^3\,du = \frac{1}{8}\left[u^4\right]_1^{5}
   = \frac{625 - 1}{8}. \]
\[ \boxed{78} \]

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$v(t) = t^2 - 4$ m/s on $[0,3]$; the student gave $-3$ metres as the
total distance. (a) The error. (b) $\int_0^2 (4-t^2)dt$.
(c) $\int_2^3 (t^2-4)dt$. (d) Total distance.}

\textbf{Solution (a) --- instructor-judged.} The signed integral of velocity is
\emph{displacement}, not distance travelled. The velocity changes sign at
$t = 2$, so the trip has to be split there and the two pieces added as positive
amounts. Credit any wording that distinguishes displacement from total distance.

\textbf{Solution (b).} On $[0,2]$ the particle moves backwards, so the distance
is the integral of $-v$: $\left[4t - \tfrac{t^3}{3}\right]_0^2 = 8 - \tfrac{8}{3}$.
\[ \boxed{\frac{16}{3}} \]

\textbf{Solution (c).} On $[2,3]$ it moves forwards:
$\left[\tfrac{t^3}{3} - 4t\right]_2^3 = (9 - 12) - \left(\tfrac{8}{3} - 8\right)$.
\[ \boxed{\frac{7}{3}} \]

\textbf{Solution (d).} Adding the two positive pieces:
\[ \boxed{\frac{23}{3}} \]
About $7.67$ metres --- and note the displacement is still $-3$, which is the
number the student reported.

\problem{$\int_0^{\sqrt{\pi/2}} x\cos(x^2)\,dx$; the student replaced $x\,dx$ by
$du$ and got $1$. (a) Name the error. (b) Corrected value.}

\textbf{Solution (a) --- instructor-judged.} With $u = x^2$ we have
$du = 2x\,dx$, so $x\,dx = \tfrac{1}{2}du$. Replacing $x\,dx$ by $du$ throws
away a factor of one half and doubles the answer. Credit any wording that names
the missing one half.

\textbf{Solution (b).} With the limits converted ($u$ runs from $0$ to
$\tfrac{\pi}{2}$),
\[ \frac{1}{2}\int_0^{\pi/2}\cos u\,du = \frac{1}{2}\left[\sin u\right]_0^{\pi/2}. \]
\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{2}} \]

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Area between $y = x^3$ and the $x$-axis on $[-2,2]$; the student
answered $0$. (a) The error. (b) Corrected area.}

\textbf{Solution (a) --- instructor-judged.} The signed integral cancels the
piece below the axis against the piece above it, which is why it comes out
zero --- but area is never negative. Split at $x = 0$ and add the magnitudes.
Credit any wording that separates the signed integral from geometric area.

\textbf{Solution (b).} By symmetry the two pieces are equal:
\[ \int_{-2}^{2}\left|x^3\right|dx = 2\int_0^2 x^3\,dx
   = 2\left[\frac{x^4}{4}\right]_0^2 = 2(4). \]
\[ \boxed{8} \]
