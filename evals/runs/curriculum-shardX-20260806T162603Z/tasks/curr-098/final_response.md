Three PDFs are ready:

## What the worksheet covers

A six-shape **Shape Set** is drawn at the top of the sheet (square, rectangle, rhombus,
parallelogram, trapezoid, kite — labelled A–F, deliberately *not* named, so the child has to look at
the shapes rather than recall vocabulary). Three attributes are defined up front: square corners,
pairs of parallel sides, and equal sides, with the practical test for each (the corner of an index
card, a ruler).

- **1–2** check one attribute on one shape.
- **3–4** sort the whole set — how many shapes have four equal sides, and how two shapes that are
  both quadrilaterals still differ on parallel sides.
- **5, 8, 9** are the application strand: a tile shop's weekly sales (9 squares, 12 rectangles,
  4 rhombuses, 5 trapezoids). Problem 5 counts every quadrilateral tile; problem 8 counts only the
  tiles with four square corners, which is the same table filtered by an attribute — that pairing is
  the point of the sheet.
- **6** asks for the number of parallel-side pairs that *most* of the shapes share, which is a
  different question from how many shapes share it (that trap is declared and printed in the key).
- **7** has the student circle every shape with four square corners and then explain why a square
  qualifies and a rhombus does not, even though both have four equal sides.
- **10** finishes with the big idea: five of the six shapes have at least one pair of parallel sides,
  and one shape can sit in several attribute groups at once.

Answers that count tiles are written on unit-marked answer lines (`tiles`), and difficulty ramps 1 → 5.

## What is verified, and what is not

- **9 of the 13 checks are machine-verified**, covering **7 of the 10 problems fully**. The attribute
  table for the Shape Set lives in the verification data, so every count the sheet asks for was
  recomputed from the same source that defines the correct sorting.
- **Four responses are instructor-judged** and are marked `---` in the key's Quick Answers list:
  problems 7(a) and 7(b), 9(b) and 10(b). Problem 7 is entirely instructor-judged — it asks the child
  to circle shapes and write reasoning, and neither is machine-readable. The key states exactly what
  each answer must contain, including the common error (circling the rhombus because it has equal
  sides).
- One **common wrong answer** is declared and machine-checked as genuinely wrong: 4 on problem 6,
  from counting how many shapes share the attribute instead of naming the shared number.

## The study guide

Three sections: checking one shape's attributes, sorting a set into overlapping groups, and using
attribute groups in a real count. Each has a rule box, a worked example that opens by naming the
strategy, and a try-it with the answer upside down inside the box. The watch-out box separates the
two attributes children most often merge — equal sides and square corners — and explains why only a
square has both.

The grade level and standards code (3.G.A) appear on the answer key only.
