\problem{\textbf{Segment Addition.}

\textbf{(a)} The \textbf{Segment Addition Postulate}: if $B$ is between $A$ and
$C$, then $AB + BC = AC$. It is a \emph{postulate} --- betweenness on a line is
assumed, not derived, so nothing earlier is available to prove it.

\textbf{(b)} Substitute and collect:
\[ (2x+3) + (3x-1) = 27 \;\Longrightarrow\; 5x + 2 = 27 \;\Longrightarrow\; 5x = 25 \]
\ans{x = 5}

\textit{Check:} $AB = 13$, $BC = 14$, and $13 + 14 = 27$.}

\problem{\textbf{Classifying five statements.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The deciding question for each row is ``could this be proved
from something earlier, and is it reversible?''

\textbf{Step 2.} Row by row:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=0.8cm, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
\item \emph{Through any two points there is exactly one line} ---
\textbf{postulate}; it is a starting assumption about lines and points.
\item \emph{An angle bisector divides an angle into two congruent angles} ---
\textbf{definition}; it states what the term means, and it is reversible.
\item \emph{Vertical angles are congruent} --- \textbf{theorem}; it is proved
from the Linear Pair Postulate and angle subtraction.
\item \emph{If $M$ is the midpoint of $\overline{AB}$, then $AM = MB$} ---
\textbf{definition} (one direction of the definition of midpoint); nothing is
proved, the term is being unpacked.
\item \emph{The angles of a triangle sum to $180^\circ$} --- \textbf{theorem};
its proof uses the Parallel Postulate.
\end{itemize}

\textit{Grading note:} accept ``definition'' or ``postulate'' on the midpoint row
only when the reason given is that no proof is involved. The reason column
carries the learning here, not the label. \emph{Manual review: read the
student's reasons.}}

\problem{\textbf{Angle Addition.}

\textbf{(a)} The \textbf{Angle Addition Postulate}: if $D$ lies in the interior
of $\angle ABC$, then $m\angle ABD + m\angle DBC = m\angle ABC$. Like segment
addition, it is assumed rather than proved.

\textbf{(b)} Substitute and collect:
\[ 4x + (3x+5) = 96 \;\Longrightarrow\; 7x + 5 = 96 \;\Longrightarrow\; 7x = 91 \]
\ans{x = 13}

\textit{Check:} $m\angle ABD = 52^\circ$ and $m\angle DBC = 44^\circ$, and
$52 + 44 = 96$.}

\problem{\textbf{A converse that fails.}

\textbf{(a)} Converse: \emph{if $x^2 = 36$, then $x = 6$.} (Hypothesis and
conclusion swap places; nothing else changes.)

\textbf{(b)} Solve completely --- ``completely'' is the whole point of the
problem:
\[ x^2 - 36 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x-6)(x+6) = 0 \]
\[ \boxed{x = -6 \ \text{ or } \ x = 6} \]

\textbf{(c)} The converse is \textbf{false}. Taking $x = -6$ gives
$x^2 = 36$ (hypothesis true) but $x \neq 6$ (conclusion false), which is exactly
what a counterexample is. The original statement stays true --- a conditional
and its converse are independent claims.}

\problem{\textbf{Testing a biconditional.}

\textbf{(a)} The two conditionals are: \emph{if $2x - 7 = 9$ then $x = 8$}, and
\emph{if $x = 8$ then $2x - 7 = 9$}.

\textbf{(b)} Solve: $2x - 7 = 9 \Rightarrow 2x = 16$.
\ans{x = 8}

The solution set is exactly $\{8\}$.

\textbf{(c)} The forward direction holds because $8$ is the \emph{only}
solution --- if there were a second one, the equation could be satisfied by
something other than $8$ and the forward conditional would fail. The backward
direction holds because substituting $8$ gives $16 - 7 = 9$. Both directions
true means the biconditional is valid. \emph{This is the general test: a
biconditional is valid exactly when the solution sets on the two sides are the
same set.}}

\problem{\textbf{The converse of the Vertical Angles Theorem.}

\textbf{(a)} Converse: \emph{if two angles are congruent, then they are vertical
angles.}

\textbf{(b)} Congruent means equal measure:
\[ 5x - 8 = 3x + 22 \;\Longrightarrow\; 2x = 30 \]
\ans{x = 15}

Then $m\angle 1 = 5(15) - 8 = 67^\circ$ and $m\angle 2 = 3(15) + 22 = 67^\circ$.

\textbf{(c)} These two angles are congruent (both $67^\circ$) but they are
adjacent, not vertical. So the hypothesis of the converse holds while its
conclusion fails --- one counterexample, and the converse is \textbf{false}. The
theorem itself remains true; only the reversed statement is being tested.}

\problem{\textbf{Why one is a postulate and the other a theorem.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Obviousness is not the criterion --- \emph{provability from
what came before} is. A postulate is a place the system starts; a theorem is
somewhere it arrives.

\textbf{Step 2.} ``Through any two points there is exactly one line'' has
nothing earlier to appeal to: it is one of the assumptions that gives the words
\emph{point} and \emph{line} their behaviour.

\textbf{Step 3.} ``Two distinct lines intersect in exactly one point'' can be
argued: suppose two distinct lines met at two different points. Those two points
would then lie on two different lines, contradicting the postulate that two
points determine exactly one line. So the theorem rests on the postulate above,
and its proof is an indirect one.

\emph{Manual review: accept any explanation that (i) says postulates are assumed
and theorems are proved, and (ii) names the two-points-determine-a-line
postulate as what the proof leans on.}}

\problem{\textbf{Perpendicular bisector, both directions.}

\textbf{(a)} The biconditional says $P$ is on the perpendicular bisector exactly
when $PA = PB$, so set the two distances equal:
\[ 4x - 5 = 2x + 7 \;\Longrightarrow\; 2x = 12 \]
\ans{x = 6}

\textbf{(b)} $PA = 4(6) - 5 = 19$ and $PB = 2(6) + 7 = 19$. They agree, as they
must.

\textbf{(c)} The half used is \emph{if $PA = PB$, then $P$ is on the
perpendicular bisector} --- the work started from equal distances and concluded
a position. The other half runs the other way: given that $P$ is on the
bisector, conclude $PA = PB$. Because the statement is a biconditional, both
halves are available, which is what makes it worth stating as one sentence
instead of two.}

\problem{\textbf{Converse of the square statement.}

\textbf{(a)} Converse: \emph{if a quadrilateral has four right angles, then it
is a square.}

\textbf{(b)} \textbf{False.}

\textbf{(c)} Counterexample: the rectangle with vertices $(0,0)$, $(6,0)$,
$(6,3)$, $(0,3)$. All four of its angles are right angles, so the hypothesis
holds. But its sides measure $6$, $3$, $6$, $3$, so it fails the part of the
definition of a square that requires \emph{all four sides congruent}. Any
non-square rectangle works; the labelled side lengths are what make it an
argument rather than a picture.

\textbf{(d)} Because the converse is false, ``a quadrilateral is a square if and
only if it has four right angles'' is \textbf{not} a valid biconditional. Only
the forward direction survives. A correct biconditional needs a condition that
pins down the shape completely, e.g. \emph{a quadrilateral is a square if and
only if it has four right angles and four congruent sides}.

\emph{Manual review: read the student's figure and reason; accept any labelled
non-square rectangle.}}

\problem{\textbf{Definitions reverse; other true statements may not.}

\textbf{(a)} As a biconditional: \emph{a ray is an angle bisector of an angle if
and only if it divides that angle into two congruent angles.} Both directions
are true because a definition is an agreement about the meaning of the term ---
that reversibility is what makes definitions the only statements you may use
``both ways'' without proof.

\textbf{(b)} \textbf{No.} Test each direction separately:

\emph{Forward} --- if two angles are complementary, then they are adjacent:
\textbf{false}. A $30^\circ$ angle drawn on this page and a $60^\circ$ angle
drawn on another page sum to $90^\circ$ and share nothing at all.

\emph{Converse} --- if two angles are adjacent, then they are complementary:
\textbf{false}. Two adjacent angles measuring $100^\circ$ and $40^\circ$ share a
side and a vertex but sum to $140^\circ$.

Both directions fail, so no biconditional can be formed. Complementary is about
\emph{measure} and adjacent is about \emph{position}, and the two ideas are
independent --- which is precisely why the definition of complementary says
nothing about position.

\emph{Manual review: accept any pair of counterexamples that separates measure
from position.}}
