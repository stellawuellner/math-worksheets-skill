\problem{Evaluate $\lim_{x \to 3} \frac{x^2-9}{x-3}$.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Substitution gives $0/0$, so factor:
$\dfrac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3} = x + 3$ for $x \ne 3$, and $3 + 3 = 6$. \ans{6}

\problem{$f(x) = \frac{x-4}{\sqrt{x}-2}$. \ (a) $f(3.9)$. \ (b) $f(4.1)$. \ (c) the limit at
$x = 4$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $\dfrac{3.9 - 4}{\sqrt{3.9} - 2} = \dfrac{-0.1}{-0.0251582}$.
\ans{3.9748}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad $\dfrac{4.1 - 4}{\sqrt{4.1} - 2} = \dfrac{0.1}{0.0248457}$.
\ans{4.0248}

\textbf{Solution (c):} \quad Multiply by the conjugate:
$\dfrac{x-4}{\sqrt{x}-2}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt{x}+2}{\sqrt{x}+2} = \dfrac{(x-4)(\sqrt{x}+2)}{x-4}
= \sqrt{x} + 2$ for $x \ne 4$, so the limit is $2 + 2$. \ans{4}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{$g(x) = x+1$ for $x<2$ and $6-x$ for $x \ge 2$. \ (a) left-hand limit.
\ (b) right-hand limit. \ (c) sketch and decide.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Approaching from the left uses the piece $x + 1$: $2 + 1$.
\ans{3}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad Approaching from the right uses $6 - x$: $6 - 2$. \ans{4}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The sketch shows a line of slope $1$ rising
to an \emph{open} circle at $(2,3)$, then a line of slope $-1$ starting at a \emph{closed}
circle at $(2,4)$ and falling. Since $3 \ne 4$, the one-sided limits disagree and
$\lim_{x\to2} g(x)$ does not exist. Full credit requires the correct endpoint markings and the
"does not exist" conclusion with the two values as its reason.

\problem{Evaluate $\lim_{x \to -1}\frac{x^2+3x+2}{x+1}$.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad $x^2 + 3x + 2 = (x+1)(x+2)$, so the quotient is $x + 2$ for
$x \ne -1$, and $-1 + 2 = 1$. \ans{1}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 3\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n-1}$. \ (a) $S_4$.
\ (b) the whole sum.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $3 + \dfrac{3}{2} + \dfrac{3}{4} + \dfrac{3}{8}
= \dfrac{24 + 12 + 6 + 3}{8}$. \ans{\tfrac{45}{8}}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad $a = 3$, $r = \frac{1}{2}$ and $|r| < 1$, so the partial sums
converge to $\dfrac{3}{1 - 1/2} = \dfrac{3}{1/2}$. Note $S_4 = 5.625$ is already close.
\ans{6}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{$h(x) = \frac{\sin x}{x}$ in radians. \ (a) $h(0.1)$. \ (b) $h(0.5)$. \ (c) the limit
at $0$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $\dfrac{\sin(0.1)}{0.1} = \dfrac{0.0998334}{0.1}$. \ans{0.9983}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad $\dfrac{\sin(0.5)}{0.5} = \dfrac{0.4794255}{0.5}$. \ans{0.9589}

\textbf{Solution (c):} \quad The values climb toward $1$ as $x$ shrinks, and the standard
limit confirms it. \ans{1}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{$p(x) = \frac{x-2}{x^2-4}$. \ (a) the limit at $2$. \ (b) sketch: hole or asymptote?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $\dfrac{x-2}{(x-2)(x+2)} = \dfrac{1}{x+2}$ for $x \ne 2$, so the
limit is $\dfrac{1}{4}$. \ans{\tfrac{1}{4}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The graph is the curve $y = \frac{1}{x+2}$
with an open circle at $\left(2, \frac{1}{4}\right)$: the factor $x-2$ cancels, so the function
is simply undefined at one point rather than blowing up. It is a \emph{hole}, not a vertical
asymptote. Full credit requires the open circle at the right height and the word hole (or an
equivalent statement about the cancelling factor).

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Evaluate $\lim_{x \to 0}\frac{\sqrt{x+9}-3}{x}$.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Multiply by the conjugate:
\[ \frac{\sqrt{x+9}-3}{x}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x+9}+3}{\sqrt{x+9}+3}
 = \frac{(x+9)-9}{x\left(\sqrt{x+9}+3\right)} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x+9}+3} \]
Substituting $x = 0$ gives $\dfrac{1}{3+3}$. \ans{\tfrac{1}{6}}

\problem{$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac1n - \frac{1}{n+1}\right)$. \ (a) the sum.
\ (b) $S_N$ and why the sum is a limit.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad The partial sums telescope to $1 - \frac{1}{N+1}$, which tends to
$1$. \ans{1}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad
$S_N = \left(1-\frac12\right)+\left(\frac12-\frac13\right)+\cdots+\left(\frac1N-\frac1{N+1}\right)$;
every interior term cancels with its neighbour, leaving $S_N = 1 - \frac{1}{N+1}$. The sum of
the series is \emph{defined} as $\lim_{N\to\infty} S_N$, and $\frac{1}{N+1} \to 0$, so that
limit is $1$. No finite addition ever reaches $1$; the limit is what gives the infinite sum its
meaning. Full credit requires the closed form and the statement that the sum is a limit of
partial sums.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{$q(n) = \left(1+\frac1n\right)^n$. \ (a) $q(100)$. \ (b) $q(10000)$. \ (c) name the
limit; why is a table not a proof?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $\left(1.01\right)^{100}$. \ans{2.7048}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad $\left(1.0001\right)^{10000}$. \ans{2.7181}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The values are climbing toward
$e \approx 2.71828$, and indeed $\lim_{n\to\infty}\left(1+\frac1n\right)^n = e$. A table cannot
prove it: a table reports finitely many inputs, while a limit is a statement about every input
beyond some point. Tables suggest limits; algebra or a theorem establishes them. Full credit
requires naming $e$ and giving the finitely-many-values reason.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{$\lim_{x\to2}\frac{x^3-8}{x^2-4}$. \ (a) the limit. \ (b) why substitution fails and
cancelling is legal.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad
$\dfrac{(x-2)\left(x^2+2x+4\right)}{(x-2)(x+2)} = \dfrac{x^2+2x+4}{x+2}$ for $x \ne 2$, and at
$x = 2$ that is $\dfrac{4+4+4}{4} = \dfrac{12}{4}$. \ans{3}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad Substituting gives $\frac{0}{0}$, which is
not a value --- it only says that $x-2$ divides both the top and the bottom. Cancelling it
produces a new function that agrees with the original at every $x \ne 2$. A limit as
$x \to 2$ never evaluates at $2$, only near it, so the two functions have the same limit and
the cancellation is legitimate. Full credit requires both the $0/0$ diagnosis and the
"agree everywhere except at the point" justification.

\problem{\textbf{Synthesis.} $r(x) = \frac{x^2-1}{x-1}$ for $x \ne 1$ and $r(1) = 3$.
\ (a) the limit at $1$. \ (b) sketch and explain the discontinuity.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad For $x \ne 1$, $\dfrac{(x-1)(x+1)}{x-1} = x+1$, so the limit is
$1 + 1$. \ans{2}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The graph is the line $y = x + 1$ with a hole
at $(1,2)$ and a separate solid dot at $(1,3)$. Continuity at $x = 1$ requires the limit and the
function value to be the same number; here the limit is $2$ and the value is $3$, so $r$ is
discontinuous at $1$ even though the limit exists. (The discontinuity is removable: redefining
$r(1) = 2$ would repair it.) Full credit requires the hole and the dot drawn in the right
places and the limit-versus-value comparison stated.
