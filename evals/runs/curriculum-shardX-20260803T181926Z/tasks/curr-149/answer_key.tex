\problem{\textbf{Warm-up: from C$(5,\,4)$ to D$(4,\,5)$ along grid lines.}\\
Step 1: read each pair the same way. C is $5$ across and $4$ up. D is $4$
across and $5$ up. Same two numbers, opposite jobs.\\
Step 2: going from C to D, the across-count drops from $5$ to $4$, which is $1$
step left. The up-count rises from $4$ to $5$, which is $1$ step up.\\
Step 3: $1 + 1$, so the trip takes $\ans{2}$ steps.\\
\emph{Watch for:} the answer $0$, from deciding the two points must be the same
place because they use the same two numbers. The grid settles it --- C and D are
plainly two different dots.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Nia's $(2,\,7)$.}\\
Step 1: name what Nia did. She read the pair as (up, across) instead of (across,
up). Counting $7$ across and $2$ up lands on the point labelled \textbf{B}. The
point she meant, $2$ across and $7$ up, is labelled \textbf{A}.\\
Step 2: count the across move from B$(7,\,2)$ to A$(2,\,7)$: from $7$ to $2$ is
$5$ steps left.\\
Step 3: count the up move: from $2$ to $7$ is $5$ steps up.\\
Step 4: $5 + 5$, so the two points are $\ans{10}$ steps apart along the grid
lines. Swapping the numbers did not move the point a little --- it moved it
right across the grid.\\
\emph{Watch for:} the answer $5$, which counts the sideways move only and stops
before the up-and-down move.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Ravi's pattern pair.}\\
Step 1: build a few pairs so the pattern is visible. Pattern X gives $0,\ 3,\
6,\ 9,\ 12$ and Pattern Y gives $0,\ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 4$, so the pairs are
$(0,0),\ (3,1),\ (6,2),\ (9,3),\ (12,4)$.\\
Step 2: name the rule connecting them. Each X-value is $3$ times its Y-value, so
the up-value is the across-value divided by $3$.\\
Step 3: the pair with across-value $12$ has up-value $12 \div 3$, which is
$\ans{4}$. The pair is $(12,\,4)$.\\
\emph{Full-credit answer says:} ``Ravi gave the first number of the pair when the
second was asked for.'' The across-value $12$ is the one he repeated.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Omar's $3$ plants.}\\
Step 1: name what Omar did. He read the number printed under the marks and used
it as his answer. On a line plot that number is the \emph{height}; it never says
how many.\\
Step 2: find the $3$ on the line and count the marks stacked above it: there are
four.\\
Step 3: $\ans{4}$ plants are exactly $3$ inches tall.\\
\emph{Watch for:} the answer $3$, which is Omar's --- the height copied instead
of the marks counted.}

\problem{\textbf{Which height happened most often?}\\
Step 1: ``most often'' means the tallest stack of marks. Comparing the stacks:
three marks above $2$, five above $2\tfrac{1}{2}$, four above $3$, two above
$3\tfrac{1}{2}$ and one above $4$.\\
Step 2: the tallest stack is the one above $2\tfrac{1}{2}$, with five marks.\\
Step 3: now answer the question that was asked --- which \emph{height}, not how
many plants. The height that happened most often is $\ans{2\tfrac{1}{2}}$
inches.\\
\emph{Watch for:} the answer $5$. That is how many marks are in the tallest
stack, which answers ``how many plants'', a different question.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Kaya's $5$ plants.}\\
Step 1: name what Kaya did. She counted the marked heights along the line ---
$2$, $2\tfrac{1}{2}$, $3$, $3\tfrac{1}{2}$, $4$ --- and there are five of them.
That counts labels, not plants.\\
Step 2: each $\times$ is one plant, so add the stacks:
$3 + 5 + 4 + 2 + 1$.\\
Step 3: altogether $\ans{15}$ plants were measured.\\
\emph{Full-credit answer says:} ``the marks are the plants; the numbers under
the line are the heights.'' A useful check: fifteen plants in a class is
believable, five different heights is not the same fact at all.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Sam's $4$th ordered pair.}\\
Step 1 (part a): count along Pattern A: $1,\ 3,\ 5,\ 7$. Its $4$th term is
$\ans{7}$, and A is named first, so $7$ belongs in the first slot.\\
Step 2 (part b): count along Pattern B: $3,\ 9,\ 15,\ 21$. Its $4$th term is
$\ans{21}$, which belongs in the second slot.\\
Step 3 (part c): the correct $4$th ordered pair is $(7,\,21)$. Sam wrote
$(21,\,7)$ --- both numbers are right, but he put Pattern B's term first, so the
pair describes a point $21$ across and $7$ up instead of $7$ across and $21$
up.\\
\emph{Watch for:} a pair written in the wrong order even when both terms are
correct. Whichever pattern is named first in $(A,\,B)$ supplies the first
number, every time.}

\problem{\textbf{Show what you know: $(6,\,2)$ and $(2,\,6)$.}\\
\emph{Open task --- flagged for manual review. A model answer is below; accept
any equivalent wording.}\\
\textbf{(a)} $(6,\,2)$ is plotted $6$ steps across from the corner and $2$ steps
up. $(2,\,6)$ is plotted $2$ steps across and $6$ steps up. The two dots sit on
opposite sides of the diagonal, well apart.\\
\textbf{(b)} Model sentence: ``The two numbers do different jobs. The first one
counts across and the second counts up, so swapping them sends the point to a
different place --- the same two numbers, used in the other order.''\\
\textbf{(c)} Model memory aids: ``along the hall, then up the stairs''; ``$x$
comes before $y$ in the alphabet, and across comes before up''; ``crawl before
you climb''.\\
\emph{What a full-credit answer must contain:} both points plotted in the right
squares and labelled; a sentence that says what \emph{each} number counts,
rather than only ``they are different''; and a memory aid that names the order,
not just the letters.}
