\problem{Bag of 5 red and 7 blue; Sam gives the probability of red as
$\frac{5}{7}$.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) The bag holds $5 + 7 = 12$ counters and every one is
equally likely, so the probability of red is
$\dfrac{5}{12}$. \ans{\tfrac{5}{12}}

(b) \textbf{Instructor-judged.} The denominator of a probability is the total
number of equally likely outcomes. Sam used 7, the number of blue counters,
which is not a total of anything the draw chooses from. Full credit needs the
words ``total number of counters'', or an equivalent statement that the bottom
of the fraction must count every counter in the bag.

\problem{Homework times of the 10 students who stayed late in the library.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) The ten times total 950 minutes, and
$950 \div 10 = 95$ minutes. The arithmetic is correct. \ans{95}

(b) \textbf{Instructor-judged.} The sample is not random. Only students who
stayed late in the library were asked, and those students are more likely than
average to be doing a lot of homework, so 95 minutes is almost certainly too
high for the school as a whole. Full credit needs the reason the group was
unusual --- not just the word ``biased'' --- and ideally the direction of the
error.

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{4 green and 6 yellow sweets; one green is eaten; Ana keeps
$\frac{4}{10}$.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) Eating a green sweet changes \emph{both} counts: 3 green
are left out of 9 sweets, so the probability is
$\dfrac{3}{9} = \dfrac{1}{3}$. \ans{\tfrac{1}{3}}

(b) \textbf{Instructor-judged.} Ana updated neither number. The green sweet that
was eaten comes off the top (4 becomes 3) and off the bottom (10 becomes 9).
Full credit needs both changes; noticing only that the total fell is half the
answer.

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{Spinner of 12 sections (4 red, 3 blue, 5 green); Ben gives ``not red''
as $\frac{4}{12}$.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) Not red means blue or green, which is $3 + 5 = 8$ of the
12 sections: $\dfrac{8}{12} = \dfrac{2}{3}$. \ans{\tfrac{2}{3}}

(b) \textbf{Instructor-judged.} Ben's denominator of 12 is right and his
numerator is not: he counted the red sections, which are exactly the outcomes
the question excludes. Full credit needs the correct favourable count of 8, or
the equivalent route $1 - \frac{4}{12} = \frac{2}{3}$.

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{40 people surveyed leaving a cinema, 30 go weekly; town of 20000.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) The sample fraction is $\dfrac{30}{40} = 0.75$, so the
survey estimates $0.75 \times 20000 = 15000$ residents. \ans{15000}

(b) \textbf{Instructor-judged.} Everybody in the sample was already at the
cinema, so the sample was selected by the very behaviour being measured, and a
resident who never goes had no chance at all of being asked. The estimate of
15000 is therefore far too high. Full credit needs the self-selecting nature of
the group plus the direction of the error.

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{Cards 1 to 20 with the 7 removed; Priya gives odd as $\frac{10}{20}$.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) There were 10 odd cards among 20. The card removed, the
7, is odd, so 9 odd cards remain among 19:
$\dfrac{9}{19}$. \ans{\tfrac{9}{19}}

(b) \textbf{Instructor-judged.} Priya used the counts from before the card was
removed. Because the removed card was itself one of the favourable outcomes, it
comes off the top as well as the bottom. Full credit needs that point; a
response that only reduces the total to 19 has half of it.

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{Class of 30; 18 play an instrument; 12 both play and sing. Leo gives
$\frac{12}{18}$.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) The student is chosen from the whole class, so the
denominator is 30: $\dfrac{12}{30} = \dfrac{2}{5}$. \ans{\tfrac{2}{5}}

(b) \textbf{Instructor-judged.} The denominator must match the group the choice
is made from. Leo divided by the 18 instrument players, which answers a
different question --- the chance that a \emph{player} also sings --- rather
than the chance that a member of the class does both. Full credit needs that
mismatch named.

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{Club of 250; 40 of them reply and 34 say yes.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) $\dfrac{34}{40} \times 250 = 0.85 \times 250 = 212.5$, so
the organiser announced about 213 members. \ans{212.5}

(b) \textbf{Instructor-judged.} The 40 repliers chose themselves. Members who
are keen on the trip are the ones most likely to answer an email about it, so
the fraction saying yes among repliers is higher than among all 250 members, and
the estimate is too high. Full credit needs the self-selection point plus the
direction of the error.

(c) \textbf{Instructor-judged.} Any method earns credit that gives every one of
the 250 members the same chance of being counted --- for example, numbering all
250 members, drawing a sample at random, and then following those particular
members up until they answer, instead of accepting whoever happens to reply.
Emailing everyone a second time does not earn credit: it repeats the same
self-selection.
