\problem{\textbf{Median of Team Alpha.}
\par
The list is already in order: $12, 14, 15, 15, 16, 18, 19, 21$. With an even
count (8 values) the median is the mean of the two middle values, the 4th and
the 5th.
\[ \frac{15 + 16}{2} = \frac{31}{2} = 15.5 \]
\par
$\ans{15.5}$}

\problem{\textbf{Median of Team Bravo.}
\par
In order: $8, 11, 15, 15, 16, 20, 24, 27$. The 4th and 5th values are again
$15$ and $16$.
\[ \frac{15 + 16}{2} = 15.5 \]
\par
$\ans{15.5}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Common wrong answer 15:} one middle value was reported on its own. An
even-sized set has \emph{two} middle values and the median sits between them ---
here the median is not even a score anyone earned.
\par\smallskip
\emph{Worth noticing:} both teams have exactly the same median. Center alone
cannot tell these two distributions apart --- that is what problem 12 is about.}

\problem{\textbf{Range of Team Alpha.}
\par
Range is the greatest value minus the least value.
\[ 21 - 12 = 9 \]
\par
$\ans{9}$}

\problem{\textbf{Range of Team Bravo.}
\par
\[ 27 - 8 = 19 \]
\par
$\ans{19}$
\par\smallskip
Bravo's range is more than twice Alpha's, even though the two medians match.}

\problem{\textbf{One marble, probability of blue.}
\par
A single draw is a simple probability: favourable outcomes over total outcomes.
The bag holds $5 + 3 + 4 = 12$ marbles, and 3 of them are blue.
\[ P(\text{blue}) = \frac{3}{12} = \frac{1}{4} \]
\par
$\ans{\dfrac{1}{4}}$}

\problem{\textbf{Interquartile range of Team Bravo.}
\par
Split the ordered list at the median into two halves of four values each:
\[ \underbrace{8,\ 11,\ 15,\ 15}_{\text{lower half}} \quad\Big|\quad
   \underbrace{16,\ 20,\ 24,\ 27}_{\text{upper half}} \]
$Q_1$ is the median of the lower half: $\dfrac{11 + 15}{2} = 13$.
\par
$Q_3$ is the median of the upper half: $\dfrac{20 + 24}{2} = 22$.
\[ \text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1 = 22 - 13 = 9 \]
\par
$\ans{9}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Common wrong answer 14:} the greatest value $27$ was used in place of
$Q_3$, giving $27 - 13$. The IQR deliberately ignores the extremes --- that is
the whole reason it exists beside the range.}

\problem{\textbf{Spinner red AND cube shows 5.}
\par
Two stages happen together, so this is a compound event. The spin does not
change the cube, so the stages are independent and the probabilities multiply.
\par
Spinner: 3 of the 8 equal sections are red, so $P(\text{red}) = \dfrac{3}{8}$.
\par
Cube: one face out of 6 shows a $5$, so $P(5) = \dfrac{1}{6}$.
\[ \frac{3}{8} \times \frac{1}{6} = \frac{3}{48} = \frac{1}{16} \]
\par
$\ans{\dfrac{1}{16}}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Grading note:} a student who adds instead of multiplying gets
$\tfrac{3}{8} + \tfrac{1}{6} = \tfrac{13}{24}$, a probability far larger than
either stage --- a useful sanity check, since ``both'' can never be more likely
than either part alone.}

\problem{\textbf{Mean of Team Bravo.}
\par
Add all eight scores, then divide by how many there are:
\[ 8 + 11 + 15 + 15 + 16 + 20 + 24 + 27 = 136,
   \qquad \frac{136}{8} = 17 \]
\par
$\ans{17}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Common wrong answer 19.43:} the total was divided by 7. Count the values
after adding them --- there are 8 scores, so the divisor is 8.
\par\smallskip
Bravo's mean (17) sits above its median (15.5) because the two high scores,
24 and 27, pull the mean upward. The median does not move.}

\problem{\textbf{Two red marbles, no replacement.}
\par
Compound event again, but the first draw changes the second, so the second
stage must be recalculated from what is left.
\par
First draw: 5 red out of 12 marbles, so $P = \dfrac{5}{12}$.
\par
Second draw: one red marble is gone, so 4 red remain out of 11 marbles, giving
$P = \dfrac{4}{11}$.
\[ \frac{5}{12} \times \frac{4}{11} = \frac{20}{132} = \frac{5}{33} \]
\par
$\ans{\dfrac{5}{33}}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Grading note:} using $\tfrac{5}{12}$ twice gives $\tfrac{25}{144}$, which
is the \emph{with}-replacement answer. The phrase ``not put back'' is what
changes the second fraction.}

\problem{\textbf{Club sign-ups.}
\par
(a) ``Altogether'' means every bar counts:
\[ 9 + 6 + 15 + 10 = 40 \]
\par
$\ans{40}$
\par
(b) Now the 40 slips are the whole sample space and the 15 Robotics slips are
the favourable outcomes:
\[ P(\text{Robotics}) = \frac{15}{40} = \frac{3}{8} \]
\par
$\ans{P = \dfrac{3}{8}}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Grading note:} the total has to be computed before the probability can
be written --- a student who uses 4 (the number of clubs) as the denominator
has counted categories instead of students.}

\problem{\textbf{At least one red in two spins.}
\par
``At least one'' covers three different outcomes (red then not-red, not-red
then red, red then red), so counting them directly is slow and error-prone.
Its opposite is a single case: \emph{neither} spin is red.
\par
Not red on one spin: $8 - 3 = 5$ of the 8 sections, so $P = \dfrac{5}{8}$.
\par
The two spins are independent (the spinner has no memory):
\[ P(\text{no red at all}) = \frac{5}{8} \times \frac{5}{8} = \frac{25}{64} \]
\[ P(\text{at least one red}) = 1 - \frac{25}{64} = \frac{39}{64} \]
\par
$\ans{\dfrac{39}{64}}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Common wrong answer $\tfrac{3}{4}$:} the two single-spin probabilities
were added, $\tfrac{3}{8} + \tfrac{3}{8}$. That double-counts the outcome where
both spins are red, and adding probabilities this way can even pass 1.}

\problem{\textbf{Synthesis --- comparing the two distributions.}
\par
(a) Split Alpha's ordered list at its median:
\[ \underbrace{12,\ 14,\ 15,\ 15}_{\text{lower half}} \quad\Big|\quad
   \underbrace{16,\ 18,\ 19,\ 21}_{\text{upper half}} \]
$Q_1 = \dfrac{14 + 15}{2} = 14.5$, \quad $Q_3 = \dfrac{18 + 19}{2} = 18.5$.
\[ \text{IQR} = 18.5 - 14.5 = 4 \]
\par
$\ans{4}$
\par
(b) Model answer: Team Alpha is more consistent. Both teams have the same
median, $15.5$, so the center says nothing about which team is steadier. The
spread does: Alpha's IQR is $4$ against Bravo's $9$, and Alpha's range is $9$
against Bravo's $19$. Alpha's middle half is packed into a 4-point band and its
whole set fits in 9 points, while Bravo's scores stretch from $8$ to $27$. On
the dot plots, Alpha's dots cluster around the middle and Bravo's trail off in
both directions.
\par\smallskip
(c) Two independent choices, one from each list, so multiply.
\par
Alpha scores greater than 18: $19$ and $21$, so 2 of the 8 --- $P = \dfrac{2}{8}$.
\par
Bravo scores greater than 18: $20$, $24$ and $27$, so 3 of the 8 ---
$P = \dfrac{3}{8}$.
\[ \frac{2}{8} \times \frac{3}{8} = \frac{6}{64} = \frac{3}{32} \]
\par
$\ans{P = \dfrac{3}{32}}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Manual review:} part (b) is a written comparison, read by a teacher.
Parts (a) and (c) are machine-checked. Credit (b) when the student says the
medians are equal AND uses at least one spread measure to name Alpha as the
more consistent team.}
