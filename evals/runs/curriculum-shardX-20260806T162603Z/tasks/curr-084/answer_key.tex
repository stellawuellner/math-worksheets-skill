% ---------------------------------------------------------------- Problem 1
\problem{This rectangle is cut into 4 equal parts, and 1 part is shaded. Write
the fraction of the rectangle that is shaded.}

\textbf{Solution:} The parts are equal, so a fraction may be named: count the
shaded parts over all the parts. There is 1 shaded part out of 4 equal parts,
so the shaded fraction is \ans{\tfrac{1}{4}}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- Problem 2
\problem{Mia cut this circle into three parts and shaded one part. The parts are
not all the same size. Say why Mia cannot call the shaded part one third yet.}

\textbf{Model answer (instructor judges):} A fraction like one third only names
one of three \emph{equal} parts. Mia's circle is cut into three parts of
different sizes, so ``1 out of 3 parts'' does not tell how much of the circle
is shaded. Mia must first cut the circle into three equal parts (or measure
with equal units) before naming the shaded amount one third.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- Problem 3
\problem{Rectangle A and Rectangle B are the same size, each cut into four parts
with one part shaded. (a) What fraction of Rectangle A is shaded? (b) Circle
the rectangle that truly shows one fourth, and tell how you know.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Rectangle A is cut into 4 \emph{equal} parts and 1 is
shaded, so the shaded fraction is \ans{\tfrac{1}{4}}

\textbf{Model answer (b), instructor judges:} Rectangle \textbf{A} should be
circled. A's four parts are all the same size, so each part is truly one
fourth. B's parts are different sizes, so B's shaded part is 1 of 4 parts but
\emph{not} one fourth of the whole.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- Problem 4
\problem{Deshawn split the rectangle into three parts using heavy lines and
shaded the small part. He says the shaded part is $\tfrac{1}{3}$ because it is
1 part out of 3. (a) How many small equal squares make the whole? (b) What
fraction is really shaded? (c) Name Deshawn's mistake.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The rectangle is a grid of 2 rows with 3 small squares
in each row, so the whole holds $2 \times 3 = 6$ equal squares. \ans{6}

\textbf{Solution (b):} The shaded part covers 1 small square out of the 6 equal
squares, so the true shaded fraction is $\tfrac{1}{6}$, not $\tfrac{1}{3}$.
The really-shaded fraction is \ans{\tfrac{1}{6}}

\textbf{Model answer (c), instructor judges:} Deshawn counted parts that are
\emph{not equal}. ``1 part out of 3'' only makes the fraction one third when
the three parts are the same size. Because they are not, he must count the
equal small squares instead.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- Problem 5
\problem{Priya drew a number line from 0 to 1 that is 8 cm long, with tick marks
that are not evenly spaced; she labeled the first mark $\tfrac{1}{4}$.
(a) How many centimeters apart should the tick marks be to show fourths?
(b) Say what was wrong with Priya's number line.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Fourths cut the whole line into 4 equal lengths. The
line is 8 cm long, so each equal length is $8 \div 4 = 2$, meaning the marks
belong every \ans{2\ \text{cm}}

\textbf{Model answer (b), instructor judges:} Priya's tick marks were not
evenly spaced, so the pieces of her line were not equal. Fraction marks on a
number line must cut the distance from 0 to 1 into \emph{equal} lengths; only
then does the first mark show one fourth.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- Problem 6
\problem{Ben folded a strip into two parts and says each part is one half. He
measures one part at 12 cm and the other at 8 cm. (a) Is Ben right? Say how
you know. (b) If the strip were folded into 2 equal parts, how long would each
part be?}

\textbf{Model answer (a), instructor judges:} No. Halves must be two
\emph{equal} parts, and 12 cm is longer than 8 cm, so the fold did not make
halves --- neither part is one half of the strip.

\textbf{Solution (b):} The whole strip is $12 + 8 = 20$ centimeters long.
Folding into 2 equal parts gives $20 \div 2 = 10$, so each part should be
\ans{10\ \text{cm}}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- Problem 7
\problem{Tia's garden is split into 4 unequal sections; the tomato section
covers 4 small squares. Tia says tomatoes take up $\tfrac{1}{4}$ of the garden
because they are 1 section out of 4. (a) What fraction do tomatoes really
cover? (b) Explain what Tia got wrong.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The garden grid holds $2 \times 4 = 8$ equal small
squares, and the tomato section covers 4 of them: $\tfrac{4}{8}$ of the garden,
which is the same as one half. The true fraction is \ans{\tfrac{1}{2}}

\textbf{Model answer (b), instructor judges:} Tia counted sections that are not
equal in size. Being 1 section out of 4 only means one fourth when all four
sections are equal. The equal small squares show tomatoes really cover half
the garden --- twice as much as Tia claimed.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------- Problem 8
\problem{The grid shows 3 rows of 4 small squares. (a) Cut the grid into six
equal parts and shade one part. (b) How many small squares are in each equal
part? (c) What fraction of the whole rectangle is one small square?}

\textbf{Model answer (a), instructor judges:} Any cutting that gives six parts
of \emph{equal size} earns credit --- for example, six 2-square dominoes
(standing or lying down). Each part must cover the same number of small
squares, and exactly one part is shaded. Parts may have different shapes as
long as their sizes are equal.

\textbf{Solution (b):} The grid holds $3 \times 4 = 12$ small squares shared
among 6 equal parts, so each part gets $12 \div 6 = 2$ squares. \ans{2}

\textbf{Solution (c):} One small square is 1 of the 12 equal squares that make
the whole, so it is \ans{\tfrac{1}{12}}
