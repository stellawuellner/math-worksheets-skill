\problem{Write $0.35$ as a fraction of two integers in lowest terms.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad The decimal terminates in the hundredths place, so it is
$\dfrac{35}{100}$. Dividing numerator and denominator by their common factor five gives
lowest terms, which proves the number is rational. \ans{\tfrac{7}{20}}

\problem{Evaluate $\sqrt{121}$, and write the value as a fraction of two integers.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad The radicand is a perfect square, since $11 \times 11 = 121$, so
the root is a whole number. Every whole number is itself over one, so the value is
rational. \ans{11}

\problem{$\sqrt{54}$ cannot be written as a fraction, so locate it instead.
(a) Between which whole numbers? (b) To the nearest hundredth? (c) Mark it.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Trap the radicand between neighbouring perfect squares:
$7^2 = 49$ and $8^2 = 64$, and $49 < 54 < 64$. Taking roots keeps the order, so the value
lies between \ans{7} and \ans{8}.

\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad Squeeze with hundredths: $7.34^2 = 53.8756$ and
$7.35^2 = 54.0225$, so the root is between them and rounds up. \ans{7.35}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The dot belongs between the whole
number ticks found in part (a), noticeably past the middle of that interval (the value is
about a third of the way past the lower tick), and labelled with the root symbol or the
rounded decimal. A dot left of the midpoint, or sitting on a tick, is not correct.

\problem{Show that the repeating decimal $0.\overline{6}$ is rational, using
$x = 0.\overline{6}$ and the subtract-the-copy method.}

\textbf{Solution:}
\begin{align*}
  x &= 0.666\ldots \\
  10x &= 6.666\ldots \\
  10x - x &= 6.666\ldots - 0.666\ldots \\
  9x &= 6
\end{align*}
The repeating tails cancel exactly, leaving a whole number on the right. Dividing by nine
and reducing gives a ratio of two integers, so the number is rational. \ans{x = \tfrac{2}{3}}

\problem{Consider $\sqrt{169}$. (a) Evaluate it. (b) Explain how the value shows the
number is rational.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $13 \times 13 = 169$, so the radicand is a perfect square.
\ans{13}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad A correct sentence links the computed
whole number to the definition: the root came out a whole number, and any whole number can
be written over one, so the value is a ratio of two integers and therefore rational.
Answers that only restate the value, or that claim the radical sign forces irrationality,
do not earn credit.

\problem{One of $\pi$ and $\dfrac{22}{7}$ is irrational and one is rational.
(a) Write $<$, $>$, or $=$ between them. (b) Mark both on the number line.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Write out enough digits to separate them:
$\pi = 3.14159\ldots$ while $\dfrac{22}{7} = 3.142857\ldots$, and the two decimals first
differ in the thousandths place, where one is smaller. \ans{<}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad Two labelled dots, both between the
ticks at three and one tenth and at three and two tenths, with the fraction's dot to the
\emph{right} of the one for the circle constant. Credit depends on the left-to-right order
being right, not on perfect placement.

\problem{Consider $\dfrac{9}{12}$. (a) Write it in lowest terms. (b) Write it as a
decimal.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Both parts share a factor of three, so dividing top and
bottom by three reduces the fraction. \ans{\tfrac{3}{4}}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad Dividing the reduced numerator by the reduced denominator
terminates after two places. \ans{0.75}

\problem{Evaluate $\sqrt{\dfrac{9}{4}}$ and write the value as a fraction of two integers
in lowest terms.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad The root of a fraction is the root of the top over the root of
the bottom, and both parts are perfect squares. The result is already in lowest terms, so
this root is rational even though it is not a whole number. \ans{\tfrac{3}{2}}

\problem{Write $\sqrt{7}$, $\sqrt{9}$, $\pi$, $\dfrac{10}{3}$ in order from least to
greatest.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Convert every entry to a decimal before comparing:
$\sqrt{7} = 2.6457\ldots$, $\sqrt{9} = 3$, $\pi = 3.14159\ldots$, and
$\dfrac{10}{3} = 3.333\ldots$. Two of these are irrational and two are rational, but
ordering only needs the decimals.
\[ \boxed{\sqrt{7} \;<\; \sqrt{9} \;<\; \pi \;<\; \tfrac{10}{3}} \]

\problem{\textbf{Challenge.} Tomas says the digits of $0.1\overline{6}$ never stop, so it
must be irrational. (a) Write it as a fraction. (b) Explain what is wrong with his reason.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Only the last digit repeats, so shift twice and subtract.
\begin{align*}
  x &= 0.1666\ldots \\
  10x &= 1.666\ldots \\
  100x &= 16.666\ldots \\
  100x - 10x &= 16.666\ldots - 1.666\ldots \\
  90x &= 15
\end{align*}
Dividing by ninety and reducing gives a ratio of two integers. \ans{x = \tfrac{1}{6}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad Tomas has confused ``never ends'' with
``never repeats''. A decimal whose digits eventually repeat a fixed block forever is
rational --- part (a) produced the fraction that proves it. Only a decimal that never ends
\emph{and} never settles into a repeating block is irrational. Full credit requires naming
the repeating block or citing the fraction from part (a).
