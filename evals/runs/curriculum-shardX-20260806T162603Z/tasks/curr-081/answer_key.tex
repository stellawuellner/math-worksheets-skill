\problem{A rectangle is cut into $4$ equal pieces; one piece is shaded. What unit fraction is shaded?}

\textbf{Solution:} Count the equal pieces that make the whole rectangle: there are $4$ of them.
A unit fraction names one of those pieces, so the top number is $1$ and the bottom number is the
count of equal pieces. \ans{\tfrac{1}{4}}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{A circle is cut into $3$ equal pieces; one piece is shaded. What unit fraction is shaded?}

\textbf{Solution:} The whole circle is made of $3$ equal wedges. One wedge out of those three is
one third. The shape changed from a rectangle to a circle, but the rule did not: the bottom number
is the number of equal pieces in the whole. \ans{\tfrac{1}{3}}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{A window pane is cut into $6$ equal pieces; one piece is shaded. What unit fraction is shaded?}

\textbf{Solution:} The pane is $2$ rows of $3$ panes, so the whole is $6$ equal pieces. One of them
is one sixth. Note that the pieces are arranged in a grid rather than a strip; what matters is that
all six are the same size. \ans{\tfrac{1}{6}}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{A chocolate bar is cut into $8$ equal pieces; one piece is shaded. (a) What unit fraction is shaded? (b) How many pieces that size fill the whole bar?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $8$ equal pieces make the whole bar, so one piece is one eighth. \ans{\tfrac{1}{8}}

\textbf{Solution (b):} Ask how many one-eighths fit in $1$ whole: $1 \div \frac{1}{8} = 8$. This is
the same fact read backwards --- a unit fraction's bottom number IS the number of copies it takes to
rebuild the whole. \ans{8}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Two shapes are each cut into four pieces, one piece shaded. (a) Circle the model that shows fourths. (b) Explain why the other does not.}

\textbf{Solution (a) --- instructor-judged.} Model \textbf{B} is the one that shows fourths: its four
pieces are all the same width. Model A also has four pieces, but they are different sizes, so no
single piece of A is one fourth of the whole. Credit B only.

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} A full-credit explanation says the pieces of model A are
\emph{not all the same size}, so counting four pieces is not enough to make fourths. Model answer:
``A has four pieces, but they are different sizes. Fourths have to be four equal pieces, so the
shaded piece of A is not one fourth.'' Answers that only say ``it looks wrong'' or that only count
the pieces do not earn full credit; the idea being graded is \emph{equal} parts.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Two ribbons of the same length are cut into $3$ and $6$ equal pieces. Compare one piece of each with $>$, $<$, or $=$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Both ribbons started the same length. Cutting into more pieces makes every piece
smaller, so a third of the ribbon is larger than a sixth of it --- the picture shows the shaded third
covering exactly two of the sixths. Bigger bottom number, smaller piece. \ans{\tfrac{1}{3} > \tfrac{1}{6}}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Three flags of the same size are cut into $2$, $4$, and $8$ equal pieces. Write the three shaded unit fractions in order, smallest first.}

\textbf{Solution:} The flags are the same size, so compare the number of pieces each was cut into.
Cutting into $8$ gives the smallest piece, then $4$, then $2$: more pieces means each piece is
smaller. Ordering the unit fractions smallest first therefore means ordering the bottom numbers
largest first. \ans{\tfrac{1}{8},\ \tfrac{1}{4},\ \tfrac{1}{2}}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{A garden bed is divided into $8$ equal plots. (a) What unit fraction is one plot? (b) Shade three plots and name the fraction shaded.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The whole bed is $8$ equal plots, so one plot is one eighth of the bed. \ans{\tfrac{1}{8}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The student should shade exactly three of the eight equal
plots (any three) and then say the shaded amount is three copies of one eighth --- three eighths.
Model answer: ``I shaded three plots. Each plot is $\frac{1}{8}$, so three of them is
$\frac{1}{8} + \frac{1}{8} + \frac{1}{8} = \frac{3}{8}$.'' Full credit needs both the correct
shading and the counting-of-unit-fractions idea; naming three eighths with no reference to the
one-eighth pieces is partial credit.
