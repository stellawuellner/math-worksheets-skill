\noindent\small\textbf{The habit this sheet builds:} a trend line will return a number for
any input you hand it, including inputs the data never covered and outputs the quiz cannot
award. Computing the prediction is the easy half; the graded half is the sentence that
says how far the prediction can be trusted. Full credit on the explanation problems needs
a reason tied to \emph{this} plot --- its range, its one unusual point, or the gap between
association and cause --- not a general remark about being careful.\normalsize

\problem{\textbf{Reading one point off the plot.}\par
Find 45 on the horizontal axis, go straight up to the dot, then across to the vertical
axis. The dot sits level with $\ans{85}$.\par
Note this is a \emph{read}, not a prediction: this student is in the data. The trend line
at 45 minutes gives $0.8(45) + 45 = 81$, so the line runs 4 points below what this student
actually scored --- normal scatter, not an error.}

\problem{\textbf{The highest score, and whether it fits.}\par
Scan the plot for the highest dot, then read \emph{down} to the axis rather than guessing
from the trend. The top dot sits at a study time of $\ans{22}$ minutes.\par
\textbf{Does it fit the trend?} No. Every other dot climbs steadily from left to right, and
the trend line predicts $0.8(22) + 45 = 62.6$ at that study time --- more than 27 points
below the score this student earned. It is an \textbf{outlier}: a real data point that does
not follow the pattern of the rest. It is worth noticing early, because it moves the mean
(problems 6 and 11) and it weakens any claim built on the trend alone.}

\problem{\textbf{Predicting inside the data range.}\par
The prediction is just the trend line evaluated at the given study time. Substitute
$x = 30$ into $y = 0.8x + 45$:
\[ y = 0.8(30) + 45 = 24 + 45 \]
The predicted score is $\ans{69}$.\par
This prediction is \textbf{interpolation}: 30 minutes lies inside the 10-to-50-minute
range the class actually studied, so the plot is real evidence for it.\par
\textit{Watch for:} adding instead of multiplying, $0.8 + 30 + 45 = 75.8$. The slope is a
rate per minute, so it multiplies the minutes.}

\problem{\textbf{A prediction and its residual.}\par
(a) Substitute $x = 25$ into the trend line:
\[ y = 0.8(25) + 45 = 20 + 45 \]
The predicted score is $\ans{65}$.\par
(b) A residual is always \emph{actual minus predicted}, in that order, so the sign carries
meaning:
\[ 68 - 65 \]
The residual is $\ans{3}$ points.\par
Because the residual is \textbf{positive}, the student scored above the line: the line
\emph{under}-predicted this student by 3 points. A negative residual would have meant the
opposite. Residuals are how you measure whether a line fits, one point at a time.}

\problem{\textbf{Why association is not cause.}\par
A scatter plot shows only that two quantities move together in this class's data. It
cannot rule out a third factor that moves both, and it cannot show which direction any
influence runs.\par
A full-credit answer names \emph{one} reason and \emph{one} plausible alternative factor.
Reasons that earn credit: the data was observed, not from an experiment where study time
was assigned; nobody controlled anything else; a pattern in ten students is not evidence
about students in general. Alternative factors that earn credit: students who already
understand the material both study more comfortably and score higher; sleep; tutoring;
interest in the subject; test-taking practice.\par
Reversal is also worth accepting: students who expect to do well may be the ones willing
to sit and study, so the arrow could point the other way.\par
Open explanation --- $\ans{\text{open response}}$; check that a specific alternative
factor is named, not just the phrase ``correlation is not causation''.}

\problem{\textbf{The mean score of all ten students.}\par
Add every score on the plot, then divide by the number of dots:
\[ 52 + 58 + 61 + 90 + 68 + 70 + 76 + 79 + 85 + 88 = 727 \]
\[ \text{mean} = \frac{727}{10} \]
The mean is $\ans{72.7}$ points.\par
\textbf{Which way does the outlier pull?} Up. The 90 sits far above the trend at its own
study time, and it is the only score that does. Problem 11 measures the pull exactly: the
mean drops to about 70.78 without it, so that single student lifts the class mean by
roughly 2 points.}

\problem{\textbf{Predicting outside the data range.}\par
The arithmetic is identical to problem 3 --- substitute $x = 60$:
\[ y = 0.8(60) + 45 = 48 + 45 \]
The trend line predicts $\ans{93}$.\par
The number is right; the confidence is the issue, and that is problem 8. Nobody in this
class studied 60 minutes, so the plot contains no evidence that the line still holds
there.}

\problem{\textbf{Interpolation versus extrapolation.}\par
\textbf{Interpolation} means predicting at an input \emph{inside} the range the data
covers. Study times on the plot run from 10 to 50 minutes, so the 30-minute prediction of
69 sits well inside that range, with real dots on both sides of it supporting the line.\par
\textbf{Extrapolation} means predicting at an input \emph{outside} that range. The
60-minute prediction of 93 is extrapolation: it assumes the straight-line pattern keeps
going past the last dot, and nothing in the data shows that it does. Real score-versus-study
relationships usually flatten out --- there is only so much a quiz can measure, and a score
cannot pass 100 at all.\par
Full credit needs both words used correctly \emph{and} the range 10 to 50 cited as the
reason. Open explanation --- $\ans{\text{open response}}$.}

\problem{\textbf{Comparing two students' scores.}\par
The question asks about \emph{scores}, so read both dots on the vertical axis and subtract
those, not the study times:
\[ 88 - 52 \]
The 50-minute student scored $\ans{36}$ points higher.\par
\textit{Watch for:} subtracting the study times, $50 - 10 = 40$. That is a difference in
minutes, not in points --- a units error that looks plausible because the numbers are close.}

\problem{\textbf{Where two trend lines agree.}\par
Two lines predict the same score at the study time where their outputs are equal, so set
the expressions equal and solve:
\[ 0.8x + 45 = 1.3x + 27 \]
\[ 45 - 27 = 1.3x - 0.8x \quad\Rightarrow\quad 18 = 0.5x \]
The two classes agree at $\ans{36}$ minutes.\par
\textit{Check:} first class $0.8(36) + 45 = 73.8$; second class $1.3(36) + 27 = 73.8$
$\checkmark$\par
Reading it: below 36 minutes the first class's line predicts higher, above it the second
class's steeper line takes over. The steeper slope means that class's scores rise faster
per minute studied.}

\problem{\textbf{The mean with the outlier removed.}\par
Drop the 90 from the list and divide by the nine scores that remain:
\[ 52 + 58 + 61 + 68 + 70 + 76 + 79 + 85 + 88 = 637 \]
\[ \text{mean} = \frac{637}{9} = 70.777\ldots \]
Rounded to the nearest hundredth, the mean is $\ans{70.78}$ points.\par
\textbf{What the comparison shows:} removing one student out of ten moved the class mean
from 72.7 down to about 70.78, nearly 2 points. One unusual value in a small data set moves
a summary noticeably --- which is exactly why a prediction built on a small sample deserves
a stated caution.\par
\textit{Watch for:} removing the score from the total but still dividing by ten, which
gives $637 \div 10 = 63.7$ --- a ``mean'' below every score but one, and impossible.}

\problem{\textbf{Challenge: judging the newspaper's claim.}\par
The claim is arithmetically consistent with the line and indefensible as a statement about
students. A full-credit paragraph makes at least four of these points:
\begin{itemize}[label={$\bullet$}, leftmargin=1.1cm, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item \textbf{The line does say it:} $0.8(75) + 45 = 105$, so the number is not a
        misreading of the line.
  \item \textbf{It is far outside the data:} study times run from 10 to 50 minutes.
        Predicting at 75 minutes is extrapolation half again beyond the largest observed
        value, and nothing on the plot supports the trend continuing there.
  \item \textbf{The prediction is impossible:} a quiz score cannot exceed 100. A model
        that returns 105 has been pushed past where it can be true, which is itself proof
        the straight line stops describing reality somewhere before 75 minutes.
  \item \textbf{One point already contradicts the trend:} the student who studied 22
        minutes scored 90, and removing that single point moves the class mean by about 2
        points. Ten students, one of them an outlier, is thin evidence for a headline.
  \item \textbf{``Score about'' hides a causal claim:} even inside the data range, the plot
        shows association, not that studying longer \emph{produces} higher scores.
\end{itemize}
\textbf{What the data does support:} within roughly 10 to 50 minutes of study, in this one
class, longer study times went with higher quiz scores, at a rate of about 0.8 points per
extra minute, with one clear exception.\par
Open synthesis --- $\ans{\text{open response}}$; check that the paragraph both makes a
defensible positive claim and names the limits.}
