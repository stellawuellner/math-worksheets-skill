\problem{Tank, $r(t) = 3t^2 + 4$ L/min,
$V(t) = \int_0^t (3u^2 + 4)\,du$. Find $V'(5)$.}

\textbf{Solution.} The Fundamental Theorem says the derivative of an
accumulation function is the integrand evaluated at the upper limit --- no
antiderivative required: $V'(t) = 3t^2 + 4$, so $V'(5) = 3(25) + 4$.
\[ \boxed{V'(5) = 79} \]
Litres per minute, which is simply the pump's rate at that moment.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Same tank. Find the volume added during the first $5$ minutes.}

\textbf{Solution.} Here we do need an antiderivative:
$\int (3t^2 + 4)\,dt = t^3 + 4t$, so
\[ V(5) = \left[t^3 + 4t\right]_0^5 = (125 + 20) - 0. \]
\[ \boxed{145\ \text{L}} \]

\problem{$P(x) = 200 + \int_0^x \frac{12}{1+t}\,dt$ thousand cells. (a) $P'(3)$.
(b) Why the culture never shrinks.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} The constant $200$ differentiates away and the integral
contributes its integrand at $x$: $P'(x) = \dfrac{12}{1 + x}$, so
$P'(3) = \dfrac{12}{4}$.
\[ \boxed{P'(3) = 3} \]
Thousand cells per day.

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} $P'(x) = \dfrac{12}{1 + x}$ is
positive for every $x$ in $[0, 10]$, and a function whose derivative is positive
throughout an interval is increasing on it. Credit any wording that connects the
sign of the integrand to the sign of $P'$; a response that only says ``the
integral keeps adding'' without naming the derivative has not used the theorem.

\problem{$Q(x) = \int_1^{x^2}\sqrt{1 + t^3}\,dt$. (a) Exact $Q'(2)$.
(b) To two decimals.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} The upper limit is $x^2$, not $x$, so the chain rule
attaches its derivative:
\[ Q'(x) = \sqrt{1 + (x^2)^3}\cdot 2x = 2x\sqrt{1 + x^6}. \]
At $x = 2$: $\sqrt{1 + 64} = \sqrt{65}$ and $2x = 4$.
\[ \boxed{Q'(2) = 4\sqrt{65}} \]

\textbf{Solution (b).}
\[ \boxed{Q'(2) \approx 32.25} \]
Millicoulombs per second.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$C(x) = \int_x^{12}(0.5t + 2)\,dt$ litres. (a) $C'(4)$. (b) Why $C'$ is
negative.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} Swap the limits to put the variable on top:
$C(x) = -\int_{12}^{x}(0.5t + 2)\,dt$, so $C'(x) = -(0.5x + 2)$ and
$C'(4) = -(2 + 2)$.
\[ \boxed{C'(4) = -4} \]
Litres per hour.

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The variable is the \emph{lower}
limit. Reversing the limits to apply the theorem introduces the minus sign, and
the physical reading agrees: raising the lower limit throws away coolant that
had already been counted, so the amount remaining falls. Credit either the
limit-reversal argument or the physical one.

\problem{Pond, $r(t) = 6 - t$ m$^3$/min, $V(x) = \int_0^x (6-t)\,dt$.
(a) $V'(8)$. (b) Where $V$ peaks. (c) Justify.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} $V'(x) = 6 - x$, so $V'(8) = 6 - 8$.
\[ \boxed{V'(8) = -2} \]

\textbf{Solution (b).} $V' = 0$ when $6 - x = 0$.
\[ \boxed{x = 6} \]

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} $V'(x) = 6 - x$ is positive for
$x < 6$ and negative for $x > 6$, so $V$ rises up to $x = 6$ and falls after it;
that makes $x = 6$ the absolute maximum on $[0, 10]$. Full credit needs the sign
change of the rate. ``$V'(6) = 0$'' alone locates a candidate, not a maximum.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Same pond. (a) $V(6)$. (b) $V(10)$.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} $\int_0^6 (6 - t)\,dt = \left[6t - \tfrac{t^2}{2}\right]_0^6
= 36 - 18$.
\[ \boxed{V(6) = 18\ \text{m}^3} \]

\textbf{Solution (b).} $\int_0^{10}(6 - t)\,dt = 60 - 50$.
\[ \boxed{V(10) = 10\ \text{m}^3} \]
The pond gains water for six minutes and then gives most of it back, which is
exactly what the sign change in part 6 predicted.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$F(x) = \int_{2x}^{x^2} e^{-t^2}\,dt$. (a) Exact $F'(1)$. (b) To three
decimals. (c) Why $F'$ is not $e^{-x^4} - e^{-4x^2}$.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} Split at a constant, say $0$:
\[ F(x) = \int_0^{x^2} e^{-t^2}\,dt - \int_0^{2x} e^{-t^2}\,dt . \]
Each piece contributes its integrand at its own limit, times that limit's
derivative:
\[ F'(x) = e^{-(x^2)^2}\cdot 2x - e^{-(2x)^2}\cdot 2 = 2x\,e^{-x^4} - 2e^{-4x^2}. \]
At $x = 1$ this is
\[ \boxed{F'(1) = 2e^{-1} - 2e^{-4}} \]

\textbf{Solution (b).}
\[ \boxed{F'(1) \approx 0.699} \]

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} Both limits move, so both
contribute, and each contribution carries the derivative of its own limit ---
$2x$ from the upper limit and $2$ from the lower. Dropping those factors is the
error the shortcut form makes. Full credit requires naming both chain-rule
factors, not merely observing that there is a subtraction.
