\problem{\textbf{$f(x) = x^3 - 4x^2 + x + 6$; test $x = 2$.}
\[ f(2) = 8 - 16 + 2 + 6 = 0 \]
A remainder of zero is exactly what ``$x=2$ is a zero'' means, so $(x - 2)$ is a
factor of $f$. $\ans{f(2) = 0}$}

\problem{\textbf{All zeros of $f(x) = x^3 - 4x^2 + x + 6$.} Divide out the
factor found in problem 1. Synthetic division by $x - 2$ on the coefficients
$1,\ -4,\ 1,\ 6$ gives the quotient coefficients $1,\ -2,\ -3$ with remainder
$0$:
\[ f(x) = (x-2)(x^2 - 2x - 3) = (x-2)(x-3)(x+1) \]
Set each factor to zero. $\ans{x = -1,\ x = 2,\ x = 3}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Check:} the three zeros multiply to $-1 \cdot 2 \cdot 3 = -6$, and for a
monic cubic that product must equal $-(\text{constant term}) = -6$. \checkmark}

\problem{\textbf{Factor $g(x) = x^3 + 2x^2 - 5x - 6$.} Constant $-6$, leading
coefficient $1$, so the candidates are $\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3, \pm 6$.
$g(1) = -8 \neq 0$; $g(-1) = -1 + 2 + 5 - 6 = 0$, so $(x+1)$ is a factor.
Dividing gives $x^2 + x - 6$, which factors by inspection.
\[ g(x) = (x+1)(x^2+x-6) \]
$\ans{(x+1)(x+3)(x-2)}$}

\problem{\textbf{$h(x) = 2x^3 - x^2 - 8x + 4$; test $x = \frac{1}{2}$.} With
leading coefficient $2$, the theorem allows $q \in \{1, 2\}$, so halves join the
candidate list.
\[ h\!\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right) = 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{8} - \tfrac{1}{4} - 4 + 4
   = \tfrac{1}{4} - \tfrac{1}{4} = 0 \]
So $x = \frac{1}{2}$ is a zero and $(2x - 1)$ is a factor.
$\ans{h(\tfrac{1}{2}) = 0}$}

\problem{\textbf{All zeros of $h(x) = 2x^3 - x^2 - 8x + 4$.} Divide out the
factor from problem 4:
\[ h(x) = (2x-1)(x^2-4) = (2x-1)(x-2)(x+2) \]
The difference of squares finishes it with no further division needed.
$\ans{x = \tfrac{1}{2},\ x = 2,\ x = -2}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Note the form of the factor:} the zero $\frac{1}{2}$ corresponds to the
factor $(2x-1)$, not $(x - \frac{1}{2})$, when you keep integer coefficients.}

\problem{\textbf{All zeros of $p(x) = x^3 - 4x^2 + 2x + 4$.} Candidates
$\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 4$. $p(1) = 3$, $p(-1) = -3$, $p(2) = 8 - 16 + 4 + 4 = 0$.
Divide by $x - 2$: the quotient is $x^2 - 2x - 2$, which has no rational zeros,
so use the quadratic formula:
\[ x = \frac{2 \pm \sqrt{4 + 8}}{2} = \frac{2 \pm 2\sqrt{3}}{2} = 1 \pm \sqrt{3} \]
$\ans{x = 2,\ x = 1 + \sqrt{3},\ x = 1 - \sqrt{3}}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Why the theorem could not find the last two:} $1 \pm \sqrt{3}$ is
irrational, and the theorem only ever produces rational candidates.}

\problem{\textbf{Factor $q(x) = 3x^3 - x^2 - 12x + 4$.} Candidates are
$\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 4, \pm \frac{1}{3}, \pm \frac{2}{3}, \pm \frac{4}{3}$.
Testing $x = \frac{1}{3}$: $3 \cdot \frac{1}{27} - \frac{1}{9} - 4 + 4 = 0$, so
$(3x - 1)$ is a factor. Division leaves $x^2 - 4$:
\[ q(x) = (3x-1)(x^2-4) \]
$\ans{(3x-1)(x-2)(x+2)}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Shortcut worth naming:} grouping also works here ---
$x^2(3x-1) - 4(3x-1)$ --- and grouping is always worth a look when the terms
pair up like this.}

\problem{\textbf{Solve $x^3 - x^2 - 13x - 3 = 0$.} Candidates $\pm 1, \pm 3$.
Substituting: $-16$, $9$, $-60$, and at $x = -3$,
$-27 - 9 + 39 - 3 = 0$. So $(x + 3)$ divides the cubic, and synthetic division
gives the quotient $x^2 - 4x - 1$:
\[ x = \frac{4 \pm \sqrt{16 + 4}}{2} = \frac{4 \pm 2\sqrt{5}}{2} = 2 \pm \sqrt{5} \]
$\ans{x = -3,\ x = 2 + \sqrt{5},\ x = 2 - \sqrt{5}}$}

\problem{\textbf{All zeros of $r(x) = x^4 - 2x^3 - 7x^2 + 8x + 12$.} Candidates
$\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3, \pm 4, \pm 6, \pm 12$. $r(-1) = 1 + 2 - 7 - 8 + 12 = 0$,
so divide by $x + 1$:
\[ r(x) = (x+1)(x^3 - 3x^2 - 4x + 12) \]
Run the routine again on the cubic: its candidates are the divisors of $12$, and
$x = 2$ gives $8 - 12 - 8 + 12 = 0$. Dividing again leaves $x^2 - x - 6$, which
factors as $(x-3)(x+2)$:
\[ r(x) = (x+1)(x-2)(x-3)(x+2) \]
$\ans{x = -2,\ x = -1,\ x = 2,\ x = 3}$}

\problem{\textbf{All zeros of $s(x) = x^3 - x^2 + 4x - 4$, including non-real
ones.} Candidates $\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 4$. $s(1) = 1 - 1 + 4 - 4 = 0$, so
$(x - 1)$ is a factor, and grouping confirms it:
$x^2(x-1) + 4(x-1) = (x-1)(x^2+4)$. Then $x^2 = -4$ gives $x = \pm 2i$.
$\ans{x = 1,\ x = 2i,\ x = -2i}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Answer to the second question:} the theorem supplied exactly one zero, the
rational one. The other two are non-real, and non-real zeros are never on a
candidate list. The count still works out: degree $3$, three zeros.}

\problem{\textbf{Factor $t(x) = 4x^3 - 8x^2 - x + 2$.} Candidates
$\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm \frac{1}{2}, \pm \frac{1}{4}$. $t(2) = 32 - 32 - 2 + 2 = 0$,
so $(x - 2)$ is a factor and division leaves $4x^2 - 1$, a difference of
squares:
\[ t(x) = (x-2)(4x^2-1) \]
$\ans{(x-2)(2x-1)(2x+1)}$}

\problem{\textbf{Manual review --- expected reasoning} for
$u(x) = x^3 - 3x - 1$. Two points must appear.
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.2cm, itemsep=2pt, topsep=3pt]
  \item The theorem is a statement about \emph{rational} zeros only. Exhausting
  the candidate list proves $u$ has no rational zero; it says nothing about
  irrational or non-real zeros. (In fact all three zeros of this cubic are real
  and irrational.)
  \item Evidence of a real zero: $u(-1) = 1 > 0$ and $u(0) = -1 < 0$. A
  polynomial is continuous, so a sign change on $[-1, 0]$ forces a zero in
  between --- the Intermediate Value Theorem. A graph or a table showing the
  same sign change is equally acceptable.
\end{enumerate}
Accept any answer making both points. Do not accept ``the theorem must have
missed one'' with no argument. A student who adds that an odd-degree polynomial
always has at least one real zero has given a second valid argument.}
