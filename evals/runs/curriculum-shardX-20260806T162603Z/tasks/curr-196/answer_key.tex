\problem{Which of the two piano-club questions is statistical?}

\textbf{Solution:} \textbf{Instructor-judged.} Question 2 is the statistical
one. Asking how many minutes the members of the club practise each day is
answered by collecting many values, and those values differ from member to
member --- that variability is the test. Question 1 names one student and one
day, so it has a single answer and nothing varies. Full credit needs the choice
and a reason that mentions the answers varying.

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{Range of the reading times 12, 20, 15, 30, 18, 25, 22.}

\textbf{Solution:} The largest value is 30 minutes and the smallest is 12, so
the range is $30 - 12 = 18$ minutes. \ans{18}

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{Pets owned by eight students: 0, 1, 2, 1, 3, 0, 2, 1.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) \textbf{Instructor-judged.} Any question about the whole
group whose answers vary earns credit --- for example ``how many pets does a
student in this group typically own?'' or ``how spread out are the numbers of
pets?''. A question with one fixed answer, such as how many pets the group owns
altogether, does not: nothing varies in it.

(b) In order the values are $0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3$. With eight values the
median is the mean of the fourth and fifth: $(1 + 1) \div 2 = 1$
pet. \ans{1}

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{Six daily high temperatures: 14, 19, 11, 22, 17, 20.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) Reading down the row, the lowest is 11 degrees. \ans{11}

(b) The highest is 22 degrees. \ans{22}

(c) Range $= 22 - 11 = 11$ degrees. Note that the range is a single number, not
a pair --- it measures how far the values reach, not where they
are. \ans{11}

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{Ten spelling marks: 7, 8, 9, 8, 6, 10, 8, 7, 9, 8.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) The marks total 80, and $80 \div 10 = 8$. \ans{8}

(b) In order: $6, 7, 7, 8, 8, 8, 8, 9, 9, 10$. The fifth and sixth are both 8,
so the median is 8. \ans{8}

(c) \textbf{Instructor-judged.} A correct description gives a centre \emph{and}
a spread: the marks cluster at 8, where the mean and the median agree, and every
value lies between 6 and 10, so the set is tightly grouped with nothing far from
the middle. Full credit needs the typical value and a statement about how spread
out the values are.

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{Twelve plant heights in order: 2, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10, 11, 13, 15, 17, 19,
21.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) The lower half is $2, 4, 5, 7, 8, 10$, and its median is
$(5 + 7) \div 2 = 6$ centimetres. \ans{6}

(b) The upper half is $11, 13, 15, 17, 19, 21$, and its median is
$(15 + 17) \div 2 = 16$ centimetres. \ans{16}

(c) Interquartile range $= 16 - 6 = 10$ centimetres. This is the width of the
middle half of the data, which is why one very tall plant would not change
it. \ans{10}

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{Ages in one family: 4, 9, 12, 38, 41.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) \textbf{Instructor-judged.} No --- as it stands it is not
a statistical question. Asking how old each person in this one family is has a
fixed list of answers; there is nothing to collect and nothing that varies from
one asking to the next. Full credit also goes to a response that rewrites it as
a statistical question, such as asking about the ages in many families. Answering
``no'' with no reason does not earn credit.

(b) Range $= 41 - 4 = 37$ years. \ans{37}

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{Owls 20, 21, 22, 23, 24 and Robins 10, 16, 22, 28, 34; both mean 22.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) Owls: $24 - 20 = 4$ points. \ans{4}

(b) Robins: $34 - 10 = 24$ points. \ans{24}

(c) \textbf{Instructor-judged.} The Owls are the more consistent team. Both
teams average 22 points, so the mean alone says the two are identical; the
ranges say otherwise --- the Owls' scores lie inside a 4-point band while the
Robins' spread across 24 points. Full credit needs the comparison of the two
ranges, not just the observation that the means are equal.

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{Library borrowers: Mon 4, Tue 7, Wed 3, Thu 9, Fri 5.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) The largest count is 9, on Thursday. \ans{\text{Thu}}

(b) $4 + 7 + 3 + 9 + 5 = 28$ borrowers in the week. \ans{28}

(c) The counts run from 3 up to 9, so the range is $9 - 3 = 6$
borrowers. \ans{6}

\vspace{0.25cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.25cm}

\problem{Nine waiting times: 1, 2, 2, 3, 4, 4, 5, 6, 45 minutes.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) The times total 72 minutes, and $72 \div 9 = 8$
minutes. \ans{8}

(b) The values are already in order and there are nine of them, so the median is
the fifth: 4 minutes. \ans{4}

(c) Range $= 45 - 1 = 44$ minutes. \ans{44}

(d) \textbf{Instructor-judged.} The median describes a typical wait better. One
person waited 45 minutes, and that single value pulls the mean up to 8 minutes
--- longer than eight of the nine waits actually recorded --- while leaving the
median at 4 minutes, where the bulk of the data sits. Full credit needs the
effect of the one extreme value on the mean and a reasoned choice between the
two measures.
