\problem{\textbf{Mia's shells: a full five-frame and $3$ more.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The frame is full, so its count is already known --- there is
nothing to gain by recounting it, and recounting is where children lose track.

\textbf{Step 2.} Start at five and touch the loose shells one at a time:
``six, seven, eight''. That is $5+3$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The last number said is the answer: $\ans{8}$ shells.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $3$ --- the child restarted at one on the loose
shells instead of counting on from the frame.}

\problem{\textbf{A full ten-frame and $4$ loose buttons.}

\textbf{Step 1.} A full ten-frame is $10$; that is the whole point of the
frame, so the count starts there.

\textbf{Step 2.} Count on for the loose buttons: ``eleven, twelve, thirteen,
fourteen''. That is $10+4$.

\textbf{Step 3.} There are $\ans{14}$ buttons.

\textit{Teaching note:} fourteen is ``ten and four more''. Hearing it that way
is the first step toward place value.}

\problem{\textbf{Mia's stickers against Ben's.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Two sets cannot be compared until each one has a single
number, so finish both counts before looking at the signs.

\textbf{Step 2.} Mia: five, then count on ``six, seven, eight'' --- that is
$5+3=8$. Ben: four, then ``five, six'' --- that is $4+2=6$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Eight is more than six, so the true sentence is
Mia's stickers $\ans{>}$ Ben's stickers.

\textit{Listen for:} ``Ben has more because his stickers are further apart.''
Only the count decides; spacing does not.}

\problem{\textbf{How many more blocks does Kip have than Sam?}

\textbf{Step 1.} ``How many more'' is a comparison, and counting on is how you
compare without subtracting: start at the smaller number and walk up to the
bigger one.

\textbf{Step 2.} Start at six: ``seven, eight, nine''. Three number words were
said before reaching nine.

\textbf{Step 3.} Kip has $\ans{3}$ more blocks.

\textit{Listen for:} counting ``six, seven, eight, nine'' and answering four.
The starting number is where you begin, not one of the extras.}

\problem{\textbf{Jar A against Jar B.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Both jars begin with a full ten-frame, so the tens are equal
and only the loose beads can decide the comparison --- but each total still has
to be counted on.

\textbf{Step 2.} Jar A: ten, then ``eleven, twelve'' --- that is $10+2=12$.
Jar B: ten, then ``eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, fifteen'' --- that is
$10+5=15$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Twelve is less than fifteen, so Jar A $\ans{<}$ Jar B.

\textit{Teaching note:} this is a preview of comparing teen numbers by their
ones digit, because the tens already match.}

\problem{\textbf{How many more leaves does Theo have than Rosa?}

\textbf{Step 1.} Again start at the smaller count and walk up --- and this walk
crosses ten, which is the part that trips children up.

\textbf{Step 2.} Start at eight: ``nine, ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen''. Five
number words were said.

\textbf{Step 3.} Theo has $\ans{5}$ more leaves.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $6$ --- eight was counted as one of the words.}

\problem{\textbf{Ada's hearts against Leo's.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Leo's first row is longer than Ada's, so this problem can only
be settled by two finished counts, never by a glance.

\textbf{Step 2.} Ada: six, then count on seven more --- ``seven, \ldots{},
thirteen'' --- so $6+7=13$. Leo: nine, then ``ten, eleven, twelve'' --- so
$9+3=12$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Thirteen is more than twelve, so
Ada's hearts $\ans{>}$ Leo's hearts.

\textit{Listen for:} ``Leo, because his row is longest.'' A longer row is a
guess; the count is the evidence.}

\problem{\textbf{Box 1 against Box 2.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The two sets are grouped differently ($8$ and $4$ against
$10$ and $2$), so nothing can be decided until both are counted on.

\textbf{Step 2.} Box 1: eight, then ``nine, ten, eleven, twelve'' --- that is
$8+4=12$. Box 2: ten, then ``eleven, twelve'' --- that is $10+2=12$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The two counts are the same number, so
Box 1 $\ans{=}$ Box 2.

\textit{Teaching note:} sets that look different can be equal. Being willing to
write the equals sign here is real understanding, not a lucky guess.}

\problem{\textbf{How many more apples does Gus have than Ivy?}

\textbf{Step 1.} Start at the smaller count and count on to the bigger one; the
number of words said is the difference.

\textbf{Step 2.} Start at nine: ``ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen,
fifteen''. Six number words were said.

\textbf{Step 3.} Gus has $\ans{6}$ more apples.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $7$ --- nine was counted as one of the extras.}

\problem{\textbf{Challenge: Tray A against Tray B.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Tray A starts from a full frame, which makes it look like the
winner before anything is counted. That impression is exactly what has to be
tested.

\textbf{Step 2.} Tray A: ten, then ``eleven, twelve, thirteen'' --- that is
$10+3=13$. Tray B: nine, then ``ten, eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen'' ---
that is $9+5=14$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Thirteen is less than fourteen, so
Tray A $\ans{<}$ Tray B.

\textit{Teaching note:} the tray with the fuller frame is the smaller set. This
is the strongest evidence on the page that counting on beats looking.}
