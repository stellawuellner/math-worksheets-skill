\textbf{Graph A, as built.} The blue curve is $y = x + 2$ on $[-2, 1)$,
$y = 6 - 2x$ on $(1, 4)$, and $y = \dfrac{2}{x-4}$ on $(4, 6]$, with
$f(1) = 1$ marked by the solid dot. Every graph answer below is the limit of the
branch the student is reading.

\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Graph A: (a) $\lim_{x\to1^-} f(x)$, (b) $\lim_{x\to1^+} f(x)$.}

\textbf{Solution:} To the left of $1$ the graph is the line $y = x + 2$, which
climbs to the open circle at height $1 + 2$, so the left-hand limit is
\ans{3}

To the right of $1$ the graph is $y = 6 - 2x$, starting at the open circle at
height $6 - 2(1)$, so the right-hand limit is \ans{4}

The solid dot at $(1,1)$ plays no part in either limit --- a limit never reads
the value at the point.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Graph A: $\lim_{x\to3} f(x)$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Near $x = 3$ the graph is the single unbroken line
$y = 6 - 2x$, with no hole or jump, so the limit is just the height there:
$6 - 2(3)$. \ans{0}

This is the easy case worth naming: when a graph is unbroken at $x = c$, the
limit equals the value.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Table for $g(x) = \dfrac{x^3-27}{x-3}$ at $2.9,\ 2.99,\ 3.01,\ 3.1$
gives $26.11,\ 26.9101,\ 27.0901,\ 27.91$. Predict and confirm
$\lim_{x\to3} g(x)$.}

\textbf{Solution:} The two sides close in on $27$ from below and above, so the
table predicts $27$. Confirm with the difference of cubes,
$a^3 - b^3 = (a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2)$:
\[
  \frac{x^3-27}{x-3} = \frac{(x-3)\left(x^2+3x+9\right)}{x-3} = x^2+3x+9
  \qquad (x \neq 3).
\]
Substituting $x = 3$ gives $9 + 9 + 9$. \ans{27}

Notice the table never actually reaches $27$; the algebra is what proves it.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to0}\frac{\sqrt{x+16}-4}{x}$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Substitution gives $\frac{4-4}{0} = \frac{0}{0}$, so multiply
by the conjugate $\sqrt{x+16}+4$:
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\sqrt{x+16}-4}{x}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x+16}+4}{\sqrt{x+16}+4}
    &= \frac{(x+16)-16}{x\left(\sqrt{x+16}+4\right)}
     = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x+16}+4} \qquad (x \neq 0).
\end{align*}
At $x = 0$ that is $\frac{1}{4+4}$. \ans{\tfrac{1}{8}}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Graph A: does $\lim_{x\to1} f(x)$ exist? What is $f(1)$, and how does
it compare?}

\textbf{Solution --- instructor-judged, no machine check.} The required
conclusion is that $\lim_{x\to1} f(x)$ \textbf{does not exist}, because the
graph approaches $3$ from the left and $4$ from the right and those two numbers
disagree. The solid dot gives $f(1) = 1$, a third number different from both:
the graph has a jump at $x = 1$ \emph{and} a defined value that sits on neither
branch. Full credit requires the non-existence with its reason plus the value
$f(1) = 1$; award partial credit for either half alone. This problem is the one
place on the sheet where the three quantities
$\lim_{x\to1^-} f$, $\lim_{x\to1^+} f$ and $f(1)$ are all different, which is
worth saying out loud when you hand it back.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Table for $q(x) = \dfrac{|x-5|}{x-5}$ at $4.9,\ 4.99,\ 5.01,\ 5.1$
gives $-1,\ -1,\ 1,\ 1$. Find (a) $\lim_{x\to5^-} q(x)$, (b) $\lim_{x\to5^+} q(x)$.}

\textbf{Solution:} The table is not approaching a value here --- it is flat at
$-1$ on one side and flat at $1$ on the other, which is what a jump looks like
numerically. The algebra confirms it: for $x < 5$, $|x-5| = -(x-5)$ so
$q(x) = -1$ identically, giving \ans{-1}

For $x > 5$, $|x-5| = x-5$ so $q(x) = 1$ identically, giving \ans{1}

Since the one-sided values differ, $\lim_{x\to5} q(x)$ does not exist.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to2}\frac{x^3-8}{x^2-4}$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Both parts vanish at $x = 2$. Factor with difference of
cubes on top and difference of squares below:
\[
  \frac{x^3-8}{x^2-4} = \frac{(x-2)\left(x^2+2x+4\right)}{(x-2)(x+2)}
  = \frac{x^2+2x+4}{x+2} \qquad (x \neq 2).
\]
At $x = 2$: $\frac{4+4+4}{4} = \frac{12}{4}$. \ans{3}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Graph A: (a) $\lim_{x\to4^-} f(x)$, (b) $\lim_{x\to4^+} f(x)$.}

\textbf{Solution:} To the left of $4$ the graph is $y = 6-2x$, falling to the
open circle at height $6 - 2(4)$, so \ans{-2}

To the right of $4$ the graph is $y = \frac{2}{x-4}$. As $x \to 4^+$ the
denominator is a small positive and the numerator is $2$, so the branch climbs
the asymptote without bound: \ans{+\infty}

One side is finite and the other is infinite, so $\lim_{x\to4} f(x)$ does not
exist --- and saying ``$+\infty$'' alone would be wrong, because the left side
is nothing of the kind.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Evaluate $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to4}\frac{\sqrt{x+5}-3}{x-4}$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Substitution gives $\frac{3-3}{0} = \frac{0}{0}$. Multiply by
the conjugate $\sqrt{x+5}+3$:
\begin{align*}
  \frac{\sqrt{x+5}-3}{x-4}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x+5}+3}{\sqrt{x+5}+3}
    &= \frac{(x+5)-9}{(x-4)\left(\sqrt{x+5}+3\right)}
     = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x+5}+3} \qquad (x \neq 4).
\end{align*}
At $x = 4$ that is $\frac{1}{3+3}$. \ans{\tfrac{1}{6}}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Synthesis. $r(x) = \dfrac{x^2-9}{|x-3|}$, table at $2.9,\ 2.99,\ 3.01,\
3.1$ giving $-5.9,\ -5.99,\ 6.01,\ 6.1$. (a) and (b) the one-sided limits,
(c) sketch and conclude.}

\textbf{Solution (a) and (b):} Split the absolute value.

For $x < 3$: $|x-3| = -(x-3)$, so
$r(x) = \dfrac{(x-3)(x+3)}{-(x-3)} = -(x+3)$, which tends to $-(3+3)$ as
$x \to 3^-$. \ans{-6}

For $x > 3$: $|x-3| = x-3$, so $r(x) = \dfrac{(x-3)(x+3)}{x-3} = x+3$, which
tends to $3+3$ as $x \to 3^+$. \ans{6}

Both agree with the table, which was heading for $-5.99$ and $6.01$ from the two
sides.

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged, no machine check.} The sketch must
show two separate branches: a line rising from the left to an \emph{open circle}
at height $-6$, and a line coming from the right down to an \emph{open circle}
at height $6$, with nothing joining them across $x = 3$. The written conclusion
must be that $\lim_{x\to3} r(x)$ does not exist, because the one-sided limits
$-6$ and $6$ disagree. Full credit needs both open circles at the right heights
and the non-existence statement. A single connected curve through $x = 3$ earns
nothing --- it contradicts the table the student has just read.
