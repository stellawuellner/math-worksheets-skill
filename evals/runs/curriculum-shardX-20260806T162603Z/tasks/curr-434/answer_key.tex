\problem{A student converts $(-3, 3)$ to polar form, enters
$\arctan\!\left(\frac{3}{-3}\right)$ and writes $\theta \approx -0.79$.
\ (a) Correct $\theta$ in $[0, 2\pi)$. \ (b) Name the error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $\arctan\!\left(\dfrac{3}{-3}\right) = \arctan(-1) = -\dfrac{\pi}{4}
\approx -0.79$. The point has $x < 0$, so the true direction is half a turn away:
$-\dfrac{\pi}{4} + \pi$. \ans{\theta = \tfrac{3\pi}{4}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The arctangent function returns values only
in $\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}, \frac{\pi}{2}\right)$, so it can never report a second-quadrant
direction. With $x$ negative and $y$ positive the point lies in the second quadrant, and $\pi$
must be added to the calculator's answer. Full credit needs both the range limitation and the
quadrant of the point.

\problem{A student converts $(-1, -\sqrt{3})$ the same way and reports
$\theta \approx 1.05$. \ (a) Correct $\theta$. \ (b) Name the error and the role of the two
negative signs.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $\dfrac{-\sqrt{3}}{-1} = \sqrt{3}$, and
$\arctan(\sqrt{3}) = \dfrac{\pi}{3} \approx 1.05$. Since $x < 0$, add $\pi$:
$\dfrac{\pi}{3} + \pi$. \ans{\theta = \tfrac{4\pi}{3}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad Both coordinates are negative, so the point
is in the third quadrant. The two minus signs cancel inside the quotient $y/x$, so the ratio
looks exactly like a first-quadrant point's ratio and the arctangent returns a first-quadrant
angle. The sign information has to be restored by adding $\pi$. Full credit requires naming
the cancellation as the cause.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{A student says $x = 2\cos t$, $y = -2\sin t$ traces $x^2 + y^2 = 4$ counterclockwise
from $(2, 0)$. \ (a) Find the point at $t = \pi/2$. \ (b) Name the error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $x = 2\cos\dfrac{\pi}{2} = 2(0)$, so \ans{x = 0}

and $y = -2\sin\dfrac{\pi}{2} = -2(1)$, so \ans{y = -2}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad At $t = 0$ the curve is at $(2, 0)$ and a
quarter turn later it is at $(0, -2)$ --- it has moved \emph{down}, so the motion is clockwise.
The rectangular equation $x^2+y^2=4$ is correct but carries no orientation; the minus sign on
the sine is what reverses the direction. Full credit needs the clockwise conclusion supported
by the two sampled points.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{A student writes: for $x = t + 1$, $y = t^2$, the curve is traced right to left as
$t$ increases. \ (a) Find $x$ at $t = -2$ and $t = 1$. \ (b) Name the error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad At $t = -2$: \ $x = -2 + 1$, so \ans{x = -1}

At $t = 1$: \ $x = 1 + 1$, so \ans{x = 2}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad $x = t + 1$ is increasing in $t$, and the two
samples confirm it: $x$ moves from $-1$ up to $2$ as $t$ runs from $-2$ to $1$. The curve is
therefore traced left to right, so the claim reverses the orientation. Full credit for any
argument that shows $x$ increasing with $t$.

\problem{For $x = \sqrt{t}$, $y = t - 3$, $t \ge 0$, a student gets $y = x^2 - 3$ and draws the
entire parabola. \ (a) Find $y$ when $x = 2$. \ (b) Name the error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $y = 2^2 - 3 = 4 - 3$. \ans{y = 1}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The algebra is right but the domain is not.
$x = \sqrt{t}$ is never negative, so the parametric curve consists only of the points with
$x \ge 0$ --- the right half of the parabola. The entire left branch the student drew is not on
the curve. Full credit requires the restriction $x \ge 0$ and the identification of the left
half as the extra piece.

\problem{A student converts $\left(-4, \frac{\pi}{3}\right)$ to rectangular form as $x = 2$,
first quadrant. \ (a) Find the correct coordinates. \ (b) Name the error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Substitute the signed $r$:
$x = (-4)\cos\dfrac{\pi}{3} = (-4)\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right)$, so \ans{x = -2}

and $y = (-4)\sin\dfrac{\pi}{3} = (-4)\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right)$, so \ans{y = -2\sqrt{3}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The student used $|r| = 4$ and dropped the
minus sign. A negative first coordinate reverses the ray, so the point lies half a turn from
the $\pi/3$ direction --- in the \emph{third} quadrant, at $(-2, -2\sqrt{3})$, not the first.
Full credit needs the dropped sign named and the third quadrant identified.

\problem{A student claims $r = 4\cos(2\theta)$ never has $r < 0$. \ (a) Find every $\theta$ in
$[0, 2\pi)$ with $r = 0$. \ (b) Explain what happens between the first two of them.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $4\cos(2\theta) = 0$ when $2\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{2}, \dfrac{3\pi}{2},
\dfrac{5\pi}{2}, \dfrac{7\pi}{2}$, i.e.
\[ \boxed{\theta = \tfrac{\pi}{4}, \ \tfrac{3\pi}{4}, \ \tfrac{5\pi}{4}, \ \tfrac{7\pi}{4}} \]

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad Between $\theta = \pi/4$ and $\theta = 3\pi/4$
the cosine of $2\theta$ is negative, so $r$ is negative there. Those points are plotted half a
turn from the direction the angle names, which is why the petal generated by second-quadrant
angles actually appears in the fourth quadrant. That reversal is exactly how a four-petal rose
fills all four quadrants. Full credit requires "$r$ is negative on that interval" plus the
half-turn relocation.

\problem{For $x = 2 + 3\cos t$, $y = -1 + 3\sin t$, a student writes
$(x-2)^2 + (y+1)^2 = 3$. \ (a) Find the point at $t = 0$. \ (b) Show it fails and correct the
equation.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $x = 2 + 3\cos 0 = 2 + 3$, so \ans{x = 5}

and $y = -1 + 3\sin 0 = -1 + 0$, so \ans{y = -1}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad Substituting $(5, -1)$ into the student's
equation gives $(5-2)^2 + (-1+1)^2 = 9$, not $3$ --- the point on the curve does not satisfy it.
The identity $\cos^2 t + \sin^2 t = 1$ gives $(x-2)^2 + (y+1)^2 = 3^2$, so the right-hand side
must be the square of the radius:
\[ \boxed{(x-2)^2 + (y+1)^2 = 9} \]
Full credit needs the failed substitution and the corrected right-hand side.
