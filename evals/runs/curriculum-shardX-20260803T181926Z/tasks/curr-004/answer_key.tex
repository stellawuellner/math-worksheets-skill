\problem{\textbf{Missing number in} $5 \; 6 \; \underline{\hspace{0.5cm}} \; 8 \; 9$.

\textbf{Solution:} Start at the number just before the blank and say the next
one: after $6$ comes $7$. Checking from the other side gives the same number,
because $7$ is one less than $8$. \ans{7}

\noindent\textit{How to coach this:} ask the child to point at each numeral and
say it aloud. The blank is filled by counting, not by guessing which numeral
looks right.}

\problem{\textbf{Two missing numbers in} $11 \; 12 \; \underline{\hspace{0.5cm}} \; 14 \; \underline{\hspace{0.5cm}} \; 16$.

\textbf{Solution:} After $12$ comes \ans{13}

and after $14$ comes \ans{15}

\noindent The teen numbers are where most children slow down, because the word
does not say the tens first. Counting aloud from $11$ all the way to $16$ is the
repair, not reading the numerals silently.}

\problem{\textbf{Which is greater,} $21$ \textbf{or} $12$?

\textbf{Solution:} $21$ comes later when you count, so it is the greater number.
\ans{21 > 12}

\noindent\textit{Why this one is here:} $21$ and $12$ use the same two digits in
a different order, and a child who is matching shapes rather than counting will
often pick $12$. Counting up out loud settles it: $12$ is said long before $21$.}

\problem{\textbf{Find the mistake:} Ben said $6 \; 7 \; 9 \; 10 \; 11$.

\textbf{Solution:} Ben jumped from $7$ to $9$. The number that belongs between
them is one more than $7$. \ans{8}

The count said correctly is $6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11$.

\noindent\textit{Common wrong answer:} a child who is skip-counting by twos
without noticing will accept $9$ as the number after $7$. Ask them to touch one
block for each number word; the missed block is easy to see and hard to argue
with.}

\problem{\textbf{Find the mistake:} Mia's cards read $13 \; 14 \; 16 \; 15 \; 17$.

\textbf{Solution:} Counting up from $13$ gives $13$, then $14$, then $15$ ---
but the third card says $16$. The cards for $15$ and $16$ traded places. Put
them back:
\[ \boxed{13 \quad 14 \quad 15 \quad 16 \quad 17} \]
The number that really follows $14$ is \ans{15}

\noindent\textit{Common wrong answer:} $16$ right after $14$. This is a
misplaced number rather than a missing one --- every number is present, so a
child who only checks "is anything missing?" will say the row is fine. The test
that works is counting the row aloud and stopping at the first word that does
not match.}

\problem{\textbf{Show how you know:} which number is missing in $17 \; 18 \; \underline{\hspace{0.5cm}} \; 20$?
\textit{(Open response --- flagged for manual review.)}

\textbf{Expected answer:} $19$, with a drawing or a spoken explanation.

\noindent\textbf{Model explanation a child might give:} "I started at $18$ and
counted one more, and that is $19$. I drew a number line and hopped one time."
A drawing of $19$ counters, or a number line with a hop from $18$ to $19$, is an
equally good answer.

\noindent\textit{What to look for when you check it:} the child should count
forward one number at a time starting from a number before the gap, rather than
guessing. The strongest sign of understanding is being able to say that the
missing number is one more than the number before it --- and, if you ask, one
less than the number after it.}
