\problem{\textbf{Factor $x^2 - 49$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Two terms with a minus sign between them: test the difference
of squares. $x^2 = (x)^2$ and $49 = (7)^2$, so $A = x$ and $B = 7$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $A^2 - B^2 = (A - B)(A + B)$ gives $(x - 7)(x + 7)$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Check by expanding: the two middle terms $+7x$ and $-7x$
cancel, leaving $x^2 - 49$ $\checkmark$ \ans{(x-7)(x+7)}}

\problem{\textbf{Factor $x^2 + 10x + 25$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Three terms, first and last both squares: $x^2 = (x)^2$,
$25 = (5)^2$. Test the perfect-square pattern.

\textbf{Step 2.} The pattern needs a middle term of $2 \cdot x \cdot 5 = 10x$,
and that is exactly what is there.

\textbf{Step 3.} Because the middle sign is positive, the factor is a sum:
$(x + 5)^2$. \ans{(x+5)^2}}

\problem{\textbf{Factor $9x^2 - 16$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} $9x^2$ is a square as long as the coefficient is: $9x^2 =
(3x)^2$, and $16 = (4)^2$, so $A = 3x$ and $B = 4$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $(3x)^2 - (4)^2 = (3x - 4)(3x + 4)$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Expanding gives $9x^2 + 12x - 12x - 16$, and the middle terms
cancel $\checkmark$ \ans{(3x-4)(3x+4)}}

\problem{\textbf{Factor $4x^2 - 12x + 9$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} $4x^2 = (2x)^2$ and $9 = (3)^2$, so if this is a perfect square
the roots are $A = 2x$ and $B = 3$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Test the middle term: $2AB = 2 \cdot 2x \cdot 3 = 12x$, and the
trinomial has $-12x$. The sizes match and the sign is negative, so the factor is
a difference.

\textbf{Step 3.} $4x^2 - 12x + 9 = (2x - 3)^2$. \ans{(2x-3)^2}}

\problem{\textbf{Patio of side $x$ with a $4$ by $4$ hot tub removed.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The paved region is the big square minus the small square:
$x^2 - 16$, which is $(x)^2 - (4)^2$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Difference of squares: $(x - 4)(x + 4)$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The factors are lengths in metres, and they are meaningful: cut
the paved region into two rectangles and rearrange, and it measures $x - 4$ by
$x + 4$. The area is unchanged, which is the geometric reason the identity is
true. \ans{(x-4)(x+4)}}

\problem{\textbf{Factor $2x^2 - 50$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} $2x^2$ is not a perfect square, so the difference-of-squares
pattern does not apply yet. Both terms share a factor of $2$, so take it out
first.

\textbf{Step 2.} $2x^2 - 50 = 2(x^2 - 25)$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Now the bracket is a difference of squares:
$x^2 - 25 = (x - 5)(x + 5)$, so the complete factorization is
$2(x - 5)(x + 5)$. \ans{2(x-5)(x+5)}}

\problem{\textbf{Square mosaic of area $x^2 + 14x + 49$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} A square's area is (side)$^2$, so factoring the area as a
perfect square is what produces the side length.

\textbf{Step 2.} $x^2 = (x)^2$, $49 = (7)^2$, and the middle term must be
$2 \cdot x \cdot 7 = 14x$ --- it is.

\textbf{Step 3.} The area is $(x + 7)^2$, so the side of the mosaic measures
$x + 7$ centimetres. \ans{(x+7)^2}}

\problem{\textbf{Error analysis: is $x^2 - 9 = (x - 3)^2$?}

\textbf{Step 1.} Expand the student's answer:
$(x - 3)^2 = x^2 - 3x - 3x + 9 = x^2 - 6x + 9$.

\textbf{Step 2.} That is not $x^2 - 9$: it has an extra $-6x$ and the constant
has the wrong sign. So the student's factorization is wrong.

\textbf{Step 3.} $x^2 - 9$ has two terms, not three, so the pattern it fits is
the difference of squares: $x^2 - 9 = (x - 3)(x + 3)$.

\textit{Manual review:} the written sentence is an open response. Accept any
wording saying the student used the perfect-square pattern on a two-term
expression, or that squaring a binomial produces a middle term the original
never had. \ans{x^2 - 6x + 9 \quad \text{and} \quad (x-3)(x+3)}}

\problem{\textbf{Factor $18x^2 - 32$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Neither $18x^2$ nor $32$ is a perfect square, but both are even:
the greatest common factor is $2$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $18x^2 - 32 = 2(9x^2 - 16)$, and now the bracket is a
difference of squares with $A = 3x$, $B = 4$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $2(3x - 4)(3x + 4)$. Expanding back gives $2(9x^2 - 16) =
18x^2 - 32$ $\checkmark$ \ans{2(3x-4)(3x+4)}}

\problem{\textbf{Factor $x^4 - 81$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} $x^4 = (x^2)^2$ and $81 = (9)^2$, so this is a difference of
squares with $A = x^2$ and $B = 9$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $x^4 - 81 = (x^2 - 9)(x^2 + 9)$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The first bracket is itself a difference of squares:
$x^2 - 9 = (x - 3)(x + 3)$. The second is a \emph{sum} of squares, which does
not factor over the integers, so the factorization stops here.
\ans{(x-3)(x+3)(x^2+9)}}
