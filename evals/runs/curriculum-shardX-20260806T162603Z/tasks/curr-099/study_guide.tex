\sstitleblock{Finding and Fixing Data and Shape Mistakes}

\skillheading{Skill 1 --- Checking a Scale}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Rule:} every step on a scale must go up by the \emph{same} amount, and the gaps between the
ticks must be the \emph{same width}.\par
To find a missing label: (start value) $+$ (how many steps across) $\times$ (step size).\par
Two things to check before reading any plot: are the labels evenly spaced in value, and are the
ticks evenly spaced on the paper?
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad A scale reads $2$, $4$, $6$, $9$. Which label is wrong, and what should it be?
\step{The steps between labels should all match, so I find the step size from the labels I trust and then rebuild the one that breaks the pattern.}
\step{From $2$ to $4$ to $6$ the step is $2$, so the fourth label is $2 + 3 \times 2$, not $9$.}

the correct label is $\ans{8}$
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad A scale reads $5$, $10$, $16$. The step size is $5$. What should the third
label be?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{15}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{Skill 2 --- Counting the Right Thing}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{The most common data mistake:} counting the numbers on the scale instead of the marks above
them.\par
Numbers on the scale $=$ the values. \quad $\times$ marks $=$ the things measured.\par
``How many were measured?'' $\Rightarrow$ add the column heights. \quad
``What is the difference between greatest and least?'' $\Rightarrow$ subtract two scale values.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad A plot has $3$ marks above $1$, $5$ above $2$ and $2$ above $3$. How many
things were measured?
\step{Each thing measured put down exactly one mark, so the answer comes from the marks, not from the labels underneath them.}
\step{Add the three column heights: $3 + 5 + 2$.}

things measured $= \ans{10}$
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad A plot has $4$ marks above $1$, $2$ above $2$ and $3$ above $3$. How many
things were measured?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{9}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{Skill 3 --- Checking a Shape Against the Attribute}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Rule:} a shape belongs to a group only if it has \emph{that group's attribute}. Having some
other attribute does not get it in.\par
Four equal sides is not the same as four square corners. \quad Four sides is not the same as two
pairs of parallel sides.\par
So: read what the group asks for, then test that one thing on the shape.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad Someone says a rhombus is a square because both have four equal sides. How
many square corners does the rhombus have?
\step{The claim is about being a square, and a square is defined by corners as well as sides, so I test the corners rather than recount the sides.}
\step{Holding a card corner against each corner of the rhombus, not one of them fits: two are sharp and two are blunt.}

square corners on a rhombus $= \ans{0}$, so it is not a square
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad Someone puts a trapezoid in the ``two pairs of parallel sides'' group. How many
pairs does a trapezoid really have?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{1}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} finding the right answer is only half of a find-and-fix problem. The other
half is saying \emph{what} went wrong --- ``they counted labels instead of marks,'' ``they added when
the question said difference.'' Naming the mistake is what stops you making it.
\end{watchoutbox}
