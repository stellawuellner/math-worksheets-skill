\sstitleblock{Multiplying by a One-Digit Number}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{The big idea.} Multiplying by one digit means multiplying that digit
by \emph{every} place of the other number --- ones, tens, hundreds, thousands
--- and then putting the places back together.\\[3pt]
\textbf{The regrouping rule:} multiply first, \emph{then} add the digit you
carried in. If the result is ten or more, write its ones digit and carry the
tens digit into the next column.\\[3pt]
\textbf{A zero is a place, not a gap.} A zero digit still gets multiplied, and
it still receives any digit carried into it.
\end{formulabox}

\skillheading{1. Multiply a big number by one digit}

\begin{formulabox}
Work right to left: ones, tens, hundreds, thousands. Write the carry above the
next column so you cannot forget it, and cross it off once you have added it.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Multiply $1362 \times 4$.
\step{Every place of $1362$ has to be multiplied by the same factor, so work
right to left and carry when a column reaches ten.}
\step{Ones: $2 \times 4 = 8$. Tens: $6 \times 4 = 24$, write $4$ and carry $2$.
Hundreds: $3 \times 4 = 12$, plus the carried $2$ is $14$ --- write $4$, carry
$1$. Thousands: $1 \times 4 = 4$, plus $1$ is $5$.}

The product is $\ans{5448}$.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Multiply $2438 \times 3$.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{7314}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{2. Estimate a product by rounding first}

\begin{formulabox}
Round the big factor to a friendly place, keep the one-digit factor as it is,
then multiply. An estimate tells you what size the real answer should be, so a
product far from it is a signal to check your work.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Estimate $3847 \times 6$, then find the exact product.
\step{The question asks how big the answer should be, so round the four-digit
factor to a place that is easy to multiply and leave the $6$ alone.}
\step{To the nearest thousand $3847$ becomes $4000$, and $4000 \times 6$ is
$24$ thousands.}

Estimate: $\ans{24000}$. \quad Exact: $\ans{23082}$, only a little below it.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Estimate $5216 \times 7$ by rounding to the nearest thousand,
then find the exact product.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: estimate $\ans{35000}$, exact $\ans{36512}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{3. Multiply to answer a story question}

\begin{formulabox}
Equal groups mean multiply: \emph{how many groups} times \emph{how many in
each group}. Read the last sentence again before you answer --- some questions
ask for the total, and some ask for what is left after the total.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} A theater has $4$ sections, and each section holds $1145$
seats. How many seats in all?
\step{Four sections of the same size are four equal groups, and equal groups
are what multiplication counts.}
\step{Multiply the seats in one section by the number of sections:
$1145 \times 4$, which is $4$ thousands, $4$ hundreds, $8$ tens and $0$ ones.}

There are $\ans{4580}$ seats.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} A farm collects $2260$ eggs each day for $8$ days. How many
eggs is that?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{18080}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} the two mistakes that cost the most marks are adding
the carry \emph{before} multiplying instead of after, and skipping a zero
digit. Both leave a product that is far from your estimate --- which is
exactly why the estimate is worth writing down first.
\end{watchoutbox}
