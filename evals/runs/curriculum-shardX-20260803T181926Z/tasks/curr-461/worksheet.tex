\wstitleblock{Differentiating Implicit Relations}{AP Calculus AB / BC}{}

\noindent\textbf{The idea, and the bookkeeping model.} In a relation such as
$x^{2} + y^{2} = 25$ the variable $y$ is not written as a formula in $x$, but on
any piece of the curve where $y$ is a differentiable function of $x$ we may still
differentiate both sides with respect to $x$. Every $y$ carries a factor
$\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ by the chain rule. Differentiate term by term:

\begin{center}\small
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|l|}\hline
\textbf{Term} & \textbf{Derivative with respect to }$x$ & \textbf{Rule used}\\\hline
$x^{3}$ & $3x^{2}$ & power rule\\\hline
$y^{3}$ & $3y^{2}\,\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ & power rule $+$ chain rule\\\hline
$xy$ & $y + x\,\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ & product rule\\\hline
$x^{2}y$ & $2xy + x^{2}\,\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ & product rule $+$ chain rule\\\hline
a constant & $0$ & ---\\\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=0.9cm, itemsep=1pt, topsep=3pt]
  \item After differentiating, collect every term containing $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ on
        one side, factor it out, and divide. The answer normally involves
        \emph{both} $x$ and $y$ --- that is expected, not a mistake.
  \item A slope at a point needs both coordinates. Before using a point, check
        that it actually lies on the curve by substituting it into the original
        relation.
  \item The tangent is horizontal where the numerator of $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ is zero
        (with the denominator nonzero), and vertical where the denominator is zero
        (with the numerator nonzero).
\end{itemize}
\vspace{0.1cm}
\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.4pt}

\problem[7.5cm]{Differentiate both sides of
\[ x^{2} + y^{2} = 25 \]
with respect to $x$ and solve for $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$.

\noindent Then, on the upper half of this circle, $y = \sqrt{25 - x^{2}}$ is an
explicit function. Substitute that expression for $y$ into your implicit answer and
confirm it agrees with the derivative you would get by differentiating
$\sqrt{25 - x^{2}}$ directly. Write both forms.}

\problem[7.5cm]{For the relation
\[ xy = 12, \]
find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$. Verify that $(3, 4)$ lies on the curve, then find the slope
of the curve there.}

\problem[7.5cm]{For the relation
\[ x^{2} + xy + y^{2} = 7, \]
find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ in terms of $x$ and $y$. Verify that $(1, 2)$ lies on the
curve, then evaluate the slope there.}

\problem[8cm]{The \emph{folium of Descartes} is the curve
\[ x^{3} + y^{3} = 9xy. \]
Find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$, verify that $(2, 4)$ lies on the curve, and find the slope
of the curve at that point.}

\problem[7.5cm]{For the relation
\[ x^{2}y + y^{3} = 10, \]
find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$. Verify that $(1, 2)$ lies on the curve, then evaluate the
slope there. Note where the product rule and the chain rule each had to be used.}

\problem[8cm]{The ellipse
\[ x^{2} + 4y^{2} = 25 \]
passes through $(3, 2)$. Verify that it does, find the slope of the curve at that
point, and write an equation of the tangent line there in point-slope form.}

\problem[8.5cm]{\textbf{Horizontal tangents.} For the curve
\[ x^{2} + xy + y^{2} = 12, \]
find $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$, then find \emph{all} points on the curve where the tangent
line is horizontal.

\noindent Set the numerator of $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ equal to zero to get a relation
between $x$ and $y$, substitute that into the original equation to get an equation
in $x$ alone, and solve. Give both points, and confirm each satisfies the original
relation.}

\problem[8.5cm]{\textbf{Challenge --- second derivative.} For
\[ x^{2} + y^{2} = 25, \]
you found $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = -\dfrac{x}{y}$. Differentiate that a second time with
respect to $x$, substituting your expression for $\dfrac{dy}{dx}$ wherever it
appears, and simplify using the original relation to write
$\dfrac{d^{2}y}{dx^{2}}$ in terms of $y$ alone.

\noindent Then write the same second derivative in terms of $x$ by substituting
$y = \sqrt{25 - x^{2}}$, and say what the sign of the result tells you about the
concavity of the upper semicircle.}
