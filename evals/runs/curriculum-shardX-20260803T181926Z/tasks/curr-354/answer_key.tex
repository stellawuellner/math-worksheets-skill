\problem{\textbf{Subtract:} $(3 - 8i) - (5 - 2i)$.
The subtraction sign belongs to the whole second number, so distribute it to BOTH
parts: $-(5 - 2i) = -5 + 2i$.
Real parts: $3 - 5 = -2$. Imaginary parts: $-8i + 2i = -6i$.
The result is $\ans{-2 - 6i}$.}

\problem{\textbf{Multiply:} $(4 + 3i)(2 - 5i)$.
FOIL first: $8 - 20i + 6i - 15i^2$.
Combine the middle terms: $8 - 14i - 15i^2$.
Now use $i^2 = -1$, so $-15i^2 = +15$ --- this is the sign students lose most often.
$8 + 15 = 23$, and the answer is $\ans{23 - 14i}$.}

\problem{\textbf{Conjugate pair:} $(5 + 2i)(5 - 2i)$.
The outer and inner products are $-10i$ and $+10i$, which cancel --- that is why a
conjugate pair has no $i$ left.
What remains is $25 - 4i^2$, and $-4i^2 = +4$ because $i^2 = -1$.
$25 + 4 = 29$, so the product is $\ans{29}$.
Note the sign: the answer is $25 + 4$, not $25 - 4$.}

\problem{\textbf{Find the mistake (Nadia).}
(a) The conjugate of $7 - 3i$ is $7 + 3i$, not $7 - 3i$ again. Nadia multiplied the
number by ITSELF and then reported the answer to a different product,
$(7 - 3i)(7 + 3i) = 58$. To clear the $i$ she must multiply by $7 + 3i$; only a
conjugate PAIR makes the imaginary terms cancel.
[Manual review: any explanation naming the conjugate $7 + 3i$ earns full credit.]

(b) Squaring the same factor: $(7 - 3i)(7 - 3i) = 49 - 21i - 21i + 9i^2$.
Combine: $49 - 42i + 9i^2$, and $9i^2 = -9$.
So $49 - 9 = 40$ and the correct value is $\ans{40 - 42i}$ --- still imaginary, which
is exactly the evidence that the wrong factor was used.}

\problem{\textbf{Divide:} $\dfrac{8 + i}{2 - 3i}$.
Multiply numerator and denominator by the conjugate of the denominator, $2 + 3i$.
Numerator: $(8 + i)(2 + 3i) = 16 + 24i + 2i + 3i^2 = 16 + 26i - 3 = 13 + 26i$.
Denominator: $(2 - 3i)(2 + 3i) = 4 + 9 = 13$ (a sum, because $-9i^2 = +9$).
Divide each part by 13: $\dfrac{13 + 26i}{13} = \ans{1 + 2i}$.}

\problem{\textbf{Find the mistake (Marcus).}
(a) Marcus computed $(3 + i)(3 - i)$ as $9 - 1 = 8$, which treats $i^2$ as $+1$.
The middle terms cancel and the last term is $-i^2 = +1$, so the product is
$9 + 1$, and the correct denominator is $\ans{10}$.
[Manual review: the explanation must say the last term flips sign because $i^2 = -1$.]

(b) Numerator: $20(3 - i) = 60 - 20i$. Divide by the correct denominator 10:
$\dfrac{60 - 20i}{10} = \ans{6 - 2i}$.
Check by multiplying back: $(6 - 2i)(3 + i) = 18 + 6i - 6i - 2i^2 = 18 + 2 = 20$.}

\problem{\textbf{Solve:} $x^2 - 6x + 13 = 0$.
Discriminant: $(-6)^2 - 4(1)(13) = 36 - 52 = -16$, negative, so the two solutions are
non-real. $\sqrt{-16} = 4i$.
Quadratic formula: $x = \dfrac{6 \pm 4i}{2} = 3 \pm 2i$.
The two solutions are $\ans{x = 3 + 2i}$ and $\ans{x = 3 - 2i}$.
They are conjugates of each other: same real part, opposite signs on the imaginary
part. That always happens when a quadratic with real coefficients has a negative
discriminant.}

\problem{\textbf{All zeros of} $P(x) = x^4 - 16$.
Factor as a difference of squares twice: $x^4 - 16 = (x^2 - 4)(x^2 + 4)$, and
$x^2 - 4 = (x - 2)(x + 2)$.
From $x^2 - 4 = 0$: $\ans{x = 2}$ and $\ans{x = -2}$.
From $x^2 + 4 = 0$: $x^2 = -4$, so $x = \pm\sqrt{-4}$, giving $\ans{x = 2i}$ and
$\ans{x = -2i}$.
The sign trap is $x^2 + 4$: it does NOT factor over the reals, and its roots are
$\pm 2i$, a conjugate pair --- writing only $2i$ leaves a fourth-degree polynomial
with three zeros.}
