\sstitleblock{Solving Exponential and Logarithmic Equations}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{One sentence, two spellings:} $\log_b M = k$ and $b^k = M$ mean the
same thing. Every technique below is that swap, used in one direction or the
other.\\[3pt]
\textbf{The three laws:} $\log_b(MN) = \log_b M + \log_b N$, \
$\log_b\!\left(\frac{M}{N}\right) = \log_b M - \log_b N$, \
$\log_b(M^k) = k\log_b M$.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Change of base:} $\log_b M = \frac{\log M}{\log b}$, which is how a
calculator produces a decimal for any base.
\end{formulabox}

\skillheading{1. Unknown in the exponent}

\begin{formulabox}
If both sides can be written with the same base, match the exponents --- exact
and no calculator. If they cannot, take a logarithm of both sides and use the
power law to bring the exponent down in front.\\[3pt]
Isolate the exponential factor first: a coefficient in front is not part of the
power.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Solve $3^x = 20$ to the nearest thousandth.
\step{Twenty is not a power of three, so the bases cannot be matched --- take a
logarithm of both sides instead.}
\step{$x\log 3 = \log 20$, so $x = \dfrac{\log 20}{\log 3}$, and evaluating
gives $2.72683\ldots$}

$x \approx \ans{2.727}$
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Solve $5^x = 90$ to the nearest thousandth.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{2.796}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{2. Unknown inside a logarithm}

\begin{formulabox}
Get the equation down to one logarithm equal to a number, then rewrite it in
exponential form: $\log_b x = k$ becomes $x = b^k$.\\[3pt]
Because a logarithm needs a positive argument, test every candidate root in the
original equation --- condensing can invent roots the equation never had.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Solve $\log_4 x = 3$.
\step{One logarithm already sits alone on the left, so the exponential
rewrite finishes the problem in a single move.}
\step{$\log_4 x = 3$ means $x = 4^3$.}

$x = \ans{64}$
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Solve $\log_3 x = 4$.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{81}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{3. Rewriting with the logarithm laws}

\begin{formulabox}
Expanding turns one logarithm into several; condensing does the reverse and is
what makes an equation solvable, because only a single logarithm can be
rewritten in exponential form.\\[3pt]
Clear coefficients with the power law first, then use the product law for a
sum and the quotient law for a difference.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Expand $\log_5(25x^3)$ for $x > 0$.
\step{The argument is a product with a power inside it, so the product law
splits it and the power law then clears the exponent.}
\step{$\log_5 25 + \log_5(x^3) = \log_5 25 + 3\log_5 x$, and $\log_5 25 = 2$
because $5^2 = 25$.}

$\ans{2 + 3\log_5 x}$
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Condense $3\log_2 x + \log_2 7$ into a single logarithm.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{\log_2(7x^3)}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} $\log(M + N)$ is not $\log M + \log N$ --- the laws
apply to products and quotients inside the logarithm, never to sums. And
$\dfrac{\log 5}{\log 2}$ is not $\dfrac{5}{2}$: take the logarithms first,
divide second.
\end{watchoutbox}
