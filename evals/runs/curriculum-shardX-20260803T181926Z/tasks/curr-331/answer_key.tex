\problem{\textbf{Both pairs of opposite sides parallel.}

\textbf{Step 1. Classify.} Two pairs of parallel sides is the definition of a
\textbf{parallelogram}. Nothing marks the sides congruent and nothing marks a
right angle, so parallelogram is as specific as the marks allow --- not
rhombus, not rectangle.

\textbf{Step 2. Pick the property.} In a parallelogram opposite angles are
congruent, and $\angle A$ and $\angle C$ are opposite.

\textbf{Step 3. Solve.} $5x = 75$, so dividing by $5$ gives

\ans{x = 15}

\textbf{Step 4. Check.} $5(15) = 75$, which matches $m\angle C$ $\checkmark$

\textit{Common wrong answer:} setting $5x + 75 = 180$ (giving $x = 21$) uses the
\emph{consecutive}-angle property on a pair of \emph{opposite} angles. Ask which
two angles share a side.}

\problem{\textbf{No marks at all.}

\textbf{Step 1. Classify.} With no congruence and no parallel marks, nothing
forces any special family. The only honest name is \emph{quadrilateral}: it may
happen to be a trapezoid, but the figure does not say so, and a name has to be
forced by the given properties, not guessed from the drawing.

\textbf{Step 2. Pick the property.} One angle fact holds for every
quadrilateral: the four interior angles sum to $360^\circ$.

\textbf{Step 3. Solve.}
$(x + 20) + 2x + (3x - 10) + (2x + 30) = 360$, so $8x + 40 = 360$ and
$8x = 320$.

\ans{x = 40}

\textbf{Step 4. Check.} The four angles are $60^\circ$, $80^\circ$,
$110^\circ$ and $110^\circ$, and they total $360^\circ$ $\checkmark$

\textit{Teaching note:} no pair of these angles sums to $180^\circ$, which
confirms there are no parallel sides --- the figure really is a plain
quadrilateral.}

\problem{\textbf{Four congruent sides plus one right angle.}

\textbf{Step 1. Classify.} Four congruent sides makes it a rhombus; a rhombus
with a right angle is a \textbf{square}. (One right angle is enough: in a
parallelogram consecutive angles are supplementary, so one $90^\circ$ angle
forces all four.)

\textbf{Step 2. Pick the property.} All four sides of a square are congruent,
so the two labelled sides are equal.

\textbf{Step 3. Solve.} $3x - 5 = x + 7$, so $2x = 12$.

\ans{x = 6}

\textbf{Step 4. Check.} $3(6) - 5 = 13$ and $6 + 7 = 13$ $\checkmark$ --- every
side is $13$ units.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} ``rhombus''. The marks force the more specific
name, and the directions ask for the most specific one.}

\problem{\textbf{Diagonals that bisect each other and are congruent.}

\textbf{Step 1. Classify.} Diagonals that bisect each other force a
parallelogram. Adding \emph{congruent} diagonals forces a \textbf{rectangle} ---
that is the diagonal test for a rectangle. Nothing says the diagonals are
perpendicular, so it need not be a square.

\textbf{Step 2. Pick the property.} Congruent diagonals means $JL = KM$.

\textbf{Step 3. Solve.} $4x - 6 = x + 9$, so $3x = 15$.

\ans{x = 5}

\textbf{Step 4. Report the length.} $JL = 4(5) - 6 = 20 - 6$.

\ans{JL = 14}

\textbf{Step 5. Check.} $KM = 5 + 9 = 14$ as well $\checkmark$

\textit{Listen for:} ``the picture looks like a rectangle, so I did not need the
diagonals.'' The figure is labelled not to scale on purpose --- the reasoning
has to come from the two diagonal facts.}

\problem{\textbf{Congruent sides with perpendicular bisecting diagonals.}

\textbf{Step 1. Classify.} Four congruent sides gives a rhombus, and the
diagonals of a rhombus are exactly the ones that bisect each other at right
angles --- consistent, and no right angle is marked at any \emph{vertex}, so
\textbf{rhombus} is the most specific name.

\textbf{Step 2. Pick the property.} The right angle at $E$ puts
$\angle BAE$, $\angle ABE$ and the $90^\circ$ angle in one triangle, $ABE$, and
a triangle's angles sum to $180^\circ$.

\textbf{Step 3. Solve.}
$90 + (4x - 6) + 2x = 180$, so $6x + 84 = 180$ and $6x = 96$.

\ans{x = 16}

\textbf{Step 4. Check.} $m\angle ABE = 4(16) - 6 = 58^\circ$ and
$m\angle BAE = 2(16) = 32^\circ$; $90 + 58 + 32 = 180$ $\checkmark$

\textit{Teaching note:} the perpendicular diagonals are what turn a
quadrilateral problem into a right-triangle problem. That move --- cut the
quadrilateral into triangles with a diagonal --- is worth naming out loud.}

\problem{\textbf{Classifying $ABCD$ from its vertices.}

\textbf{Step 1. Plan.} Slopes decide parallel and perpendicular; distances
decide congruent. Compute both for two adjacent sides.

\textbf{Step 2. Slopes.} For $A(-2, 1)$ and $B(2, 3)$:
$\dfrac{3 - 1}{2 - (-2)} = \dfrac{2}{4}$, so the slope of $\overline{AB}$ is

\ans{\tfrac{1}{2}}

For $B(2, 3)$ and $C(4, -1)$: $\dfrac{-1 - 3}{4 - 2} = \dfrac{-4}{2}$, so the
slope of $\overline{BC}$ is

\ans{-2}

\textbf{Step 3. What the slopes force.} The product is
$\frac{1}{2} \cdot (-2) = -1$, so $\overline{AB} \perp \overline{BC}$: the angle
at $B$ is right. Running the same computation on the other two sides gives
slope $\frac{1}{2}$ for $\overline{DC}$ and $-2$ for $\overline{AD}$, so both
pairs of opposite sides are parallel and all four angles are right --- a
rectangle so far.

\textbf{Step 4. Lengths.}
$AB = \sqrt{4^2 + 2^2} = \sqrt{20}$, so

\ans{AB \approx 4.47}

$BC = \sqrt{2^2 + (-4)^2} = \sqrt{20}$, so

\ans{BC \approx 4.47}

\textbf{Step 5. Name it.} Adjacent sides are congruent as well as
perpendicular, so the rectangle is in fact a \textbf{square}. Perpendicular
adjacent sides give the right angles; equal adjacent sides give the equal
sides.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} stopping at ``rectangle''. The lengths are what
promote it, and the directions ask for the most specific name.}

\problem{\textbf{Is a rhombus a rectangle?} \emph{Open justification --- flagged
for manual review. The response below is a model full-credit answer.}

\textbf{Step 1. Answer.} \emph{Sometimes} --- and only in one case.

\textbf{Step 2. The separating property.} A rhombus is a parallelogram with
four congruent \emph{sides}; a rectangle is a parallelogram with four right
\emph{angles}. Neither definition mentions the other's condition, so being a
parallelogram is all they are guaranteed to share. The rhombus $WXYZ$ on the
sheet has four congruent sides and no right angle, which is a counterexample on
its own.

\textbf{Step 3. When it is true.} A quadrilateral that satisfies both
definitions --- four congruent sides \emph{and} four right angles --- is a
square, and a square is both a rhombus and a rectangle. So ``sometimes'', with
the overlap being exactly the squares.

\textbf{Step 4. The marking.} On the figure the student should add a right-angle
square at one vertex (equivalently, mark the diagonals congruent). Either
addition promotes the rhombus to a square, which is the only rhombus that is
also a rectangle.

\textit{Full credit:} the word \emph{sometimes}, a reason that names congruent
sides against right angles, and a mark that would force a right angle. Answering
``never'' is a common slip --- it forgets the square.}

\problem{\textbf{One pair congruent and parallel.} \emph{Open written argument
--- flagged for manual review. Model answer below.}

\textbf{Step 1. Set up.} Given: $\overline{QR} \cong \overline{TS}$ and
$\overline{QR} \parallel \overline{TS}$. Draw the diagonal $\overline{QS}$; it
cuts the quadrilateral into $\triangle QRS$ and $\triangle STQ$.

\textbf{Step 2. The argument.}
$\angle RQS \cong \angle TSQ$, because they are alternate interior angles on
the parallel sides $\overline{QR}$ and $\overline{TS}$ with transversal
$\overline{QS}$. Also $\overline{QS} \cong \overline{SQ}$ (reflexive), and
$\overline{QR} \cong \overline{TS}$ is given. By SAS,
$\triangle QRS \cong \triangle STQ$.

\textbf{Step 3. Finish.} CPCTC gives $\overline{RS} \cong \overline{QT}$, so
both pairs of opposite sides are congruent, and a quadrilateral with both pairs
of opposite sides congruent is a parallelogram. (The named shortcut for the
whole argument: \emph{if one pair of opposite sides is both congruent and
parallel, the quadrilateral is a parallelogram}.)

\textbf{Step 4. Rectangle?} No. Nothing in the given marks constrains any
angle, and the drawing itself shows a slanted parallelogram with no right
angle. A rectangle would additionally need a right angle, or congruent
diagonals.

\textit{Full credit:} a chain that reaches parallelogram with a reason named at
each step, plus a ``no'' on the rectangle question backed by what is missing.
Asserting the parallelogram conclusion with no reasons is the common shortfall.}
