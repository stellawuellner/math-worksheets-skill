\problem{\textbf{Slope through $(1, 2)$ and $(4, 8)$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Two points and no context, so the slope formula applies
directly --- label one point $(x_1, y_1)$ and the other $(x_2, y_2)$ and keep
that labelling for both subtractions.

\textbf{Step 2.} $m = \dfrac{8 - 2}{4 - 1} = \dfrac{6}{3}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $m = \ans{2}$: $y$ goes up $2$ for every $1$ that $x$ goes up.}

\problem{\textbf{Line B on Figure 1.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Read two points the line passes \emph{exactly} through --- a
point read between grid lines is a guess, and slope from a guess is a guess.

\textbf{Step 2.} Line B runs through the origin $(0, 0)$ and through
$(8, 8)$, so $m = \dfrac{8 - 0}{8 - 0}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $m = \ans{1}$.

\textit{Teaching note:} a slope of $1$ is the $45^\circ$ line. It is worth
naming, because students often report the slope of a diagonal as ``up and
across'', which is a description, not a number.}

\problem{\textbf{Taxi fare $C = 4d + 7$.}

\textbf{(a) Step 1.} The equation is already in $y = mx + b$ form, so the rate
of change is the coefficient of $d$ --- the fee $7$ is charged once and never
changes, so it cannot be part of a rate.

\textbf{Step 2.} Checking it against two points: at $d = 0$ the cost is $7$ and
at $d = 1$ the cost is $11$, so $m = \dfrac{11 - 7}{1 - 0}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The rate is $\ans{4}$ dollars per mile.

\textbf{(b) Step 4.} Substitute $d = 3$: $C = 4(3) + 7 = 12 + 7$.

\textbf{Step 5.} A three-mile ride costs $\ans{19}$ dollars.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $33$ --- the student computed $(4 + 7)\times 3$,
charging the flat pick-up fee once for every mile.}

\problem{\textbf{Line A on Figure 1.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Again pick two lattice points; the graph marks $(0, 2)$ and
$(4, 14)$ on the line.

\textbf{Step 2.} $m = \dfrac{14 - 2}{4 - 0} = \dfrac{12}{4}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The rate of change is $\ans{3}$.

\textit{Teaching note:} $(0, 2)$ is the $y$-intercept, so Line A is
$y = 3x + 2$. Naming the equation of a line the student has just measured is
what ties the graph form to the equation form.}

\problem{\textbf{Water in the tub.}

\textbf{(a) Step 1.} ``How many litres run in'' asks for a change in the
output, not a rate, so subtract the two litre readings and stop there.

\textbf{Step 2.} At minute $8$ there are $50$ litres and at minute $5$ there
are $32$, so the change is $50 - 32 = \ans{18}$ litres.

\textbf{(b) Step 3.} A rate is that change divided by the change in time.
Using minutes $2$ and $5$: $m = \dfrac{32 - 14}{5 - 2} = \dfrac{18}{3}$.

\textbf{Step 4.} The rate is $\ans{6}$ litres per minute.

\textit{Check it is really constant:} from minute $5$ to minute $8$ the same
calculation gives $\dfrac{18}{3} = 6$ again. Equal rates over every interval is
what makes the table linear.}

\problem{\textbf{Comparing two streaming plans.}

\textbf{(a) Step 1.} Plan B is a table, so its rate comes from two rows:
$m = \dfrac{84 - 42}{6 - 3} = \dfrac{42}{3}$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Plan B costs $\ans{14}$ dollars per hour.

\textbf{(b) Step 3.} Plan A is $y = 12x$, which is already $y = mx$, so its
rate is $12$ dollars per hour. Now both rates are in the same units and the
numbers can be compared directly.

\textbf{Step 4.} $12$ is less than $14$, so Plan A's rate $\ans{<}$ Plan B's
rate --- Plan A is the cheaper plan per hour.

\textit{Listen for:} ``Plan B is bigger because the numbers in the table are
bigger.'' The table's numbers are totals for different numbers of hours; only
the rate is comparable.}

\problem{\textbf{Line C on Figure 1.}

\textbf{Step 1.} This line falls from left to right, so expect a negative
answer before computing anything --- that expectation is the check on the sign.

\textbf{Step 2.} The line passes through $(0, 16)$ and $(8, 0)$, so
$m = \dfrac{0 - 16}{8 - 0} = \dfrac{-16}{8}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The rate of change is $\ans{-2}$.

\textbf{Step 4.} What the sign means: $y$ goes \emph{down} by $2$ for every $1$
that $x$ goes up. The steepness is $2$; the direction is the minus sign.}

\problem{\textbf{Leaking barrel $L = -5x + 40$.}

\textbf{(a) Step 1.} The equation is in $y = mx + b$ form, so the rate is the
coefficient of $x$; the $40$ is how much was in the barrel at the start.

\textbf{Step 2.} The rate of change is $\ans{-5}$ litres per minute --- the
barrel loses $5$ litres each minute.

\textbf{(b) Step 3.} Substitute $x = 6$: $L = -5(6) + 40 = -30 + 40$.

\textbf{Step 4.} After six minutes $\ans{10}$ litres are left.

\textit{Worth asking:} at what time does the barrel run dry? Setting
$-5x + 40 = 0$ gives $x = 8$ minutes, which is where this rule stops
describing the barrel.}

\problem{\textbf{A table that is not linear.}

\textbf{(a) Step 1.} A single rate only describes the whole table if it is the
same over every interval, so compute it interval by interval rather than once.

\textbf{Step 2.} From $x = 0$ to $x = 2$: $m = \dfrac{4 - 0}{2 - 0} = \ans{2}$.

\textbf{(b) Step 3.} From $x = 2$ to $x = 4$:
$m = \dfrac{12 - 4}{4 - 2} = \dfrac{8}{2} = \ans{4}$.

\textbf{Step 4.} The two rates are different, so the points do not lie on one
straight line: between the first pair the graph climbs $2$ per step, and
between the second pair it climbs $4$ per step. A linear relationship has one
rate everywhere.

\textit{Grading note:} accept any wording that says the rate changed. Do not
accept ``it is not linear because the numbers are not even'' --- evenness is
not the test.}

\problem{\textbf{Mara and Ben.}

\textbf{(a) Step 1.} Mara's data is a table, so her speed is the change in
metres over the change in seconds: $m = \dfrac{25 - 10}{5 - 2} = \dfrac{15}{3}$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Mara runs at $\ans{5}$ metres per second.

\textbf{(b) Step 3.} Ben's rule $y = 4.5x$ is already $y = mx$, so his speed is
$4.5$ metres per second. Both are now in metres per second and can be compared.

\textbf{Step 4.} $5$ is greater than $4.5$, so Mara's speed $\ans{>}$ Ben's
speed.

\textit{Teaching note:} the whole point of this problem is that a table and an
equation are not comparable until both have been reduced to a rate in the same
units. Doing that reduction is the skill; the comparison afterwards is easy.}

\problem{\textbf{Slope through $(-3, 5)$ and $(2, -5)$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} With negative coordinates the danger is subtracting in
opposite orders top and bottom, so fix the labelling first:
$(x_1, y_1) = (-3, 5)$ and $(x_2, y_2) = (2, -5)$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $m = \dfrac{-5 - 5}{2 - (-3)} = \dfrac{-10}{5}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $m = \ans{-2}$.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $2$ --- the numerator was taken as
$5 - (-5) = 10$ while the denominator stayed $2 - (-3) = 5$, mixing the two
orders. Both subtractions must start from the same point.}

\problem{\textbf{Synthesis: ordering four rates.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Collect the four rates found earlier on the sheet. Line A
(Problem 4) is $3$; Line C (Problem 7) is $-2$; the tub table (Problem 5) is
$6$; the barrel equation (Problem 8) is $-5$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Order them on a number line, not by how big they look. The two
negatives come first, and $-5$ is further left than $-2$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Least to greatest:
$\ans{-5, \; -2, \; 3, \; 6}$.

\textbf{Step 4.} \textit{(Second part: open response --- flagged for manual
review; read the student's sentence.)} Any of the three answers can earn full
credit if the reason fits the representation:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2cm, itemsep=2pt, topsep=3pt]
\item \emph{equation} --- the rate is simply the number in front of $x$, with
no computation at all;
\item \emph{table} --- one subtraction and one division, and you can check the
rate is constant by repeating it on another pair of rows;
\item \emph{graph} --- you can see the direction and the steepness at a glance,
though you must land on lattice points to get an exact value.
\end{itemize}

\textit{Grading note:} require a reason tied to how the rate is obtained.
``The graph, because I like graphs'' is not a justification.

\textit{Listen for:} a student who orders the rates as $2, 3, 5, 6$ by
steepness, ignoring sign. A rate of $-5$ is the fastest \emph{fall}, and it is
still the smallest number.}
