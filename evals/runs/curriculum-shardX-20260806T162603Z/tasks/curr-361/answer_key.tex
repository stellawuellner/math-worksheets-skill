\problem{Factor completely: $x^{3} - 27$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Two terms, and both are perfect cubes: $x^{3}$ and $3^{3}$.
The difference-of-cubes pattern with $a = x$ and $b = 3$ gives
$(a - b)(a^{2} + ab + b^{2})$. The trailing quadratic
$x^{2} + 3x + 9$ has discriminant $9 - 36 < 0$, so it does not factor
further. \ans{(x-3)(x^2+3x+9)}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Factor completely: $8x^{3} + 125$.}

\textbf{Solution:} $8x^{3} = (2x)^{3}$ and $125 = 5^{3}$, so this is a sum of
cubes with $a = 2x$ and $b = 5$: $(a + b)(a^{2} - ab + b^{2})$. That gives
$a^{2} = 4x^{2}$, $ab = 10x$ and $b^{2} = 25$.
\ans{(2x+5)(4x^2-10x+25)}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Factor completely: $x^{3} + 4x^{2} - 9x - 36$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Four terms, so group them in pairs:
\begin{align*}
  x^{2}(x + 4) - 9(x + 4) &= (x + 4)(x^{2} - 9)
\end{align*}
The second factor is a difference of squares, so it breaks down again --- this
is where "factor completely" earns its name. \ans{(x+4)(x-3)(x+3)}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Factor completely: $x^{4} - 13x^{2} + 36$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Treat $x^{2}$ as a single letter: two numbers with product
$36$ and sum $-13$ are $-4$ and $-9$, so the expression is
$(x^{2} - 4)(x^{2} - 9)$. Both are differences of squares.
\ans{(x-2)(x+2)(x-3)(x+3)}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Factor completely: $2x^{3} - 16$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Neither $2x^{3}$ nor $16$ is a perfect cube, but the common
factor $2$ comes out first and leaves one that is:
$2(x^{3} - 8)$, and $8 = 2^{3}$. Applying the difference of cubes with
$a = x$, $b = 2$: \ans{2(x-2)(x^2+2x+4)}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Factor completely: $3x^{3} - 2x^{2} - 12x + 8$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Group in pairs; the shared bracket is $3x - 2$:
\begin{align*}
  x^{2}(3x - 2) - 4(3x - 2) &= (3x - 2)(x^{2} - 4)
\end{align*}
and $x^{2} - 4$ is a difference of squares. \ans{(3x-2)(x-2)(x+2)}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$x^{4} - 5x^{2} + 4$. (a) Factor completely. (b) $x$-intercepts.
(c) Sketch.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} As a quadratic in $x^{2}$: product $4$, sum $-5$, so
$-1$ and $-4$, giving $(x^{2} - 1)(x^{2} - 4)$. Both are differences of
squares. \ans{(x-1)(x+1)(x-2)(x+2)}

\textbf{Solution (b):} Each factor set to zero gives one intercept. \ans{x = 1,\ -1,\ 2,\ -2}

\textbf{Part (c) --- instructor-judged.} The sketch must cross the $x$-axis at
those four values and nowhere else, and must rise on both far ends (even degree,
positive leading coefficient). Between $-1$ and $1$ the curve is above the axis
(at $x = 0$, $y = 4$); between $1$ and $2$, and between $-2$ and $-1$, it dips
below. Accept any smooth freehand curve with those five features; do not require
accurate heights. Grade the sketch against the intercepts the student listed in
part (b).

\vspace{0.15cm}
\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Factor completely: $64x^{3} - 1$.}

\textbf{Solution:} $64x^{3} = (4x)^{3}$ and $1 = 1^{3}$, so this is a difference
of cubes with $a = 4x$ and $b = 1$: $a^{2} = 16x^{2}$, $ab = 4x$, $b^{2} = 1$.
\ans{(4x-1)(16x^2+4x+1)}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Factor completely: $x^{6} - 64$.}

\textbf{Solution:} $x^{6} = (x^{3})^{2}$ and $64 = 8^{2}$, so start with the
difference of squares:
\begin{align*}
  x^{6} - 64 &= (x^{3} - 8)(x^{3} + 8)
\end{align*}
Now each factor is a cube pattern: $x^{3} - 8 = (x - 2)(x^{2} + 2x + 4)$ and
$x^{3} + 8 = (x + 2)(x^{2} - 2x + 4)$. Taking the squares route first is what
gets all four factors; taking cubes first
($x^{6} - 64 = (x^{2})^{3} - 4^{3}$) stops one step short.
\ans{(x-2)(x^2+2x+4)(x+2)(x^2-2x+4)}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$x^{3} + 2x^{2} - 4x - 8$. (a) Factor. (b) Solve. (c) Which zero does
the graph touch, and why?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Group in pairs: $x^{2}(x + 2) - 4(x + 2) = (x + 2)(x^{2} - 4)$,
and $x^{2} - 4 = (x - 2)(x + 2)$, so the factor $x + 2$ appears twice.
\ans{(x+2)^2(x-2)}

\textbf{Solution (b):} Setting each factor to zero gives $x = -2$ (from the
repeated factor) and $x = 2$. \ans{x = -2,\ 2}

\textbf{Part (c) --- instructor-judged.} A full-credit explanation identifies
$x = -2$ and says it comes from a factor raised to the power $2$, so it has
multiplicity two, and an even multiplicity makes the curve touch the axis and
turn back rather than pass through. A student who tests the sign of the
expression just either side of $-2$ and observes that it does not change has
demonstrated it. Naming the repeated factor with no statement about the graph
earns nothing.
