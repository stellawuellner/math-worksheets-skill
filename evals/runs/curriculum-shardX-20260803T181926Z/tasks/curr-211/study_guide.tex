\sstitleblock{Solving Linear Equations with Variables on Both Sides}

\skillheading{Skill 1 --- From a Balance or Table Model to an Equation}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{The model:} a balanced pair of pans is an equation. A sealed bag
$\square$ holds $x$ counters; a loose counter $\bullet$ is 1. Whatever is on the
left is worth the same as whatever is on the right.\\[3pt]
\textbf{The legal moves:} remove the same number of bags from both pans
(subtract $kx$ from both sides), remove the same number of counters from both
pans (subtract a constant), or split both pans into the same number of equal
groups (divide). A table works the same way: two cost columns set equal to each
other is a break-even equation.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:}\quad Left pan: 2 bags and 3 counters. Right pan: 1 bag and 7 counters.
\step{Both pans balance, so the two amounts are equal --- that is what turns the picture into $2x + 3 = x + 7$ rather than into two separate expressions.}
\step{Take 1 bag off each pan: $x + 3 = 7$. Take 3 counters off each pan: $x = \ans{4}$, so each bag holds 4 counters.}
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:}\quad Left pan: 3 bags and 4 counters. Right pan: 1 bag and 10
counters. Write the equation and solve it.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{x = 3}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{Skill 2 --- Collecting the Variable Terms on One Side}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Rule:} an equation stays true when the same quantity is added to, or
subtracted from, both sides. Use that to get every $x$ on one side and every plain
number on the other, then divide by the coefficient.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Which side?} Subtract the \emph{smaller} $x$-coefficient, so the variable
term that survives is positive --- fewer sign errors. From $7x - 2 = 4x + 13$,
subtract $4x$, not $7x$.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Always check} by putting the answer back into the original equation and
evaluating both sides separately. They must give the same number.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:}\quad Solve $7x - 2 = 4x + 13$.
\step{Both sides carry an $x$ term, so nothing can be isolated yet; the first move is to collect the variable on the side with the larger coefficient.}
\step{Subtract $4x$ from both sides: $3x - 2 = 13$. Add 2 to both sides: $3x = 15$. Divide by 3 to get $x = \ans{5}$, and check: $7(5) - 2 = 33 = 4(5) + 13$.}
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:}\quad Solve $9x + 4 = 5x - 8$.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{x = -3}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{Skill 3 --- Distribute First, Then Collect}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Rule:} when a side is a product such as $5(x - 2)$, multiply it out before
collecting --- $5(x-2) = 5x - 10$. Multiply the outside factor by \emph{every}
term inside, signs included.\\[3pt]
\textbf{When the variable disappears:} after collecting, an $x$-free sentence is
left. If it is true, such as $15 = 15$, every number works and the equation has
infinitely many solutions. If it is false, such as $8 = 5$, no number works and
the equation has no solution.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:}\quad Solve $5(x - 2) = 3x + 4$.
\step{The left side is a product, so it cannot be combined with anything until it is distributed --- that is the only move available first.}
\step{Distribute: $5x - 10 = 3x + 4$. Subtract $3x$: $2x - 10 = 4$. Add 10: $2x = 14$, so $x = \ans{7}$, and $5(7 - 2) = 25 = 3(7) + 4$.}
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:}\quad Solve $2(3x + 1) = 4x + 12$.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{x = 5}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} a move must hit \emph{both} sides --- subtracting $2x$ from
the left only is the most common error on these. And when you distribute a
negative factor, the sign of every term inside changes:
$-3(x - 4) = -3x + 12$, not $-3x - 12$.
\end{watchoutbox}
