\problem{One of the 120 surveyed students is chosen at random. Find $P(\text{instrument})$.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad The instrument row totals $40$ students out of the grand total of
$120$, so $P = \dfrac{40}{120}$. \ans{P = \tfrac{1}{3}}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{A student is chosen from those who play an instrument. Find
$P(\text{sport} \mid \text{instrument})$.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad The condition replaces the pool: only the $40$ instrument players
count, and $18$ of them play a sport, so $P = \dfrac{18}{40}$. \ans{P = \tfrac{9}{20}}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{A student is chosen from those who play a sport. Find
$P(\text{instrument} \mid \text{sport})$.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Now the sport column is the pool: $60$ students, of whom $18$ play an
instrument, so $P = \dfrac{18}{60}$. Note this is a different number from problem 2 --- the
order of the two events in a conditional probability matters. \ans{P = \tfrac{3}{10}}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Test independence of ``plays a sport'' and ``plays an instrument''.
\ (a) $P(\text{sport}) \cdot P(\text{instrument})$. \ (b) $P(\text{sport and instrument})$.
\ (c) What does the comparison say?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $P(\text{sport}) = \dfrac{60}{120} = \dfrac{1}{2}$ and
$P(\text{instrument}) = \dfrac{40}{120} = \dfrac{1}{3}$, so the product is
$\dfrac{1}{2} \cdot \dfrac{1}{3}$. \ans{\tfrac{1}{6}}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad $18$ of the $120$ students do both, so
$P = \dfrac{18}{120}$. \ans{\tfrac{3}{20}}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} \quad $\dfrac{1}{6} = \dfrac{20}{120}$ but the
actual joint count is $\dfrac{18}{120}$. The two are not equal, so the events are \emph{not}
independent: knowing a student plays an instrument lowers the chance they also play a sport.
Full credit requires the comparison and the "not independent" conclusion, stated in that
direction.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Two cards are dealt from a 52-card deck without replacement. Find
$P(\text{both hearts})$.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad $P(\text{first heart}) = \dfrac{13}{52}$. After a heart is removed,
$12$ hearts remain among $51$ cards, so $P(\text{second heart} \mid \text{first heart}) =
\dfrac{12}{51}$. The multiplication rule gives
$\dfrac{13}{52} \cdot \dfrac{12}{51} = \dfrac{1}{4} \cdot \dfrac{4}{17}$. \ans{P = \tfrac{1}{17}}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Each part is defective with probability $0.04$, independently. Two parts are
selected. Find $P(\text{both defective})$.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad The parts are independent, so the conditional factor equals the
unconditional one and the rule collapses to a product:
$P = (0.04)(0.04)$. \ans{P = 0.0016}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Each part is good with probability $0.96$, independently. Five are selected. Find
$P(\text{at least one defective})$, to four decimal places.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad ``At least one'' is the complement of ``none'', and ``none defective''
means all five are good:
$P(\text{all good}) = 0.96^5 = 0.8153726976$, so
$P(\text{at least one defective}) = 1 - 0.96^5 = 0.1846273\ldots$ \ans{P \approx 0.1846}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Two independent trials, success probability $p$, failure probability $q$.
\ (a) Expand $(p+q)^2$. \ (b) What does each term count?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $(p+q)^2 = (p+q)(p+q) = p^2 + pq + qp + q^2$. \ans{p^2 + 2pq + q^2}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad $p^2$ is the probability of two successes;
$q^2$ is the probability of two failures; $2pq$ is the probability of one of each. The factor
of $2$ is there because ``one success and one failure'' happens in two orders --- success then
failure, or failure then success --- and the two products $pq$ and $qp$ are the two orderings.
Full credit requires all three terms matched and the $2$ explained as the count of orderings.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Screening: $5\%$ have the condition; the test is positive for $90\%$ of those who do
and $8\%$ of those who do not. \ (a) $P(\text{positive})$. \ (b)
$P(\text{condition} \mid \text{positive})$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Split the positives into the two groups they can come from:
\[ P(\text{positive}) = (0.05)(0.90) + (0.95)(0.08) = 0.045 + 0.076 \]
\ans{P \approx 0.121}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad Of that total, only the first piece comes from people who really
have the condition, so
$P(\text{condition} \mid \text{positive}) = \dfrac{0.045}{0.121} = 0.37190\ldots$
\ans{P \approx 0.372}

Note how far this is from the test's $90\%$ sensitivity: because the condition is rare, most
positive results come from the large healthy group.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Survey table again. \ (a) $P(\text{neither})$. \ (b)
$P(\text{no instrument} \mid \text{no sport})$. \ (c) Does the condition change the chance?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $38$ students play neither, out of $120$, so
$P = \dfrac{38}{120}$. \ans{P = \tfrac{19}{60}}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad The ``no sport'' column holds $60$ students, $38$ of whom play no
instrument, so $P = \dfrac{38}{60}$. \ans{P = \tfrac{19}{30}}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} \quad Unconditionally,
$P(\text{no instrument}) = \dfrac{80}{120} = \dfrac{2}{3} \approx 0.667$. Conditionally on
playing no sport it is $\dfrac{19}{30} \approx 0.633$. The two differ, so the condition does
change the chance and the characteristics are not independent. Full credit requires both
numbers compared and the conclusion drawn.
