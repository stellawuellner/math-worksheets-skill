Here are the three documents for the grades 6–7 sampling and probability
workshop — ten problems on random samples and simple probability models.

**Worksheet (`ws_sampling_curr198.pdf`, 5 pages).** Every problem states its
sample, its counts and its units: a raffle drum, a homework sample, tagged fish
in a pond, a prize spinner (drawn on the sheet), pets per household, walkers at
a 900-student school, a number cube rolled 300 times, seven April temperatures,
a skate-park survey, and a simulation to design. Three skills alternate so the
student has to pick the method rather than repeat one:

- probability of one event, and predicting a count over many trials
  (problems 1, 4, 7, 10)
- using a random sample to estimate a population (problems 3, 6, 9)
- summarizing the sample itself — mean, mode, median, IQR (problems 2, 5, 8)

Difficulty ramps 1 to 5. Problem 8 plants the classic error (a classmate
answering 19, the range, for the interquartile range) and asks the student to
name what that number actually is. Problem 9 has correct arithmetic resting on a
biased sample and asks why the estimate should not be trusted. Problem 10 asks
for a complete simulation design — device, outcome rule, one trial, number of
trials, and how to turn results into a probability estimate.

**Answer key (`ak_sampling_curr198.pdf`, 3 pages).** Quick-answer bank, then a
full solution for each problem: why the model applies, the arithmetic, and a
sense check (complement adding to one on the spinner, "a quarter of 900 is 225"
on the walkers, "3/20 of 80 is 12" on the fish). The three open-response parts
carry a model answer plus a "look for" line naming what a correct response must
contain. Grade level and the curriculum summary print here only.

**Study guide (`ss_sampling_curr198.pdf`, 2 pages).** Three sections — the
probability of one event, estimating from a random sample, and summarizing a
sample with median and IQR — each with a rule box, a worked example, and a
try-it whose answer is upside down inside the box. The sampling rule box says
plainly that biased sampling cannot be repaired by arithmetic.
