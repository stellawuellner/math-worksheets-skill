# Kindergarten / Grade 1 — Counting On to Compare Two Sets

Three PDFs are ready: the student worksheet, a full step-by-step answer key, and
a two-page study guide.

**What the worksheet covers.** Ten problems on counting on to compare two sets.
Every problem shows the objects to be counted — five-frames, ten-frames, and
rows of shapes — so nothing depends on reading a number the child cannot see.

The first two problems are a warm-up on the count-on move itself (a full frame
plus a few loose objects). After that the three jobs are interleaved so the
child has to decide which one the question is asking for: write $>$ or $<$
between two sets, or answer "how many more". Problem 8 has two sets that look
different and count the same, so $=$ is the right answer; problem 10 closes with
the sheet's point made as plainly as it can be — the tray with the fuller frame
turns out to be the smaller set, and only counting on reveals it.

Each problem gets 4–5.5 cm of blank space under the picture plus its own answer
line, which is why the sheet runs five pages. That is deliberate: this is space
a five-year-old needs to draw, tally and cross off, not padding.

**What the answer key contains.** A worked solution for every problem, written
as the count a teacher would say out loud — "start at five: six, seven, eight" —
rather than an equation. Each solution names the misconception it is guarding
against: restarting at one instead of counting on, counting the starting number
as one of the extras, and deciding by which row looks longer. Two of those are
declared misconception traps and appear in the key's "common wrong answers"
bank, so you know what a 3 on problem 1 or a 7 on problem 9 means. The key also
carries the curriculum section with the standard (K.CC.A.1–K.CC.C.7) and the
difficulty range. The grade level prints only there — never on the sheet the
child holds.

**What the study guide contains.** Three teaching sections — count on to find
how many, compare two sets, and find how many more — each with a rule box in
child-sized language, a worked example whose first step says why that method
fits, and a try-it problem with the answer printed upside down inside the box.
A watch-out box closes on the two errors this topic produces: counting the
starting number as one of the extras, and judging by row length instead of by
count.

**Verification.** All 10 answers were recomputed independently with SymPy and
passed, including the three comparison symbols and both declared misconception
traps (each confirmed to give a visibly different number from the correct
answer). Nothing on this set is flagged for manual review.
