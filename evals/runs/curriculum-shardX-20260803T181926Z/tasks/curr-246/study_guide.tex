\sstitleblock{Describing a Scatter Plot: Direction, Form, Strength}

\skillheading{Skill 1 --- Direction: which way does it go?}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Direction} is one of three words: \textbf{positive} ($y$ tends to rise as $x$
rises), \textbf{negative} ($y$ tends to fall), or \textbf{none}.\par
\smallskip
\textbf{How to decide with evidence, not a glance:} compare the $y$-value at the far left
with the $y$-value at the far right, then check that the points in between agree.
Left $<$ right and climbing throughout $\Rightarrow$ positive. Left $>$ right and falling
throughout $\Rightarrow$ negative. If the highest and lowest $y$-values sit in the
\emph{middle} rather than at the ends, there is no direction.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad A plot's leftmost point is $(1, 12)$ and its rightmost is
$(9, 30)$, with the points in between rising steadily. Name the direction.
\step{Direction is a statement about how $y$ changes across the whole plot, so compare the
two ends first and then confirm the middle agrees.}
\step{The left end is at $12$ and the right end at $30$, and every point in between climbs
--- so the comparison is representative.}

$\Rightarrow\quad \ans{12 < 30}$, so the direction is \textbf{positive}
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad A plot's leftmost point is $(2, 45)$ and its rightmost is
$(20, 18)$, with the points falling steadily. Compare the two end values.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{45 > 18}$, negative}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{Skill 2 --- Form: what shape do the points make?}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Form} is \textbf{linear} (the points follow a straight line) or \textbf{nonlinear}
(they bend).\par
\smallskip
\textbf{The test:} walk the points left to right and ask whether they ever \emph{reverse}.
A line only ever goes one way. A single turning point --- a lowest or highest value in the
middle rather than at an end --- means the form is nonlinear.\par
\smallskip
Check form \emph{before} naming a direction. On a curve, ``positive'' and ``negative'' do
not describe anything.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad A plot has points $(1, 20)$, $(2, 12)$, $(3, 8)$, $(4, 13)$,
$(5, 24)$. Is the form linear or nonlinear?
\step{The quickest evidence for form is a turning point, so find the $x$-value carrying the
smallest $y$ and see whether it sits at an end or in the middle.}
\step{The smallest $y$ is $8$, sitting at $x = 3$ --- in the middle. The points fall to it
and then rise away from it.}

$\Rightarrow\quad$ turning point at $x = \ans{3}$, so the form is \textbf{nonlinear}
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad A plot has points $(2, 40)$, $(4, 22)$, $(6, 14)$, $(8, 20)$,
$(10, 38)$. At which $x$ does it turn?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $x = \ans{6}$, nonlinear}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{Skill 3 --- Strength: how tight is the band?}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Strength} is how close the points sit to the trend line: \textbf{strong} (a narrow
band), \textbf{moderate}, or \textbf{weak} (a wide cloud).\par
\smallskip
\textbf{Residual} $=$ actual $y$ $-$ predicted $y$. Small residuals mean a strong
association. The \textbf{range} of the residuals (largest minus smallest) measures the
width of the band, so a small range means strong.\par
\smallskip
\textbf{Strength is not slope.} A steep line can have a wide cloud around it. Slope says how
fast $y$ changes; strength says how reliably.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad Five points have residuals $1, -1, 0, 1, -1$ from their trend line.
How strong is the association?
\step{Strength is about the width of the band the points occupy, so summarise the residuals
by their range rather than judging the picture by eye.}
\step{The largest residual is $1$ and the smallest is $-1$, so the range is $1 - (-1)$.}

$\Rightarrow\quad$ range $= \ans{2}$, a narrow band: \textbf{strong}
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad Five points have residuals $7, -6, 8, -7, 6$. Find the range and say
how strong the association is.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: range $= \ans{15}$, weak}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} an \textbf{outlier} --- one point far off the pattern --- makes a
strong association look weak and drags the mean toward itself. Say so explicitly:
``strong positive linear, with one outlier'' beats both ``strong'' and ``weak''.
\end{watchoutbox}
