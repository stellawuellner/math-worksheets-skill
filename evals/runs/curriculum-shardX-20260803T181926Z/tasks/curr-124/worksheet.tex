\wstitleblock{Equivalent Fractions and Comparing --- Look at Both Numbers}{Grades 4--5}{}

\noindent\small A fraction has two jobs to read. The \textbf{bottom} number says
how many equal pieces the whole was cut into, so a bigger bottom number means
\emph{smaller} pieces. The \textbf{top} number says how many of those pieces you
have.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=0.8cm, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item Looking at only the tops, or only the bottoms, is not a way to compare
        two fractions. It is right only when the other number already matches.
  \item When neither number matches, rewrite both fractions with the same
        bottom number, then compare the tops.
  \item Draw a picture or a number line whenever it helps. Show your work.
\end{itemize}
\normalsize

\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem[5cm]{The bottom numbers already match, so the pieces are the same size.
Write $<$, $>$, or $=$ in the blank:\par
\vspace{0.15cm}
\hspace{1.5cm}$\dfrac{3}{8}$ \quad \ansblank \quad $\dfrac{5}{8}$\par
\vspace{0.15cm}
Then write one sentence saying why counting the top numbers is safe here.}

\problem[5cm]{This time the \emph{top} numbers match, so you are holding the same
number of pieces --- but the pieces are not the same size. Write $<$, $>$, or
$=$:\par
\vspace{0.15cm}
\hspace{1.5cm}$\dfrac{3}{5}$ \quad \ansblank \quad $\dfrac{3}{8}$}

\problem[5cm]{\textbf{Find and fix.} Ben writes
$\dfrac{2}{9} > \dfrac{2}{4}$, and says, ``Nine is bigger than four.''\par
Ben compared only the bottom numbers. Fix his answer: write $<$, $>$, or $=$ in
the blank, and say in a few words what a bigger bottom number really does to the
pieces.\par
\vspace{0.15cm}
\hspace{1.5cm}$\dfrac{2}{9}$ \quad \ansblank \quad $\dfrac{2}{4}$}

\problem[5cm]{\textbf{Find and fix.} Cara wants to rewrite $\dfrac{3}{5}$ with
$40$ on the bottom. She writes $\dfrac{3}{40}$, changing only the bottom
number.\par
Whatever you do to the bottom, you must do to the top. Work out the correct top
number for $\dfrac{\square}{40}$ and write it on the answer line.}

\problem[6cm]{Which is greater, $\dfrac{3}{4}$ or $\dfrac{5}{7}$? Neither number
matches, so counting tops or bottoms cannot decide it.\par
Rewrite both fractions with $28$ on the bottom. Write the top number that
$\dfrac{3}{4}$ becomes on the answer line, and write $<$, $>$, or $=$ in this
blank:\par
\vspace{0.15cm}
\hspace{1.5cm}$\dfrac{3}{4}$ \quad \ansblank \quad $\dfrac{5}{7}$}

\problem[5cm]{Write $\dfrac{6}{8}$ in its simplest form --- the equal fraction
with the smallest possible numbers. Divide the top and the bottom by the same
number.}

\problem[7cm]{Put these three fractions in order from \textbf{smallest to
largest}:\par
\vspace{0.15cm}
\hspace{1.5cm}$\dfrac{2}{3}$, \quad $\dfrac{3}{5}$, \quad $\dfrac{7}{10}$\par
\vspace{0.15cm}
Rewriting all three with $30$ on the bottom will settle it. Write the three
fractions in order on the answer line.}

\problem[8cm]{\textbf{Explain, then compare.}\par
\textbf{(a)} A friend says, ``To compare two fractions, just look at the top
numbers.'' Explain, in your own words, why that does not work, and give one
example where the friend's shortcut gives the wrong answer.\par
\textbf{(b)} Compare $\dfrac{5}{6}$ and $\dfrac{7}{9}$ by rewriting both with
$18$ on the bottom. Write the top number that $\dfrac{5}{6}$ becomes on the
answer line, and write $<$, $>$, or $=$ in this blank:\par
\vspace{0.15cm}
\hspace{1.5cm}$\dfrac{5}{6}$ \quad \ansblank \quad $\dfrac{7}{9}$}
