\problem{Circle $x^2+y^2=25$ near $(3,4)$, upper branch $y = \sqrt{25-x^2}$.
(a) $\frac{dy}{dx}$ in terms of $x$. (b) Its value at $x = 3$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Write the branch as $\left(25 - x^2\right)^{1/2}$ and use
the chain rule:
\[
  \frac{dy}{dx} = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(25-x^2\right)^{-1/2}(-2x)
    = \frac{-x}{\sqrt{25-x^2}} .
\]
So the derivative is \ans{\dfrac{-x}{\sqrt{25-x^2}}}

\textbf{Solution (b):} At $x = 3$ the radical is $\sqrt{25-9} = 4$, so the slope
is $\frac{-3}{4}$. \ans{-\tfrac{3}{4}}

That $4$ in the denominator is exactly the $y$-coordinate of the point --- which
is the clue that the implicit form will be simpler.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Same circle, implicitly: differentiate $x^2+y^2=25$ and evaluate at
$(3,4)$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Differentiate both sides with respect to $x$, remembering the
chain-rule factor on $y^2$:
\begin{align*}
  2x + 2y\frac{dy}{dx} &= 0 \\
  \frac{dy}{dx} &= -\frac{x}{y} .
\end{align*}
At $(3,4)$ this is $-\frac{3}{4}$. \ans{-\tfrac{3}{4}}

Same tangent line as problem 1, reached in two lines instead of a chain-rule
computation, and valid on the lower half of the circle too. Substituting the
coordinates the wrong way round gives $-\frac{4}{3}$, which is the reciprocal
and is the error to watch for.

\problem{$x^2 + xy + y^2 = 7$ at $(1,2)$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Check the point first: $1 + 2 + 4 = 7$. \ Now differentiate,
using the product rule on $xy$:
\begin{align*}
  2x + \left(y + x\frac{dy}{dx}\right) + 2y\frac{dy}{dx} &= 0 \\
  (x + 2y)\frac{dy}{dx} &= -(2x + y) \\
  \frac{dy}{dx} &= -\frac{2x+y}{x+2y} .
\end{align*}
At $(1,2)$: $-\frac{2+2}{1+4} = -\frac{4}{5}$. \ans{-\tfrac{4}{5}}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{$x^3 + y^3 = 9$ at $(1,2)$.}

\textbf{Solution:} The point checks: $1 + 8 = 9$. Differentiating,
\[
  3x^2 + 3y^2\frac{dy}{dx} = 0 \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
  \frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x^2}{y^2} .
\]
At $(1,2)$: $-\frac{1}{4}$. \ans{-\tfrac{1}{4}}

Dropping the squares and writing $-x/y$ would give $-\frac{1}{2}$; the squares
come from differentiating cubes and cannot be dropped.

\problem{Parabola $y^2 = 4x$ at $(1,2)$. (a) Derivative of the branch
$y = 2\sqrt{x}$. (b) The implicit slope at $(1,2)$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $y = 2x^{1/2}$, so
$\frac{dy}{dx} = 2 \cdot \tfrac{1}{2}x^{-1/2} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$.
\ans{\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}}

\textbf{Solution (b):} Implicitly, $2y\frac{dy}{dx} = 4$, so
$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{2}{y}$. At $(1,2)$ that is $\frac{2}{2}$. \ans{1}

Part (a) at $x = 1$ gives $\frac{1}{\sqrt{1}} = 1$ as well: the two forms
$\frac{1}{\sqrt{x}}$ and $\frac{2}{y}$ are the same function on this branch,
because $y = 2\sqrt{x}$ there.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{$x^2y + y^2 = 12$ at $(2,2)$.}

\textbf{Solution:} The point checks: $4(2) + 4 = 12$. The first term is a
product, so
\begin{align*}
  2xy + x^2\frac{dy}{dx} + 2y\frac{dy}{dx} &= 0 \\
  \left(x^2 + 2y\right)\frac{dy}{dx} &= -2xy \\
  \frac{dy}{dx} &= -\frac{2xy}{x^2 + 2y} .
\end{align*}
At $(2,2)$: $-\frac{8}{4+4} = -\frac{8}{8}$. \ans{-1}

Skipping the product rule --- differentiating $x^2y$ as $x^2\frac{dy}{dx}$ alone
--- makes the numerator vanish and produces a slope of $0$. A horizontal tangent
here would be visible nonsense, which is a useful check.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{$x^2y^3 = 8$ at $(1,2)$.}

\textbf{Solution:} The point checks: $1 \cdot 8 = 8$. Product rule on the left,
with the chain rule inside $y^3$:
\begin{align*}
  2xy^3 + x^2\left(3y^2\frac{dy}{dx}\right) &= 0 \\
  \frac{dy}{dx} &= -\frac{2xy^3}{3x^2y^2} = -\frac{2y}{3x} .
\end{align*}
At $(1,2)$: $-\frac{4}{3}$. \ans{-\tfrac{4}{3}}

The simplification from $\frac{2xy^3}{3x^2y^2}$ to $\frac{2y}{3x}$ is worth
insisting on --- it makes the substitution a one-line job.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Challenge. Folium $x^3 + y^3 = 6xy$ at $(3,3)$. (a) The slope.
(b) The symmetry argument.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The point checks: $27 + 27 = 54$ and $6(9) = 54$.
Differentiate both sides, product rule on the right:
\begin{align*}
  3x^2 + 3y^2\frac{dy}{dx} &= 6y + 6x\frac{dy}{dx} \\
  \left(3y^2 - 6x\right)\frac{dy}{dx} &= 6y - 3x^2 \\
  \frac{dy}{dx} &= \frac{2y - x^2}{y^2 - 2x} .
\end{align*}
At $(3,3)$: $\frac{6-9}{9-6} = \frac{-3}{3}$. \ans{-1}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged, no machine check.} Swapping $x$ and
$y$ leaves $x^3+y^3=6xy$ unchanged, so the curve is symmetric about the line
$y = x$. The point $(3,3)$ lies on that mirror line, so reflecting the curve
carries the tangent at $(3,3)$ to itself. A reflection in $y = x$ turns a slope
$m$ into $\frac{1}{m}$, so $m = \frac{1}{m}$ and $m = \pm 1$. It cannot be $+1$,
because that is the direction of the mirror line itself and the curve crosses
$y=x$ at $(3,3)$ rather than running along it --- leaving $m = -1$, which is
what part (a) computed. Full credit needs the symmetry observation and the
conclusion that the slope must be its own reciprocal; award partial credit for
noticing the symmetry alone.
