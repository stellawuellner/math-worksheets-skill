\sstitleblock{Sign Errors in Parabola Transformations}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Vertex form:} \quad $y = a(x - h)^2 + k$ \quad vertex $(h, k)$, axis of symmetry $x = h$.\par
\textbf{The one question that settles every sign:} \emph{what value of $x$ makes the
squared factor zero?} That value \textbf{is} $h$. Read $h$ that way and you never have to
remember a direction rule.\par
$a$ multiplies \textbf{outputs}: $|a| > 1$ stretches, $0 < |a| < 1$ compresses, and $a < 0$
flips the parabola over its own vertex. $a$ never moves the vertex.
\end{formulabox}

\skillheading{Skill 1 --- Reading the vertex out of the form}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Rule:} the horizontal shift goes \emph{opposite} the printed sign, because the form
subtracts $h$.\par
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
$(x - 5)$ \ $\Rightarrow$ \ $h = 5$: & right 5\\
$(x + 3)$ \ $\Rightarrow$ \ $h = -3$: & left 3\\
\end{tabular}\par
The vertical part has no such twist: $+k$ is up, $-k$ is down.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad Find the minimum value of $y = (x - 2)^2 + 6$.
\step{The minimum of an upward parabola happens at the vertex, so I need $h$ --- the $x$ that makes the squared factor zero.}
\step{$x - 2 = 0$ gives $x = 2$ (right 2, not left). Then $y = (2 - 2)^2 + 6 = 0 + 6$.}
$\Rightarrow\quad$ minimum value $= \ans{6}$ at the point $(2, 6)$
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad Find the minimum value of $y = (x - 7)^2 + 1$.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{1}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{Skill 2 --- Writing the equation from a description}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Build it in this order:} reflection and stretch into $a$, horizontal shift inside
the parentheses (\emph{opposite} sign), vertical shift outside (same sign).\par
right $p$: $(x - p)$ \quad left $p$: $(x + p)$ \quad up $q$: $+q$ \quad down $q$: $-q$
\quad reflect over the $x$-axis: $a < 0$
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad Reflect $y = x^2$ over the $x$-axis, shift left 3 and up 10. What is the value at $x = -3$?
\step{Each described move maps to one slot of $a(x-h)^2 + k$, so I write the equation first and only then substitute.}
\step{Reflection gives $a = -1$, left 3 gives $(x + 3)$, up 10 adds 10: \ $y = -(x + 3)^2 + 10$. Then $y(-3) = -(-3 + 3)^2 + 10 = 0 + 10$.}
$\Rightarrow\quad \ans{10}$
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad Stretch $y = x^2$ vertically by 2, shift left 5 and down 6. What is the value at $x = -5$?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{-6}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{Skill 3 --- Finding and fixing a sign error}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{How to audit someone's work in three checks:}
\par\textbf{1.} Does their $h$ make the squared factor zero? If not, the shift direction is flipped.
\par\textbf{2.} Is their vertex value the largest or smallest output? A wrong $h$ still gives a real point --- it is just not the vertex.
\par\textbf{3.} Did the sign of $a$ survive? Losing it turns a maximum into a minimum.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad A student says the vertex of $y = (x + 8)^2 - 2$ is at $x = 8$ and the minimum is 254. Fix it.
\step{Check 1 first: does their $h$ zero the squared factor? $8 + 8 = 16 \ne 0$, so their $h$ is the culprit.}
\step{$x + 8 = 0$ gives $x = -8$, so $y = (-8 + 8)^2 - 2 = 0 - 2$. Their 254 is $y(8)$ --- a genuine point on the curve, just not the lowest.}
$\Rightarrow\quad$ true minimum $= \ans{-2}$ at $(-8, -2)$
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad A student reports the vertex of $y = -(x - 9)^2 + 4$ as $x = -9$, with maximum $-320$. What is the real maximum?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{4}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} A wrong $h$ almost never produces an obviously silly number --- it
produces a real point on the parabola, which is why these errors survive. Always test the
candidate vertex by checking that the squared factor is zero there. And remember that a
negative $a$ reflects the graph without moving it: the shifts stay exactly where $h$ and
$k$ put them.
\end{watchoutbox}
