\noindent\textbf{How to grade this.} Accept any correct route, but the exponent
arithmetic must be visible. A student who writes only the final power on
problems 5 and 10 has not shown whether the exponents were added or multiplied,
which is the whole distinction this sheet tests.

\problem{Expand and rewrite $3^2 \cdot 3^3$ as one power of $3$.

\textbf{Solution:} $3^2$ is two factors of $3$ and $3^3$ is three factors of
$3$; written together that is $3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3 \cdot 3$, five factors
of $3$. Counting factors is exactly what adding exponents does, so
$3^2 \cdot 3^3 = \ans{3^5}$, which equals $243$.}

\problem{Write $x^4 \cdot x^6$ as a single power of $x$.

\textbf{Solution:} Same base, so the factors simply pile up: four of them and
then six more. Add the exponents: $x^4 \cdot x^6 = x^{4+6} = \ans{x^{10}}$.}

\problem{Write $\dfrac{5^7}{5^4}$ as a single power of $5$.

\textbf{Solution:} Expanded, the top holds seven factors of $5$ and the bottom
holds four. Four pairs cancel, leaving three factors on top --- which is what
subtracting the exponents records:
\[ \frac{5^7}{5^4} = 5^{7-4} = \ans{5^3} \]
As a number that is $125$.}

\problem{Write $\dfrac{y^9}{y^3}$ as a single power of $y$.

\textbf{Solution:} Subtract the exponents: $y^{9-3} = \ans{y^6}$.}

\problem{Simplify $a^3 \cdot a^2$, then evaluate at $a = 2$.

\textbf{Solution:} The bases match, so add the exponents:
$a^3 \cdot a^2 = a^{3+2} = a^5$. Substituting $a = 2$ gives $2^5 = \ans{32}$.
Note that multiplying the exponents instead would give $a^6$ and the value
$64$ --- a different number, which is how you can tell the two laws apart.}

\problem{Simplify $\dfrac{12x^5}{4x^2}$.

\textbf{Solution:} Handle the coefficient and the variable part separately.
The coefficients divide: $12 \div 4 = 3$. The powers of $x$ follow the quotient
law: $x^{5-2} = x^3$. Together:
\[ \frac{12x^5}{4x^2} = \ans{3x^3} \]}

\problem{Read $\dfrac{6^4}{6^4}$ two ways and say what $6^0$ must equal.

\textbf{Solution:} (a) By cancelling: the top and the bottom are the same
number, so the quotient is $1$. (b) By the quotient law: $6^{4-4} = 6^0$. Both
readings describe the same expression, so $6^0$ and $1$ are the same number:
\[ 6^0 = \ans{1} \]
This is why any nonzero base to the zero power is defined as one --- it is
forced by the quotient law, not an arbitrary rule.}

\problem{Apply the quotient law to $\dfrac{4^2}{4^5}$.

\textbf{Solution:} Subtract as usual, even though the answer is negative:
$4^{2-5} = 4^{-3}$. Expanding shows the same thing: two factors on top cancel
two of the five below, leaving three factors of $4$ in the denominator.
\[ \frac{4^2}{4^5} = \ans{4^{-3}} = \frac{1}{4^3} = \frac{1}{64} \]
A negative exponent is a location (denominator), never a negative number.}

\problem{Compare $3^4 \cdot 3^2$ with $\dfrac{3^7}{3^1}$.

\textbf{Solution:} Simplify each side to a single power of $3$ before comparing.
Left: add the exponents, $3^{4+2} = 3^6$. Right: subtract the exponents,
$3^{7-1} = 3^6$. Same base, same exponent, so the two expressions are the same
number ($729$), and the correct symbol is $\ans{=}$.}

\problem{Jae's error: $2^5 \cdot 2^3$ answered as $32768$.

\textbf{Solution:} Jae multiplied the exponents. The product of powers law adds
them, because the two groups of factors are being placed side by side, not
nested: $2^5 \cdot 2^3 = 2^{5+3} = 2^8$. Evaluating,
$2^8 = \ans{256}$. Jae's route computed $2^{15}$ instead, which is more than a
hundred times too large --- a useful reasonableness check on its own.}
