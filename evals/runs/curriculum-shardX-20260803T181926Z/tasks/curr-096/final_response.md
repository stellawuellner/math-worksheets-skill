# Reading scaled picture graphs and bar graphs (Grades 2–3)

Three PDFs are ready:

- **`ws_graphs_curr096.pdf`** — the student worksheet, 8 problems.
- **`ak_graphs_curr096.pdf`** — the answer key, with the reasoning written out.
- **`ss_graphs_curr096.pdf`** — a 2-page study guide the child can keep beside them.

## What the worksheet does

The one idea on this sheet is that **a picture or a grid line can be worth more than one
thing**. A younger child reads a graph by counting one-for-one, and a scaled graph is
designed to break that habit. So every problem forces a look at the key or the scale before
any counting happens.

Two data models run through the sheet, and each problem carries its own copy of the graph
it needs, so nothing has to be flipped back to:

- A **picture graph** of books read in March, where one book picture stands for 4 books —
  and one row deliberately ends in a *half* picture.
- A **bar graph** of bikes in the school rack, with a scale that counts by twos — and one
  bar that deliberately stops halfway between two grid lines.

The eight problems: read one row (Ana, 20 books), read another (Ben, 12), compare two rows
in books rather than in pictures (Cara reads 4 more than Ana), read one bar (Wednesday,
12 bikes), read all five bars and total them (41 bikes), read the half-way bar (Tuesday, 7
bikes), read the half picture (Dev, 10 books), and finally explain a friend's mistake.

Three problems carry a declared misconception trap, which is checked by machine and printed
in the answer key so you can see the wrong answer coming: counting grid lines instead of
reading the scale (6 instead of 12), rounding a bar up to the nearest labelled line (8
instead of 7), and counting a half picture as a whole one (12 instead of 10). The numbers
were chosen so each of those errors lands on a visibly different answer.

## What was verified, and what was not

**Seven of the eight problems were machine-checked** before the PDFs were made: every value
read off a graph, the comparison, and the week's total were recomputed independently from
the same data that draws the charts, so the picture and the answer cannot disagree. All
seven passed.

**Problem 8 is flagged for manual review.** It asks the child to explain what Jo forgot
when she said "Ana read 5 books, because there are 5 pictures." No machine can grade an
explanation, so the answer key gives a model answer, lists the wordings that deserve full
credit ("each picture is 4 books", "you have to go 4, 8, 12, 16, 20", "the key says so"),
and names two answers worth pushing back on gently.

## The study guide

Three short sections, each with a rule, a small worked graph, and a distinct try-it whose
answer is printed upside down inside the box: picture graphs with a key, bar graphs whose
scale skips numbers, and "how many more / how many altogether". All six worked examples and
try-its were verified by the same checker as the worksheet.

Standard covered: 2.MD / 3.MD (measurement and data). Difficulty ramps from 1 to 5, with the
two trickiest reads — the half-way bar and the half picture — placed at the end.
