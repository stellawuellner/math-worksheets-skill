\sstitleblock{Finding the Missing Factor}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{What a missing factor is.} In $\square \times b = c$ the box holds the
\emph{missing factor}: how many are in each equal group, or how many equal
groups there are.\\[3pt]
\textbf{The rule:} a missing factor is found by dividing. Total $\div$ known
factor $=$ missing factor.\\[3pt]
\textbf{The check:} multiply back. The two factors must make the total again.
\end{formulabox}

\skillheading{1. Missing factor from an equal-groups picture}

\begin{formulabox}
Count one group, not the whole picture. Then ask which number the picture is
missing: the number of groups, or the number in each group. Write the
multiplication sentence first, and let the division fact fill the box.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} $5$ equal rows of counters, $30$ counters in all. How many
counters are in each row?
\step{The rows are equal, so this is groups times size, and the size of a row
is what is missing.}
\step{Divide the total by the number of groups: $30 \div 5$, and check it with
$5 \times 6 = 30$.}

Each row holds $\ans{6}$ counters.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} $3$ equal baskets hold $21$ apples in all. How many apples are
in one basket?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{7}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{2. Missing factor in a number sentence}

\begin{formulabox}
With no picture, use the fact family. Every multiplication sentence carries two
division facts, and one of them names the box directly.\\[3pt]
A division sentence with a missing divisor is the same question in disguise:
rewrite it as a multiplication sentence before you divide.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Find the missing factor in $6 \times \square = 48$.
\step{One factor and the total are known, so the matching division fact names
the missing factor.}
\step{Divide the total by the known factor: $48 \div 6$, then check with
$6 \times 8 = 48$.}

The missing factor is $\ans{8}$.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Find the missing number in $40 \div \square = 8$.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{5}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{3. Missing factor in a story}

\begin{formulabox}
Read for the two group words first: how many groups, and how many in each
group. One of them is missing --- that is your box.\\[3pt]
Write the equation before computing, and finish the answer with the unit the
story uses, such as \emph{rows}, \emph{packs}, or \emph{chairs}.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} A shop puts $54$ apples into $6$ equal crates. How many
apples are in each crate?
\step{The crates are equal groups and the number in one crate is missing, so
the story is groups times size equals total.}
\step{The equation is $6 \times \square = 54$, so divide: $54 \div 6$, and
check with $6 \times 9 = 54$.}

Each crate holds $\ans{9}$ apples.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} A baker fills trays with $7$ rolls each and uses $35$ rolls in
all. How many trays does she fill?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{5}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} subtracting the two numbers in the sentence is the most
common mistake, and it never answers a missing-factor question. Always finish
by multiplying your answer back to the total.
\end{watchoutbox}
