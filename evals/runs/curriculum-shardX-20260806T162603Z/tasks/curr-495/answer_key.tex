\problem{$x(t) = t^2 + 1$, $y(t) = 3t$. Find the speed at $t = 2$.}

\textbf{Solution:} $x'(t) = 2t$ and $y'(t) = 3$, so at $t = 2$ the velocity is $\langle 4, 3\rangle$ and the speed is $\sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{25}$. \ans{5}

\problem{Where do $r = 2$ and $r = 4\cos\theta$ meet, for $\theta$ in $[0, 2\pi)$?}

\textbf{Solution:} Set the two expressions for $r$ equal: $4\cos\theta = 2$, so $\cos\theta = \tfrac{1}{2}$. On $[0, 2\pi)$ that gives \ans{\theta = \frac{\pi}{3},\ \frac{5\pi}{3}}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{$r(t) = 5 + 2\cos t$ litres per hour. How much enters between $t = 0$ and $t = \pi$?}

\textbf{Solution:} Accumulated amount is the integral of the rate:
\[ \int_0^{\pi} (5 + 2\cos t)\,dt = \Big[5t + 2\sin t\Big]_0^{\pi} = 5\pi + 0. \]
So the reservoir gains \ans{5\pi \approx 15.708\ \text{L}}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{$x = \sin(2t)$, $y = t^2$. (a) Find $\frac{dx}{dt}$. (b) Find $\frac{dy}{dx}$ at $t = \frac{\pi}{6}$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} By the chain rule, \ans{\frac{dx}{dt} = 2\cos(2t)}

\textbf{Solution (b):} $\dfrac{dy}{dt} = 2t$, so $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{2t}{2\cos(2t)}$. At $t = \frac{\pi}{6}$ the denominator is $2\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = 1$ and the numerator is $\frac{\pi}{3}$. \ans{\frac{dy}{dx} \approx 1.047}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Area inside $r = 4\cos\theta$ and outside $r = 2$.}

\textbf{Solution:} The curves meet at $\theta = \pm\frac{\pi}{3}$, and between those angles $4\cos\theta \ge 2$, so
\[ A = \frac{1}{2}\int_{-\pi/3}^{\pi/3}\Big((4\cos\theta)^2 - 2^2\Big)d\theta
     = \Big[4\theta + 4\sin 2\theta\Big]_{0}^{\pi/3}. \]
Evaluating gives \ans{A = \frac{4\pi}{3} + 2\sqrt{3} \approx 7.653}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{$x(t) = 3t$, $y(t) = 2t^{3/2}$ on $[0,4]$, metres. Find the distance travelled.}

\textbf{Solution:} $x'(t) = 3$ and $y'(t) = 3\sqrt{t}$, so the speed is $\sqrt{9 + 9t} = 3\sqrt{1+t}$ and
\[ \int_0^4 3\sqrt{1+t}\,dt = 2\Big[(1+t)^{3/2}\Big]_0^4 = 2\big(5\sqrt{5} - 1\big). \]
So the particle travels \ans{10\sqrt{5} - 2 \approx 20.361\ \text{m}}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{$x'(t) = 4\cos t$, $y'(t) = 4\sin t$, starting at $(2,0)$. Find the position at $t = \frac{\pi}{2}$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Integrate each component from its starting value: $x(t) = 2 + 4\sin t$ and $y(t) = 4 - 4\cos t$. At $t = \frac{\pi}{2}$, $\sin = 1$ and $\cos = 0$, so the coordinates are $2 + 4$ and $4 - 0$. \ans{(x, y) = (6,\ 4)}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{$r = 1 + \cos\theta$ and $r = 3\cos\theta$. (a) Solve simultaneously. (b) Find $r$ there. (c) Why does the algebra miss the pole?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $1 + \cos\theta = 3\cos\theta$ gives $\cos\theta = \tfrac{1}{2}$, so on $[0, 2\pi)$ \ans{\theta = \frac{\pi}{3},\ \frac{5\pi}{3}}

\textbf{Solution (b):} At either angle, $r = 3\cos\frac{\pi}{3} = 3\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)$. \ans{r = 1.5}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} Both curves do pass through the pole, but at different parameter values: the cardioid reaches $r = 0$ at $\theta = \pi$, and the circle reaches $r = 0$ at $\theta = \frac{\pi}{2}$. Since no single $\theta$ makes both radii zero, the simultaneous equations cannot produce it. A polar point has many $(r, \theta)$ names, so the pole must always be checked separately. Full credit says both of those things.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{$r(t) = 12e^{-0.2t}$ litres per minute. (a) Amount in the first $5$ minutes. (b) When is the rate half its initial value?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \[ \int_0^5 12e^{-0.2t}\,dt = \Big[-60e^{-0.2t}\Big]_0^5 = 60 - 60e^{-1}. \]
So the pipe delivers \ans{60\left(1 - e^{-1}\right) \approx 37.93\ \text{L}}

\textbf{Solution (b):} The initial rate is $r(0) = 12$, so solve $12e^{-0.2t} = 6$, i.e. $e^{-0.2t} = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and $-0.2t = -\ln 2$. \ans{t = 5\ln 2 \approx 3.466\ \text{min}}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Velocity $\langle 2t,\ t^2 - 1\rangle$ m/s from the origin, $0 \le t \le 3$. (a) Distance. (b) Position at $t=3$. (c) Why is (a) larger?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The speed simplifies exactly:
\[ \sqrt{(2t)^2 + (t^2-1)^2} = \sqrt{t^4 + 2t^2 + 1} = t^2 + 1, \]
so $\displaystyle\int_0^3 (t^2+1)\,dt = \Big[\tfrac{t^3}{3} + t\Big]_0^3 = 9 + 3$. The distance is \ans{12\ \text{m}}

\textbf{Solution (b):} Starting from the origin, $x(t) = t^2$ and $y(t) = \tfrac{t^3}{3} - t$, so at $t = 3$ the coordinates are $9$ and $9 - 3$. \ans{(x, y) = (9,\ 6)}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} The straight-line distance from the origin to $(9,6)$ is $\sqrt{117} \approx 10.817$, less than $12$. The path is curved: integrating the speed adds up every centimetre actually travelled, while the displacement compares only the two endpoints. The two agree only when the motion is along a straight line in a single direction. Full credit makes that comparison.
