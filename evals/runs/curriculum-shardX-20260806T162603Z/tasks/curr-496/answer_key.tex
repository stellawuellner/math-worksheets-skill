\problem{Partial sums of $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(1/3)^n$ are $1,\ 1.3333,\ 1.4444,\ 1.4815$. Find the exact sum and say what the table suggests.}

\textbf{Solution:} The ratio is $r = \frac{1}{3}$ and the first term is $1$, so the series converges and
\[ S = \frac{\text{first term}}{1 - r} = \frac{1}{1 - \frac{1}{3}} = \frac{1}{\frac{2}{3}}. \]
So the sum is \ans{\frac{3}{2}}

\textbf{Written half --- instructor-judged.} The table shows the partial sums climbing and flattening toward about $1.5$, which is evidence for the limit but not a proof of it: no finite list of partial sums can rule out a later change of behaviour. The geometric-series formula is what proves the value. Full credit makes that distinction.

\problem{Find the exact sum of $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 1/n^2$.}

\textbf{Solution:} This is the $p$-series with $p = 2 > 1$, so it converges, and its sum is the standard value \ans{\frac{\pi^2}{6}}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{$\sum \frac{3n^2+1}{5n^2+2n}$. (a) Find the limit of the terms. (b) What does the nth-term test conclude?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Divide numerator and denominator by $n^2$: the terms become $\dfrac{3 + 1/n^2}{5 + 2/n}$, which tends to \ans{\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n = \frac{3}{5}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The nth-term test says: if $\lim a_n \neq 0$ then $\sum a_n$ diverges. Here the limit is $\frac{3}{5}$, so the series diverges. Had the limit been $0$, the test would have concluded nothing at all --- it never proves convergence, and $\sum \frac{1}{n}$ is the standing counterexample. Full credit states the test in that one direction and gives both halves.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{A ball dropped from $2$ m rebounds to $\frac{3}{5}$ of its previous height forever. Total vertical distance?}

\textbf{Solution:} The first drop is $2$ metres. After that, the $n$-th bounce contributes an up-and-down pair of $2 \cdot 2\left(\frac{3}{5}\right)^n = 4\left(\frac{3}{5}\right)^n$ metres, so the bouncing accounts for
\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 4\left(\frac{3}{5}\right)^{n} = \frac{4\cdot\frac{3}{5}}{1 - \frac{3}{5}}. \]
That bouncing distance is \ans{6\ \text{m}}

Adding the initial drop gives a total of \ans{8\ \text{m}}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Partial sums of $\sum 1/n^4$ are $1,\ 1.0625,\ 1.0748,\ 1.0788$. (a) Exact sum. (b) Why does it converge?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} This is the $p$-series with $p = 4$, whose sum is the standard value \ans{\frac{\pi^4}{90}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} A $p$-series $\sum \frac{1}{n^p}$ converges precisely when $p > 1$, and here $p = 4$. The table is that rule made visible: the terms shrink so fast that four of them already put the partial sum within $0.005$ of the limit, whereas the $p = 1$ series creeps upward forever. Full credit ties the exponent to what the table is doing.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{$\sum n\sin(1/n)$. (a) Find the limit of the terms. (b) State the conclusion and explain.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Substituting $u = 1/n$ turns the terms into $\dfrac{\sin u}{u}$ with $u \to 0$, a limit already known to be \ans{\lim_{n\to\infty} n\sin\!\left(\tfrac{1}{n}\right) = 1}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The terms approach $1$, not $0$, so by the nth-term test the series diverges. The reason they fail to shrink is that the two factors cancel: $\sin(1/n)$ shrinks like $1/n$, and the factor $n$ grows at exactly that rate, so the product settles at $1$. Full credit gives that cancellation, not just the limit.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{$0.\overline{45} = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 45/100^n$. (a) Write it as a fraction. (b) Why is every repeating decimal rational?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} First term $\frac{45}{100}$, ratio $\frac{1}{100}$:
\[ S = \frac{45/100}{1 - 1/100} = \frac{45}{99}. \]
In lowest terms that is \ans{\frac{5}{11}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} Any repeating decimal has a block of $k$ digits repeating, so it equals $\sum_{n\ge1} \frac{B}{10^{kn}}$ where $B$ is the block read as an integer. That is a geometric series with rational first term and rational ratio $10^{-k} < 1$, so it converges and its sum $\frac{B}{10^{k}-1}$ is a ratio of integers. Full credit gives the general argument, not a second example.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{(i) $\sum (2/3)^n$, (ii) $\sum 1/\sqrt{n}$, (iii) $\sum \frac{n}{n+4}$. (a) Sum (i). (b) Limit for (iii). (c) Classify all three.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Geometric with first term $1$ and $r = \frac{2}{3}$, so the sum is $\dfrac{1}{1 - 2/3}$. \ans{\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\tfrac{2}{3}\right)^{n} = 3}

\textbf{Solution (b):} Dividing through by $n$ gives $\dfrac{1}{1 + 4/n}$, so the limit is \ans{\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{n}{n+4} = 1}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} (i) converges: geometric with ratio $\frac{2}{3}$, and $|r| < 1$. (ii) diverges: it is the $p$-series with $p = \frac{1}{2} \le 1$. (iii) diverges: its terms approach $1$, not $0$, so the nth-term test applies. Full credit names the deciding quantity in each case --- the ratio, the exponent, the limit of the terms --- rather than only the verdict.
