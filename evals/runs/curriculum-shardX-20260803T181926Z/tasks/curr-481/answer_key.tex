\problem{\textbf{$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = x - y$ --- slope table.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The right-hand side is the slope. Substitute each point's own
coordinates, in the order $x$ then $y$; nothing is solved, only evaluated.

\textbf{Step 2.} At $(1,0)$: $1 - 0 = \ans{1}$

At $(2,-1)$: $2 - (-1) = \ans{3}$

At $(-1,2)$: $-1 - 2 = \ans{-3}$

At $(0,2)$: $0 - 2 = \ans{-2}$

\textbf{Step 3 (the drawing).} Segments of slope $1$, $3$, $-3$ and $-2$ at
those four points. Draw them short --- a slope field segment shows direction,
not length --- and steeper for the larger magnitudes.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $-1$ at the first point --- the subtraction was
done in the wrong order, as $y - x$.}

\problem{\textbf{$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 2 - y$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} There is no $x$ on the right-hand side, so the slope depends
only on the height. Every segment in a horizontal row is parallel.

\textbf{Step 2.} At height $y = 0$: $2 - 0 = \ans{2}$

At height $y = 3$: $2 - 3 = \ans{-1}$

At height $y = 4$: $2 - 4 = \ans{-2}$

\textbf{Step 3.} Horizontal segments need slope zero: $2 - y = 0$, so the
constant solution is the line $\ans{y = 2}$

\textbf{Step 4 (the drawing).} A row of horizontal segments along that line,
a row of gentle upward segments along the bottom, and rows tilting downward
more and more steeply above it.

\textbf{Reading it:} solutions below the line rise toward it and solutions
above it fall toward it, so the constant solution attracts everything.}

\problem{\textbf{Which equation made this field?}

\textbf{Step 1.} Every segment in a given vertical column of the printed field
has the same slope, and the slope changes as you move left or right. That is
the fingerprint of a right-hand side that contains $x$ and no $y$.

\textbf{Step 2.} In the column $x = 4$ the segments rise one unit for every two
across, a slope of $\ans{2}$; in the column $x = -2$ they fall half a unit per
unit across, a slope of $\ans{-1}$. Both match $\dfrac{x}{2}$ exactly.

\textbf{Step 3.} Setting $\dfrac{x}{2} = 0$ gives $\ans{x = 0}$, so the
horizontal segments lie along the whole $y$-axis --- which is exactly what the
picture shows.

\textbf{Step 4 (the justification --- manual review).} \emph{Full credit
says:} the field is $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{x}{2}$. It cannot be
$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{y}{2}$, because that field would be constant along
each horizontal row and its horizontal segments would run along the $x$-axis,
not the $y$-axis. It cannot be $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = x - y$, because that field
changes as you move straight up a column and its horizontal segments lie on a
slanted line.}

\problem{\textbf{$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 2x$ through $(1,2)$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The slope depends only on $x$, so each vertical column carries
one repeated segment: steeply falling on the far left, horizontal along the
$y$-axis, steeply rising on the far right.

\textbf{Step 2.} Antidifferentiate: the general antiderivative of $2x$ is
$\ans{x^2}$, so every solution has the form $y = x^2 + C$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Fix $C$ from the given point: $2 = 1^2 + C$, so $C = 1$ and
$y = x^2 + 1$. Check it at the given point: $y(1) = \ans{2}$ $\checkmark$

\textbf{Step 4.} At the bottom of the curve, $y(0) = \ans{1}$

\textbf{Step 5 (the sketch).} An upward parabola with its lowest point on the
$y$-axis one unit above the origin, riding tangent to the field's segments the
whole way. The family $y = x^2 + C$ is just this curve slid up and down --- and
that is why the field looks the same in every horizontal row.}

\problem{\textbf{The finished field for $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = x - y$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} At $(2,2)$ the two coordinates are equal, so the field's
segment is horizontal: slope $2 - 2 = \ans{0}$

\textbf{Step 2.} At $(3,0)$: $3 - 0 = \ans{3}$, a steeply rising segment ---
and the picture agrees.

\textbf{Step 3.} Horizontal segments need $x - y = 0$, that is $\ans{y = x}$
This is the diagonal line of flat segments running across the field.

\textbf{Step 4 (the description --- manual review).} \emph{Full credit says:}
the curve through $(0,3)$ starts steeply downward (slope $-3$ there), flattens
as it drops toward the diagonal, and from then on runs just below the line
$y = x - 1$, climbing with it. For large $x$ it is indistinguishable from that
line: the field pushes every solution toward the same slanted asymptote.}

\problem{\textbf{$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = x^2 - 1$ through $(0,1)$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The right-hand side is zero when $x^2 = 1$, so the field has
two whole columns of horizontal segments. Those columns are where the solution
curve turns.

\textbf{Step 2.} Antidifferentiate: $\ans{\frac{x^3}{3} - x}$ is the general
antiderivative, so $y = \dfrac{x^3}{3} - x + C$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Fix $C$ from the given point: $1 = 0 - 0 + C$, so $C = 1$
and $y = \dfrac{x^3}{3} - x + 1$. Check: $y(0) = \ans{1}$ $\checkmark$

\textbf{Step 4.} At the right edge: $y(2) = \dfrac{8}{3} - 2 + 1 =
\dfrac{5}{3}$, which to two decimal places is $\ans{1.67}$

\textbf{Step 5 (the sketch).} The curve rises to a local maximum in the
left-hand horizontal column, falls to a local minimum in the right-hand one,
then rises again --- exactly the shape the two flat columns force, and it
passes back up through about $1.67$ at the right edge.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $0.67$ --- the antiderivative was evaluated with
no constant, so the initial condition was never used.}

\problem{\textbf{$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = x + y$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Substitute each point. At $(1,1)$: $1 + 1 = \ans{2}$

At $(2,-2)$: $2 + (-2) = \ans{0}$

At $(-3,1)$: $-3 + 1 = \ans{-2}$

\textbf{Step 2.} Horizontal segments need $x + y = 0$, so they sit along
$\ans{y = -x}$, the falling diagonal through the origin.

\textbf{Step 3 (the drawing).} Around the origin the nine segments run from
slope $-2$ at the top left, through slope $0$ all along the falling diagonal,
to slope $2$ at the bottom right.

\textbf{Step 4.} The steepest segment on the grid is at the top right corner,
where $x$ and $y$ are both as large as the grid allows and the sum $x + y$ is
therefore the largest slope drawn. The bottom left corner is equally steep
downward --- the field is symmetric through the origin.}

\problem{\textbf{$\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{y-3}{2}$ --- equilibrium and
long-run behaviour.}

\textbf{Step 1.} There is no $x$ on the right, so the field is constant along
every horizontal row and the whole picture is decided by height.

\textbf{Step 2.} Setting $\dfrac{y-3}{2} = 0$ gives the equilibrium solution
$\ans{y = 3}$: a horizontal line of horizontal segments, and a genuine constant
solution of the differential equation.

\textbf{Step 3.} At height $y = 5$ the slope is $\dfrac{5-3}{2} = \ans{1}$,
positive --- above the line the field points upward, away.

At height $y = 1$ the slope is $\dfrac{1-3}{2} = \ans{-1}$, negative --- below
the line the field points downward, also away.

\textbf{Step 4 (the sketches and the explanation --- manual review).}
\emph{Full credit says:} the curve through $(0,5)$ starts with slope $1$ and
gets steeper as it climbs, running away upward; the curve through $(0,1)$
starts with slope $-1$ and steepens as it falls. Neither can reach the
equilibrium line, because approaching it means the slopes shrink toward zero
while pointing the wrong way --- the segments near the line push away from it,
not toward it. Equivalently, the constant function is itself a solution through
every point of that line, and two different solution curves cannot pass through
the same point.

\textit{Contrast with problem 2:} there the arrows pointed \emph{toward} the
constant solution. Same kind of picture, opposite long-run story --- the sign
of the coefficient on $y$ is what decides it.}
