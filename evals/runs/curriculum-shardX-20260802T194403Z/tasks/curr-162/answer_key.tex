\noindent\small Every solution uses the same relationship,
$\dfrac{\text{part}}{\text{whole}} = \dfrac{\text{percent}}{100}$. What changes from
problem to problem is \emph{which} of the three is missing. The first line of each
solution names it.\normalsize

\problem{\textbf{18 of 60 seats filled --- what percent?}\par
Part $=18$, whole $=60$, percent unknown.
\[\frac{18}{60}=\frac{p}{100}\quad\Rightarrow\quad 60p = 1800 \quad\Rightarrow\quad p = \frac{1800}{60}\]
Equivalently, $18 \div 60 = 0.3$ and $0.3 \times 100$ gives the rate. $\ans{30\%}$}

\problem{\textbf{21 of 25 correct --- what percent?}\par
Part $=21$, whole $=25$, percent unknown. Because $25 \times 4 = 100$, scale the whole
ratio up by 4 instead of dividing:
\[\frac{21}{25}=\frac{21\times 4}{25\times 4}=\frac{84}{100}\]
A denominator of 100 reads off as the percent directly. $\ans{84\%}$}

\problem{\textbf{40\% of the club is 12 members --- how many members?}\par
Here the \emph{percent} and the \emph{part} are known and the whole is missing, so the
unknown goes in the denominator:
\[\frac{12}{n}=\frac{40}{100}\quad\Rightarrow\quad 40n = 1200 \quad\Rightarrow\quad n = 30\]
Check: 40\% of 30 is $0.4 \times 30 = 12$ $\checkmark$. \ans{n = 30 \text{ members}}}

\problem{\textbf{36 of 45 penalties made --- what percent?}\par
Part $=36$, whole $=45$, percent unknown.
\[\frac{36}{45}=\frac{4}{5}=\frac{80}{100}\]
Simplifying first ($36/45$ divides by 9) turns this into a fifths problem, and fifths
convert to hundredths by multiplying by 20. $\ans{80\%}$}

\problem{\textbf{An 18-dollar discount is 15\% of the original price --- find the price.}\par
The 18 dollars is the part; 15 is the percent; the original price is the missing whole.
\[\frac{18}{n}=\frac{15}{100}\quad\Rightarrow\quad 15n = 1800 \quad\Rightarrow\quad n = 120\]
Check: 15\% of 120 is $0.15 \times 120 = 18$ $\checkmark$. \ans{n = 120 \text{ dollars}}}

\problem{\textbf{45 of 250 surveyed chose blue --- what percent?}\par
Part $=45$, whole $=250$, percent unknown. Since $250 \times 4 = 1000$, scaling by 4 lands
on thousandths, so halve once more --- or just divide:
\[\frac{45}{250}=0.18 = \frac{18}{100}\]
$\ans{18\%}$}

\problem{\textbf{6 cracked tiles is 3\% of the shipment --- how many tiles?}\par
Small percent, small part, missing whole:
\[\frac{6}{n}=\frac{3}{100}\quad\Rightarrow\quad 3n = 600 \quad\Rightarrow\quad n = 200\]
Sanity check on size: 3\% is a tiny slice, so the whole must be far bigger than 6 --- and
200 is. Check: $0.03 \times 200 = 6$ $\checkmark$. \ans{n = 200 \text{ tiles}}}

\problem{\textbf{Sales rose from 250 loaves to 300 --- what percent increase?}\par
For a percent \emph{change}, the part is the amount of change and the whole is always the
\textbf{original} amount. Change $= 300 - 250 = 50$; original $= 250$.
\[\frac{50}{250}=\frac{20}{100}\]
$\ans{20\% \text{ increase}}$\par
\textit{Common wrong move:} using the new amount, 300, as the whole. That gives about
16.7\%, which answers a different question.}

\problem{\textbf{5.40 dollars of tax on an 80-dollar purchase --- what rate?}\par
The tax is the part and the purchase is the whole:
\[\frac{5.40}{80}=0.0675 = \frac{6.75}{100}\]
A tax rate below 10\% should come out as a small percent; 6.75 is reasonable.
$\ans{6.75\%}$}

\problem{\textbf{155 yes votes is 62\% of the voters --- how many voted?}\par
Part $=155$, percent $=62$, whole missing:
\[\frac{155}{n}=\frac{62}{100}\quad\Rightarrow\quad 62n = 15500 \quad\Rightarrow\quad n = 250\]
Check: $0.62 \times 250 = 155$ $\checkmark$. Note the whole is larger than the part, as it
must be whenever the percent is under 100. \ans{n = 250 \text{ students}}}

\problem{\textbf{Price fell from 96 dollars to 72 --- what percent decrease?}\par
Percent change again, so the whole is the original price, 96. Change $= 96 - 72 = 24$.
\[\frac{24}{96}=\frac{1}{4}=\frac{25}{100}\]
$\ans{25\% \text{ decrease}}$\par
\textit{Note:} raising 72 back to 96 would be a $24/72 = 33.3\%$ increase, not 25\% --- the
two percents differ because they are measured against different wholes.}

\problem{\textbf{After a 20\% increase the gym has 168 members --- how many before?}\par
This is the synthesis problem: 168 is \emph{not} the part that was added. Last year's
membership is 100\% of itself, and this year is that plus 20\% more, so 168 corresponds to
120\%.
\[\frac{168}{n}=\frac{120}{100}\quad\Rightarrow\quad 120n = 16800 \quad\Rightarrow\quad n = 140\]
Check: 20\% of 140 is 28, and $140 + 28 = 168$ $\checkmark$. \ans{n = 140 \text{ members}}\par
\textit{Common wrong move:} taking 20\% of 168 and subtracting, which gives 134.4 --- that
removes 20\% of the \emph{new} total instead of undoing 20\% of the old one.}
