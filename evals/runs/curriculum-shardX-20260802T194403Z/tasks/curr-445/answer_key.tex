\problem{\textbf{Numerical --- read the trend, both sides.}

\textbf{From the left:} $5.9,\ 5.99,\ 5.999$. \textbf{From the right:}
$6.1,\ 6.01,\ 6.001$. Both columns close in on the same number, so the limit is
that number.
\ans{6}

\textbf{Expected sentence:} the limit describes where $f$ is heading as $x$
approaches $3$, and the definition deliberately never evaluates $f$ at $3$
itself. Since $f(x)=x+3$ for every $x\neq 3$, the approach is perfectly ordinary
even though $f(3)$ is undefined --- a hole in the graph, not a break in the
trend.}

\problem{\textbf{Algebraic --- factor and cancel.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Substitution gives $\tfrac{0}{0}$, an indeterminate form, so
rewrite rather than conclude.

\textbf{Step 2.} Difference of squares:
$\dfrac{x^2-16}{x-4}=\dfrac{(x-4)(x+4)}{x-4}=x+4$ for $x\neq 4$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Now substitution is legal: $4+4$.
\ans{8}

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $0$ --- reading $\tfrac{0}{0}$ as a value. It is
not one; it is the signal that a common factor is hiding in both parts.}

\problem{\textbf{Graphical --- a hole does not stop a limit.}

\textbf{Read the graph.} As $x$ moves toward $2$ from the left the curve rises
toward height $4$; from the right it falls toward the same height $4$. Both
sides agree.
\ans{4}

\textbf{Expected sentence:} the open circle records that $g(2)$ is undefined,
which is a statement about the single point $x=2$. The limit is about the
values of $g$ at inputs \emph{near} $2$, and those are unaffected. Algebraically
$g(x)=x+2$ for $x\neq2$, which is why the graph is a straight line with one
point removed.}

\problem{\textbf{Series --- a finite partial sum.}

\textbf{Expand the sigma notation} by substituting $n=1,2,3,4,5$:
\[
\sum_{n=1}^{5}(2n-1)=1+3+5+7+9
\]
\textbf{Add:} $1+3=4$, $+5=9$, $+7=16$, $+9=25$.
\ans{25}

\textit{Worth noticing:} the running totals are $1,4,9,16,25$ --- the perfect
squares. The $k$th partial sum of the odd numbers is $k^2$.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $16$ --- the sum stopped at $n=4$. The upper limit
of the sigma is inclusive, so $n=5$ contributes a term too.}

\problem{\textbf{Numerical --- build the table, then name the exact value.}

\textbf{Left entry ($x=-0.01$):}
$\dfrac{\sqrt{0.99}-1}{-0.01}=\dfrac{-0.0050126\ldots}{-0.01}=0.5012562\ldots$
\ans{0.5013}

\textbf{Right entry ($x=0.01$):}
$\dfrac{\sqrt{1.01}-1}{0.01}=\dfrac{0.0049876\ldots}{0.01}=0.4987562\ldots$
\ans{0.4988}

\textbf{The exact limit.} The two entries squeeze in on $0.5$ from opposite
sides. Confirm it algebraically with the conjugate:
$\dfrac{\sqrt{x+1}-1}{x}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt{x+1}+1}{\sqrt{x+1}+1}
=\dfrac{x}{x(\sqrt{x+1}+1)}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x+1}+1}$, which at $x=0$ is
$\dfrac{1}{1+1}$.
\ans{\tfrac{1}{2}}

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $2$ --- the denominator $\sqrt{1}+1$ reported
instead of the whole fraction. The table rules it out immediately: the entries
sit near $0.5$, not near $2$.

\textit{Grading note:} the table alone is \emph{evidence}, not proof --- it can
never rule out something happening between the sampled points. The algebra is
what settles it.}

\problem{\textbf{Algebraic --- cubes over squares.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Substitution gives $\tfrac{0}{0}$; both top and bottom vanish
at $x=2$, so both carry a factor of $(x-2)$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Factor each:
\[
\frac{x^3-8}{x^2-4}=\frac{(x-2)(x^2+2x+4)}{(x-2)(x+2)}
=\frac{x^2+2x+4}{x+2}\quad (x\neq 2)
\]
\textbf{Step 3.} Substitute $x=2$: $\dfrac{4+4+4}{4}=\dfrac{12}{4}$.
\ans{3}

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $2$ --- from factoring the cube as
$(x-2)(x^2+4)$, dropping the middle term $2x$. The cube pattern is
$a^3-b^3=(a-b)(a^2+ab+b^2)$; the middle term is not optional.}

\problem{\textbf{Graphical --- one-sided limits and a jump.}

\textbf{(a) From the left} the graph follows the line through $(0,1)$ rising to
the open circle at height $2$:
\ans{2}

\textbf{(b) From the right} the graph starts at the solid dot at height $3$ and
falls away:
\ans{3}

\textbf{(c) Manual review --- expected justification.} The two-sided limit
exists only when both one-sided limits exist \emph{and} are equal. Here they are
$2$ and $3$, which are not equal, so $\lim_{x\to1}h(x)$ does \textbf{not}
exist. Accept any answer that compares the two one-sided values; do not accept
``because the graph breaks'' with no reference to them. Note also that
$h(1)=3$ exists --- a function can have a value at a point where the limit does
not, which is the mirror image of problem 3.}

\problem{\textbf{Series --- an infinite geometric sum.}

\textbf{Identify the pieces.} First term $a=3$ (take $n=0$), common ratio
$r=\tfrac{1}{2}$, and $|r|<1$, so the series converges.

\textbf{Apply the formula} $S=\dfrac{a}{1-r}$:
$S=\dfrac{3}{1-\tfrac{1}{2}}=\dfrac{3}{\tfrac{1}{2}}$.
\ans{6}

\textbf{The partial-sum limit it represents.}
$S_k=\displaystyle\sum_{n=0}^{k}3\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n}
=6\left(1-\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{k+1}\right)$, and the value above is
$\lim_{k\to\infty}S_k$. The sum of an infinite series is defined as that limit.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $3$ --- from taking the $n=1$ term,
$\tfrac{3}{2}$, as the first term. The index starts at $n=0$, so $a=3$.}

\problem{\textbf{Algebraic --- rationalize the numerator.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Substitution gives $\tfrac{0}{0}$, and the trouble is a
radical, so multiply by the conjugate of the numerator.

\textbf{Step 2.}
\[
\frac{\sqrt{x+9}-3}{x}\cdot\frac{\sqrt{x+9}+3}{\sqrt{x+9}+3}
=\frac{(x+9)-9}{x(\sqrt{x+9}+3)}=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x+9}+3}\quad (x\neq 0)
\]
\textbf{Step 3.} Substitute $x=0$: $\dfrac{1}{3+3}$.
\ans{\tfrac{1}{6}}}

\problem{\textbf{Numerical --- end behaviour from a table.}

\textbf{At $x=100$:} $\dfrac{2(100)+3}{100-1}=\dfrac{203}{99}=2.0505050\ldots$
\ans{2.0505}

\textbf{At $x=1000$:} $\dfrac{2(1000)+3}{1000-1}=\dfrac{2003}{999}=2.0050050\ldots$
\ans{2.0050}

\textbf{The limit.} The values are marching down toward $2$. Algebraically,
divide top and bottom by $x$: $\dfrac{2+3/x}{1-1/x}$, and both $3/x$ and $1/x$
go to $0$ as $x$ grows.
\ans{2}

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $0.5$ --- the leading coefficients divided the
wrong way round. The table is the check: its entries sit just above $2$.}

\problem{\textbf{Graphical then algebraic --- a horizontal asymptote.}

\textbf{Read the graph.} Far out in either direction the curve flattens against
the dashed line, so the limit is the height of that line.
\ans{3}

\textbf{Confirm algebraically.} Divide numerator and denominator by $x^2$:
\[
\frac{3x^2+1}{x^2+2}=\frac{3+\dfrac{1}{x^2}}{1+\dfrac{2}{x^2}}
\;\longrightarrow\; \frac{3+0}{1+0}
\]
\textit{Common wrong answer:} $0.5$ --- the height where the curve crosses the
$y$-axis. That is $p(0)$, a single point, not the end behaviour.

\textit{Teaching note:} a horizontal asymptote \emph{is} a limit statement about
end behaviour. The curve may cross an asymptote elsewhere; what matters is where
it settles.}

\problem{\textbf{Synthesis --- the sum of a series is a limit.}

\textbf{(a) The fifth partial sum.}
$S_5=\tfrac{1}{2}+\tfrac{1}{4}+\tfrac{1}{8}+\tfrac{1}{16}+\tfrac{1}{32}$. Over
the common denominator $32$ this is $\tfrac{16+8+4+2+1}{32}$.
\ans{\tfrac{31}{32}}

\textbf{(b) The infinite sum.} Geometric with $a=\tfrac{1}{2}$ and
$r=\tfrac{1}{2}$, so $S=\dfrac{1/2}{1-1/2}$.
\ans{1}

\textit{Common wrong answer to (b):} $2$ --- from using $a=1$ in
$\tfrac{a}{1-r}$. The first term here is the $n=1$ term, $\tfrac{1}{2}$.

\textbf{(c) Manual review --- expected explanation.} The running totals are
$\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{3}{4},\tfrac{7}{8},\tfrac{15}{16},\tfrac{31}{32}$: each one
closes half of the remaining gap to $1$, so $S_k=1-\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^k$
and no partial sum ever reaches $1$. Numerically the totals approach $1$;
graphically the plotted partial sums flatten against the line $y=1$;
algebraically $\lim_{k\to\infty}S_k=1$. The sum of an infinite series is
\emph{defined} to be that limit --- not the result of an endless addition, which
is why an infinite sum can be a finite number at all. Accept any answer that
names the sum as a limit of partial sums; do not accept ``you keep adding
forever''.}
