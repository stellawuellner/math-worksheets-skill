\problem{\textbf{Zara jogs $\frac{1}{2}$ mile in $\frac{1}{4}$ hour --- miles
per hour?}

\textbf{Step 1.} ``Per hour'' means the time has to become one hour, so divide
the distance by the time: $\frac{1}{2} \div \frac{1}{4}$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Dividing by a fraction is multiplying by its reciprocal:
$\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{4}{1} = \frac{4}{2}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} In lowest terms that is $2$ miles per hour. \ans{2}

\textit{Sense check:} a quarter of an hour is four times a shorter stretch
than an hour, so the distance should be four times bigger --- and it is.}

\problem{\textbf{$\frac{3}{4}$ of a page in $\frac{1}{2}$ of a minute ---
pages per minute?}

\textbf{Step 1.} The unit here is one minute, so divide the pages by the
minutes: $\frac{3}{4} \div \frac{1}{2}$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Multiply by the reciprocal: $\frac{3}{4} \times \frac{2}{1}
= \frac{6}{4}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Reduce: $\frac{6}{4} = \frac{3}{2}$ pages per minute, which
is one and a half pages. \ans{\tfrac{3}{2}}

\textit{Listen for:} a student who says the answer must be smaller than
$\frac{3}{4}$ because it is a division. Dividing by a number below one makes
things bigger.}

\problem{\textbf{$\frac{2}{3}$ of a litre in $\frac{1}{6}$ of a minute ---
litres per minute?}

\textbf{Step 1.} Divide the litres by the minutes: $\frac{2}{3} \div
\frac{1}{6}$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\frac{2}{3} \times \frac{6}{1} = \frac{12}{3}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} That reduces to $4$ litres per minute. \ans{4}

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $\frac{1}{4}$ --- the division was written upside
down, minutes over litres. Ask what the answer would mean: a quarter of a
minute per litre is a different question.}

\problem{\textbf{$1\frac{1}{2}$ cups of flour make $\frac{3}{4}$ of a batch ---
cups per batch?}

\textbf{Step 1.} One whole batch is the unit, so divide the flour by the part
of a batch it made. First rewrite the mixed number: $1\frac{1}{2} =
\frac{3}{2}$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\frac{3}{2} \div \frac{3}{4} = \frac{3}{2} \times
\frac{4}{3} = \frac{12}{6}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} That is $2$ cups of flour for a whole batch. \ans{2}

\textit{Teaching note:} the mixed number must become improper before the
reciprocal step. Flipping $1\frac{1}{2}$ as it stands is the classic slip.}

\problem{\textbf{Rowan $\frac{3}{5}$ km in $\frac{1}{4}$ h; Sadie
$\frac{2}{3}$ km in $\frac{1}{3}$ h.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The two walks cover different distances in different times,
so nothing can be compared until both are written as kilometres per one hour.

\textbf{Step 2.} Rowan: $\frac{3}{5} \div \frac{1}{4} = \frac{3}{5} \times
\frac{4}{1} = \frac{12}{5}$ km/h. \ans{\tfrac{12}{5}}

\textbf{Step 3.} Sadie: $\frac{2}{3} \div \frac{1}{3} = \frac{2}{3} \times
\frac{3}{1} = \frac{6}{3} = 2$ km/h. \ans{2}

\textbf{Step 4.} $\frac{12}{5}$ is $2.4$, and $2.4$ is more than $2$, so
Rowan's speed is the greater of the two: \ans{>}

\textit{Teaching note:} Sadie covers more ground per trip, so a student who
compares the distances alone picks the wrong walker. The rate is what settles
it.}

\problem{\textbf{$\frac{5}{8}$ of a mile in $\frac{5}{6}$ of an hour --- miles
per hour?}

\textbf{Step 1.} Divide the distance by the time: $\frac{5}{8} \div
\frac{5}{6}$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\frac{5}{8} \times \frac{6}{5} = \frac{30}{40}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Reduce by $10$: the unit rate is $\frac{3}{4}$ of a mile per
hour. \ans{\tfrac{3}{4}}

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $\frac{4}{3}$ --- the reciprocal was taken on the
wrong fraction, giving hours per mile instead of miles per hour. A unit rate
below the distance is fine here: the trip took most of an hour, so one full
hour covers only a little more.}

\problem{\textbf{A hose fills $\frac{7}{8}$ of a barrel in $\frac{1}{2}$ of an
hour.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The question asks per hour, so divide barrels by hours:
$\frac{7}{8} \div \frac{1}{2}$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\frac{7}{8} \times \frac{2}{1} = \frac{14}{8}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Reduce: $\frac{7}{4}$ of a barrel per hour, which is one and
three quarters barrels. \ans{\tfrac{7}{4}}

\textit{Sense check:} half an hour filled most of a barrel, so a full hour
should fill more than one. It does.}

\problem{\textbf{$\frac{3}{4}$ of a pound of cheese costs $6$ dollars ---
dollars per pound?}

\textbf{Step 1.} The unit is one pound, so divide the cost by the weight:
$6 \div \frac{3}{4}$.

\textbf{Step 2.} A whole number divides the same way: $6 \times \frac{4}{3}
= \frac{24}{3}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} That is $8$ dollars for one pound. \ans{8}

\textit{Sense check:} three quarters of a pound cost six dollars, so a whole
pound must cost more than six --- eight is believable, four would not be.}

\problem{\textbf{Painting $\frac{2}{5}$ of a wall takes $\frac{3}{4}$ of an
hour.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Per hour means walls divided by hours: $\frac{2}{5} \div
\frac{3}{4}$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\frac{2}{5} \times \frac{4}{3} = \frac{8}{15}$, which will
not reduce because $8$ and $15$ share no factor.

\textbf{Step 3.} In one hour, $\frac{8}{15}$ of the wall gets painted.
\ans{\tfrac{8}{15}}

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $\frac{15}{8}$ --- hours over wall instead of
wall over hours. That number answers ``how many hours for the whole wall'',
which is a fair question but not this one.}

\problem{\textbf{Brand A: $\frac{2}{3}$ lb for $2$ dollars. Brand B:
$\frac{3}{4}$ lb for $3$ dollars.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The packs are different sizes, so the sticker prices cannot
be compared. Put both on the same footing: dollars for one pound.

\textbf{Step 2.} Brand A: $2 \div \frac{2}{3} = 2 \times \frac{3}{2} = 3$
dollars per pound. \ans{3}

\textbf{Step 3.} Brand B: $3 \div \frac{3}{4} = 3 \times \frac{4}{3} = 4$
dollars per pound. \ans{4}

\textbf{Step 4.} Three dollars a pound is less than four, so brand A is the
better buy and the true statement uses the less-than sign: \ans{<}

\textit{Teaching note:} brand A is also the cheaper sticker price here, so ask
the follow-up: what would happen if brand B's pack cost $2.50$? The unit rate
is what makes that answerable.}

\problem{\textbf{A cyclist rides $4\frac{1}{2}$ miles in $\frac{3}{4}$ of an
hour.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Rewrite the mixed number first: $4\frac{1}{2} = \frac{9}{2}$
miles. Then divide by the time.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\frac{9}{2} \div \frac{3}{4} = \frac{9}{2} \times
\frac{4}{3} = \frac{36}{6}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} That is $6$ miles per hour. \ans{6}

\textit{Sense check:} three quarters of an hour covered four and a half miles,
so a full hour covers a bit more --- six is right, and anything under four and
a half would be wrong on sight.}

\problem{\textbf{$2\frac{1}{4}$ litres in $\frac{1}{3}$ of a minute --- how
many litres in $4$ minutes?}

\textbf{Step 1.} This is two steps. The machine's rate per minute is not
given, so find the unit rate first, then multiply it by the number of minutes
asked for.

\textbf{Step 2.} Unit rate: $2\frac{1}{4} = \frac{9}{4}$, and $\frac{9}{4}
\div \frac{1}{3} = \frac{9}{4} \times \frac{3}{1} = \frac{27}{4}$ litres per
minute.

\textbf{Step 3.} Now scale up: $\frac{27}{4} \times 4 = 27$ litres in four
minutes. \ans{27}

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $9$ --- the given $2\frac{1}{4}$ litres was
treated as the per-minute rate and multiplied by four. But that amount took
only a third of a minute, so the rate is three times bigger.}
