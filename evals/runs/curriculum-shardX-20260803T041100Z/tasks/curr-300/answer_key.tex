\problem{\textbf{Tutoring price table --- cost of 6 hours.}
\par
Evidence first. The first differences are $40-25 = 15$, $55-40 = 15$,
$70-55 = 15$ --- constant, so the model is linear.
\par
The value at $h = 0$ is the start-up charge, $25$ dollars, and the constant
increase is the rate, $15$ dollars per hour:
\[ C(h) = 25 + 15h \]
Evaluate at $h = 6$: $\;25 + 15(6) = 25 + 90 = 115$.
\par
$\ans{115}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Common wrong answer 240:} the one-hour total of $40$ was multiplied by
$6$. That charges the $25$ dollar start-up fee six times. Only the \emph{rate}
repeats; the intercept happens once.}

\problem{\textbf{Tutoring --- how many hours cost 175 dollars?}
\par
Same linear model, now solved rather than evaluated:
\begin{align*}
  25 + 15h &= 175 \\
  15h &= 150 \\
  h &= 10
\end{align*}
\par
$\ans{h = 10}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Check:} $25 + 15(10) = 175$ $\checkmark$}

\problem{\textbf{Ball height at $t = 3.5$ seconds.}
\par
Evidence: first differences $48$, $16$, $-16$ are not constant, but the second
differences are $16 - 48 = -32$ and $-16 - 16 = -32$ --- constant. Constant
second differences mean a quadratic model, which is why
$h(t) = -16t^2 + 64t$ is the right form.
\par
Substitute $t = 3.5$:
\[ h(3.5) = -16(3.5)^2 + 64(3.5) = -16(12.25) + 224 = -196 + 224 = 28 \]
\par
$\ans{28 \text{ ft}}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Common wrong answer 168 ft:} the first step of $48$ feet was used as a
constant rate, giving $48 \times 3.5$. A linear extrapolation from a quadratic
table runs away from the truth quickly --- here it puts the ball six times too
high, and above its own maximum.}

\problem{\textbf{When is the ball at ground level?}
\par
Ground level means $h(t) = 0$. Factor rather than expand:
\begin{align*}
  -16t^2 + 64t &= 0 \\
  -16t(t - 4) &= 0
\end{align*}
The zero-product property gives $t = 0$ or $t = 4$.
\par
$\ans{t = 0 \text{ or } t = 4}$
\par\smallskip
Both roots are meaningful here: $t = 0$ is the launch and $t = 4$ is the
landing. The vertex sits halfway between them at $t = 2$, which matches the
table's maximum of $64$ ft.}

\problem{\textbf{Parking garage --- 5 hours.}
\par
The rule changes at a stated boundary, so the model is piecewise: a flat $4$
dollars covers the first $2$ hours, and only the hours \emph{past} 2 are
charged at $3$ dollars each.
\par
Hours past the boundary: $5 - 2 = 3$.
\[ 4 + 3(5 - 2) = 4 + 9 = 13 \]
\par
$\ans{13}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Common wrong answer 19:} the hourly rate was applied to all five hours,
$4 + 3(5)$. The flat fee already pays for the first two hours; charging them
again is the classic piecewise slip.}

\problem{\textbf{Pool depth.}
\par
(a) First differences: $12-7 = 5$, $17-12 = 5$, $22-17 = 5$. Constant, so the
model is linear with slope $5$.
\par
At $x = 1$ the depth is $7$, so $7 = 5(1) + b$ and $b = 2$:
\[ D(x) = 5x + 2 \]
At $x = 10$: $\;5(10) + 2 = 52$.
\par
$\ans{52}$
\par
(b) Model answer: the table's \emph{first} differences are constant --- the
depth rises by exactly $5$ cm every minute. A quadratic model would show first
differences that change from step to step, with the \emph{second} differences
constant instead. Since the first differences never change, no $x^2$ term is
needed, and a straight line fits every point in the table.
\par\smallskip
\emph{Manual review:} part (b) is a written justification, read by a teacher;
part (a) is machine-checked. Credit an answer that names constant first
differences and contrasts them with what a quadratic table would look like.}

\problem{\textbf{Parking garage --- a 22 dollar ticket.}
\par
Same piecewise rule, solved for the input. A $22$ dollar charge is well past
the flat-fee region, so the second piece applies:
\begin{align*}
  4 + 3(t - 2) &= 22 \\
  3(t - 2) &= 18 \\
  t - 2 &= 6 \\
  t &= 8
\end{align*}
\par
$\ans{t = 8}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Check the piece:} $8 > 2$, so the second piece really is the one in
force. On a piecewise problem the answer is only valid if it lands in the
interval whose formula produced it.}

\problem{\textbf{Hoodie revenue of 400 dollars.}
\par
Revenue is price times quantity, and the quantity itself depends on the price
--- a product of two linear expressions, which is exactly what makes the model
quadratic.
\begin{align*}
  p(60 - 2p) &= 400 \\
  60p - 2p^2 &= 400 \\
  -2p^2 + 60p - 400 &= 0 \\
  p^2 - 30p + 200 &= 0 \\
  (p - 10)(p - 20) &= 0
\end{align*}
\par
$\ans{p = 10 \text{ or } p = 20}$
\par\smallskip
Two prices give the same revenue --- a low price selling many hoodies
($10 \times 40$) and a high price selling few ($20 \times 20$). A linear model
could never produce two answers, which is itself evidence the model is
quadratic.}

\problem{\textbf{Two gyms --- when do the plans cost the same?}
\par
Both stories name a fixed fee plus a fixed monthly rate, so both models are
linear and the question is where they meet:
\[ \begin{cases} y = 20 + 8x \\ y = 44 + 5x \end{cases} \]
Substitute the first into the second:
\begin{align*}
  20 + 8x &= 44 + 5x \\
  3x &= 24 \\
  x &= 8
\end{align*}
Then $y = 20 + 8(8) = 84$, and the check on the other model gives
$44 + 5(8) = 84$ $\checkmark$
\par
$\ans{x = 8, \; y = 84}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Reading the answer:} before 8 months Gym A is cheaper (its joining fee is
smaller); after 8 months Gym B is cheaper (its rate is smaller). The
intersection is where the advice changes.}

\problem{\textbf{Where a quadratic model and a linear model agree.}
\par
Agreement means equal outputs at the same input, so set the two models equal
and solve:
\begin{align*}
  x^2 - 4x + 7 &= 2x + 2 \\
  x^2 - 6x + 5 &= 0 \\
  (x - 1)(x - 5) &= 0
\end{align*}
So $x = 1$ or $x = 5$. Substitute each into the \emph{linear} model, which is
the easier of the two:
\par
$x = 1$: $\;y = 2(1) + 2 = 4$. \qquad $x = 5$: $\;y = 2(5) + 2 = 12$.
\par
$\ans{(1,\ 4) \text{ and } (5,\ 12)}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Check in the quadratic:} $1 - 4 + 7 = 4$ $\checkmark$ and
$25 - 20 + 7 = 12$ $\checkmark$. A line and a parabola can meet twice, once, or
not at all --- getting two points here is normal, not an error.}

\problem{\textbf{Population --- finding Rae's mistake.}
\par
What Rae's evidence actually shows: the first differences are
$9-5 = 4$, $17-9 = 8$, $29-17 = 12$. They are \emph{not} constant, so the
growth is not at a fixed rate and no linear model fits. The second differences
are $8-4 = 4$ and $12-8 = 4$ --- constant, which is the signature of a
quadratic model. Rae used the very first difference as though it held forever.
\par
With the correct model $P(t) = 2t^2 + 2t + 5$:
\[ P(6) = 2(36) + 2(6) + 5 = 72 + 12 + 5 = 89 \]
\par
$\ans{89}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Rae's answer, 29, is exactly $5 + 4 \times 6$} --- the linear
extrapolation. It also happens to be the year-3 population, so a student who
sanity-checks against the table should notice the model has stopped growing.}

\problem{\textbf{Synthesis --- the rideshare fare.}
\par
The rule changes at $6$ miles, so the model is piecewise:
\[ F(m) = \begin{cases} 3 + 2m, & 0 \le m \le 6 \\[2pt]
   3 + 12 + 1.25(m - 6), & m > 6 \end{cases} \]
(a) A $10$ mile ride is past the boundary, so use the second piece. The first
$6$ miles cost $2 \times 6 = 12$ dollars, and the remaining
$10 - 6 = 4$ miles cost $1.25$ each:
\[ 3 + 12 + 1.25(10 - 6) = 3 + 12 + 5 = 20 \]
\par
$\ans{20}$
\par
(b) A $27.50$ dollar fare is larger than the $15$ dollar fare at the boundary,
so the second piece is again in force:
\begin{align*}
  3 + 12 + 1.25(m - 6) &= 27.5 \\
  1.25(m - 6) &= 12.5 \\
  m - 6 &= 10 \\
  m &= 16
\end{align*}
\par
$\ans{m = 16}$
\par
(c) Model answer: a linear function has one constant rate of change everywhere,
but this fare has two --- $2$ dollars per mile up to $6$ miles and $1.25$
dollars per mile after that. No single slope can be both. The graph is two
connected straight segments: a steeper one from $(0, 3)$ to $(6, 15)$, then a
shallower one continuing from $(6, 15)$ onward. The two segments meet without a
gap, so the fare is continuous, but the bend at $6$ miles is a corner, and that
corner is exactly what a single line cannot have.
\par\smallskip
\emph{Manual review:} part (c) is written reasoning, read by a teacher; parts
(a) and (b) are machine-checked. Credit an answer that names the two different
rates and describes a bend at the $6$ mile boundary.}
