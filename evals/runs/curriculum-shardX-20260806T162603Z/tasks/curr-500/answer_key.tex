\problem{$f(x) = e^{2x}$, $P_3(x) = 1 + 2x + 2x^2 + \frac{4}{3}x^3$. (a) Find $f'''$. (b) Estimate $f(0.1)$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Each differentiation brings down a factor of $2$, so after three: \ans{f'''(x) = 8e^{2x}}

That matches the cubic coefficient: $f'''(0)/3! = 8/6 = \frac43$.

\textbf{Solution (b):} $P_3(0.1) = 1 + 0.2 + 0.02 + \frac43(0.001)$. \ans{P_3(0.1) \approx 1.22133}

\problem{$x\cos x \approx x - \frac{x^3}{2} + \frac{x^5}{24}$. Estimate the value at $x = 0.4$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Multiplying the cosine series by $x$ shifts every exponent up by one, giving those three terms. At $x = 0.4$:
\[ 0.4 - \frac{0.064}{2} + \frac{0.01024}{24} = 0.4 - 0.032 + 0.00042667. \]
So the estimate is \ans{0.36843}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Lagrange bound for $P_3$ of $e^x$ at $x = 1$ on $[0,1]$. (a) The bound. (b) Why $M = e$?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $|R_3(1)| \le \dfrac{M\,|1-0|^{4}}{4!} = \dfrac{e}{24}$. \ans{\frac{e}{24} \approx 0.11326}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} $M$ must bound $|f^{(4)}|$ over the whole interval. Every derivative of $e^{x}$ is $e^{x}$ again, and $e^{x}$ is increasing, so on $[0,1]$ its largest value is $e^{1} = e$. Full credit justifies the choice rather than quoting a number.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{$f(x) = \ln x$ about $a = 1$. (a) Find $f'''$. (b) Estimate $\ln(1.2)$ with $P_3$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $f'(x) = x^{-1}$, $f''(x) = -x^{-2}$, $f'''(x) = 2x^{-3}$. \ans{f'''(x) = \frac{2}{x^{3}}}

At $a = 1$ this is $2$, and $2/3! = \frac13$, the cubic coefficient shown.

\textbf{Solution (b):} With $x - 1 = 0.2$: \; $0.2 - \dfrac{0.04}{2} + \dfrac{0.008}{3}$. \ans{P_3(1.2) \approx 0.18267}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{$\frac{1}{1+3x^2} \approx 1 - 3x^2 + 9x^4$. Estimate the value at $x = 0.2$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Substituting $u = -3x^{2}$ into the geometric series gives those terms. At $x = 0.2$:
\[ 1 - 3(0.04) + 9(0.0016) = 1 - 0.12 + 0.0144. \]
So the estimate is \ans{0.8944}

(The true value is $1/1.12 \approx 0.892857$; the next term, $-27x^{6}$, accounts for most of the gap.)

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{$\cos(0.5)$ estimated through the $x^4$ term. (a) Alternating error bound. (b) What did you check?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The first omitted term is the $x^{6}$ term, of size $\dfrac{0.5^{6}}{6!} = \dfrac{0.015625}{720}$. \ans{\approx 0.0000217}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The alternating bound requires two things: the series alternates in sign at this input, and the term sizes $\frac{0.5^{2k}}{(2k)!}$ decrease to zero. Both hold for the cosine series at $x = 0.5$, so the remainder is at most the first omitted term. Full credit checks both conditions before quoting the bound.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{$f(x) = \sqrt{x}$ about $a = 4$. (a) Find $f''$. (b) Estimate $\sqrt{4.4}$ with $P_2$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $f'(x) = \frac12 x^{-1/2}$ and $f''(x) = -\frac14 x^{-3/2}$, i.e. \ans{f''(x) = -\frac{1}{4x\sqrt{x}}}

At $a = 4$ this is $-\frac{1}{32}$, and $-\frac{1}{32}/2! = -\frac{1}{64}$, the quadratic coefficient shown.

\textbf{Solution (b):} With $x - 4 = 0.4$: \; $2 + \dfrac{0.4}{4} - \dfrac{0.16}{64} = 2 + 0.1 - 0.0025$. \ans{P_2(4.4) = 2.0975}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{$\sum_{n\ge0}(-1)^n/n!$. (a) Exact sum and decimal. (b) Error bound after four terms.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} This is the Maclaurin series for $e^{x}$ at $x = -1$. \ans{e^{-1} \approx 0.3679}

\textbf{Solution (b):} The terms alternate and their sizes $\frac{1}{n!}$ decrease to zero, so the error after the terms $n = 0,1,2,3$ is at most the size of the $n = 4$ term, $\frac{1}{4!}$. \ans{\frac{1}{24} \approx 0.0417}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{Lagrange bound for $P_3$ of $\ln(1+x)$ at $x = 0.5$, with $f^{(4)}(x) = -6/(1+x)^4$. (a) The bound. (b) Choosing $M$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $|R_3(0.5)| \le \dfrac{M(0.5)^{4}}{4!}$ with $M = 6$, so the bound is $\dfrac{6(0.0625)}{24}$. \ans{0.015625}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} $|f^{(4)}(x)| = \dfrac{6}{(1+x)^{4}}$ is a positive constant over a growing denominator, so it is largest where $1+x$ is smallest --- at the left end, $x = 0$, where it equals $6$. Taking $M = 6$ therefore bounds the fourth derivative across the whole interval. Full credit explains why the maximum sits at that end rather than asserting the value.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{$f(x) = xe^x$, $P_3(x) = x + x^2 + \frac{x^3}{2}$. (a) Find $f'''$. (b) Estimate $f(0.3)$. (c) Which bound?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Differentiating $xe^{x}$ repeatedly gives $(x+1)e^{x}$, then $(x+2)e^{x}$, then \ans{f'''(x) = (x+3)e^{x}}

At $0$ this is $3$, and $3/3! = \frac12$, the cubic coefficient shown.

\textbf{Solution (b):} $P_3(0.3) = 0.3 + 0.09 + \dfrac{0.027}{2}$. \ans{P_3(0.3) = 0.4035}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} Use the Lagrange form. The Maclaurin series for $xe^{x}$ is $x + x^{2} + \frac{x^{3}}{2} + \frac{x^{4}}{6} + \cdots$, all of whose terms are positive at a positive input, so the series does not alternate and the alternating remainder bound simply does not apply. The Lagrange form has no such condition: bound $|f^{(4)}|$ on $[0, 0.3]$ and divide by $4!$. Full credit names the alternation condition as the thing that decides between the two.
