\problem{Field for $\frac{dy}{dx} = x - y$, dot at $(0,1)$. (a) Slope at
$(2,-1)$. (b) Sketch the solution through the dot.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} Substitute the point into the differential equation ---
that is all a slope field is, evaluated one point at a time:
$\frac{dy}{dx} = 2 - (-1)$.
\[ \boxed{3} \]

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The curve through $(0,1)$ should
start with slope $0 - 1 = -1$, dip, and then turn upward, staying tangent to
every segment it passes. Award full credit when the sketch is tangent to the
field throughout and passes through the dot; a curve that cuts across the
segments earns nothing, however smooth it looks. (This family approaches the
line $y = x - 1$ from above, which is a useful thing to point out.)

\problem{Field for $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{y}{2}$. (a) Slope at $(1,4)$.
(b) Where the segments are horizontal.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} The slope does not depend on $x$ at all:
$\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{4}{2}$.
\[ \boxed{\frac{dy}{dx} = 2} \]

\textbf{Solution (b).} $\frac{y}{2} = 0$.
\[ \boxed{y = 0} \]
Every segment along the horizontal axis is flat, and the whole row at a given
height is parallel --- the signature of a slope that depends only on $y$.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Field for $\frac{dy}{dx} = -\frac{x}{y}$ with $y > 0$, dot at $(3,4)$.
(a) Slope there. (b) Sketch and describe.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} $-\dfrac{3}{4}$:
\[ \boxed{-\frac{3}{4}} \]

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The solution curves are the upper
halves of circles centred at the origin; through $(3,4)$ the curve is the arc of
$x^2 + y^2 = 25$, rising to a flat top at $(0,5)$ and falling away on both
sides. Award full credit only when the sketch is tangent to the field
\emph{and} the description names the circular shape; one without the other is
half.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Field for $\frac{dy}{dx} = 2x$, dot at $(1,2)$. (a) General solution.
(b) $C$. (c) Sketch.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} Antidifferentiate the right-hand side:
\[ \boxed{y = x^2 + C} \]

\textbf{Solution (b).} The curve passes through $(1,2)$, so $2 = 1 + C$.
\[ \boxed{C = 1} \]

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} The particular solution is
$y = x^2 + 1$: an upward parabola with its vertex on the vertical axis at
height $1$, passing through the dot. Award full credit when the sketch is a
parabola through the marked point that flattens where the printed segments are
flat and steepens symmetrically to either side.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Field for $\frac{dy}{dx} = x$ or $\frac{dy}{dx} = y$. (a) Where
$\frac{dy}{dx} = y$ is flat. (b) Which equation is printed.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} $y = 0$:
\[ \boxed{y = 0} \]

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The printed field is
$\frac{dy}{dx} = y$. In it the slope depends only on the height, so every
segment in a horizontal row is parallel and the row along the horizontal axis is
flat. Under $\frac{dy}{dx} = x$ the parallel segments would run down each
\emph{vertical} column instead, and the flat ones would lie along the vertical
axis. Full credit needs that row-versus-column observation, not just the correct
choice --- a coin flip gets the choice right half the time.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Field for $\frac{dy}{dx} = xy$, dot at $(0,2)$, solution
$y = 2e^{x^2/2}$. (a) $\int x\,dx$. (b) $y$ at $x = 2$. (c) Sketch.}

\textbf{Solution (a).}
\[ \boxed{\frac{x^2}{2} + C} \]
Separating gives $\ln|y| = \frac{x^2}{2} + C$, and exponentiating with
$y(0) = 2$ produces the stated solution.

\textbf{Solution (b).} $y(2) = 2e^{4/2}$.
\[ \boxed{2e^{2}} \]
About $14.78$, which is off the top of the printed grid.

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} The curve leaves the dot flat (the
slope at $x = 0$ is zero for every height), then climbs ever more steeply to the
right and mirrors that to the left. Award full credit when the sketch is
tangent to the field throughout, flat where the field is flat, and never crosses
the horizontal axis.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Field for $\frac{dy}{dx} = y(4-y)$, dot at $(0,1)$. (a) Horizontal
segments. (b) Long-run behaviour.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} $y(4-y) = 0$ at
\[ \boxed{y = 0 \text{ or } y = 4} \]
These are the equilibrium solutions: constant functions whose graphs are the two
flat rows in the field.

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} Between the two flat rows every
segment slopes upward, so a solution starting at $y = 1$ rises. As it approaches
$y = 4$ the segments flatten, so the curve levels off and approaches $4$ from
below without reaching it. Full credit needs both the rising behaviour and the
levelling off at $4$.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Field for $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{x+1}{y}$, $y > 0$, dot at $(0,2)$,
solution $y = \sqrt{x^2 + 2x + 4}$. (a) Slope at $(1,2)$. (b) $y$ at $x = 2$.
(c) Sketch and agreement.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} $\dfrac{1 + 1}{2}$:
\[ \boxed{1} \]

\textbf{Solution (b).} $y(2) = \sqrt{4 + 4 + 4} = \sqrt{12}$.
\[ \boxed{3.464} \]

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} The curve rises through $(0,2)$,
flattening near $x = -1$ where the numerator vanishes and steepening to the
right. A correct response names one specific agreement: for example that the
curve steepens as $x$ grows because the slope grows with $x$, or that it flattens
as the height grows because the slope is divided by $y$. Full credit needs a
named feature, not the general claim that they agree.
