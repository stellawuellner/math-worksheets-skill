\sstitleblock{Vertex, Axis, Intercepts and Opening Direction}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{The four features, and where each one comes from:}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.1cm, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{Vertex} --- the turning point. Vertex form $y = a(x-h)^2 + k$
shows it as $(h,k)$; standard form $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ gives its $x$-value as
$-\frac{b}{2a}$, and you substitute to get the height.
\item \textbf{Axis of symmetry} --- the vertical line through the vertex,
$x = h$ or $x = -\frac{b}{2a}$. The graph is a mirror image across it.
\item \textbf{Intercepts} --- the $y$-intercept is $c$ (set $x = 0$); the
$x$-intercepts are the solutions of $y = 0$, and the axis sits midway between
them.
\item \textbf{Opening direction} --- decided by the sign of $a$ alone:
$a > 0$ opens up (vertex is a minimum), $a < 0$ opens down (vertex is a
maximum).
\end{itemize}
\end{formulabox}

\skillheading{1. Reading features off vertex form}

\begin{formulabox}
In $y = a(x-h)^2 + k$ the vertex is $(h,k)$ and the axis is $x = h$. The sign
inside the parentheses flips: $(x+p)^2$ is $(x-(-p))^2$, so $h$ is negative
there. The size of $a$ sets the width --- bigger than one is narrower than
$y = x^2$, smaller is wider.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Give the vertex, the axis and the opening direction of
$y = 2(x+3)^2 - 5$.
\step{The equation is already in vertex form, so no algebra is needed --- read
$h$ and $k$ off it, then let the sign of $a$ settle the direction.}
\step{$(x+3)^2 = (x-(-3))^2$, so $h = -3$ and the axis is $x = -3$; the
constant outside gives $k$. Substituting confirms it:
$y(-3) = 2(0)^2 - 5$.}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}
\begin{axis}[axis lines=center, xmin=-6.4, xmax=1, ymin=-6, ymax=8,
  xtick={-6,-3,0}, ytick={-5,0,5},
  tick label style={font=\tiny}, width=5.6cm, height=4.4cm,
  enlargelimits=false, samples=40]
  \addplot[thick, blue, domain=-5.5:-0.5] {2*(x+3)^2 - 5};
  \fill (axis cs:-3,-5) circle (1.7pt);
\end{axis}
\end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
Vertex height at $x = -3$: $\ans{-5}$, so the vertex is $(-3,-5)$ and, since
$a$ is positive, the parabola opens up.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} For $y = -(x-4)^2 + 7$, what is the $y$-value at the vertex,
and does the parabola open up or down?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{7}$ at $x = 4$; opens down}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{2. Reading features off standard form}

\begin{formulabox}
Find the axis first: $x = -\frac{b}{2a}$. Keep \emph{both} minus signs --- the
one in the formula and any minus already on $b$ or on $a$. Substituting that
$x$ back into the equation gives the vertex height, and $c$ is the
$y$-intercept for free.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Find the vertex and the axis of $y = x^2 - 10x + 21$.
\step{Nothing here is in vertex form and the intercepts are not obvious, so go
through the axis-of-symmetry formula rather than hunting for factors.}
\step{$a = 1$ and $b = -10$, so the axis is
$x = -\dfrac{-10}{2(1)} = 5$. Substituting: $y(5) = 25 - 50 + 21$.}
Vertex height at $x = 5$: $\ans{-4}$, so the vertex is $(5,-4)$, the axis is
$x = 5$, and the $y$-intercept is $21$.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Find the vertex of $y = x^2 + 8x + 7$. (Axis first, then
substitute.)\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: axis $x = -4$, vertex height $\ans{-9}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{3. Using the intercepts and the sign of $a$}

\begin{formulabox}
The $x$-intercepts are the solutions of $y = 0$, so factor when you can: from
$(x-p)(x-q)$ they are $p$ and $q$. The axis of symmetry sits exactly midway
between them, which is a second, independent route to the vertex --- and a free
check on $-\frac{b}{2a}$. A parabola may have two, one or no $x$-intercepts,
but it always has exactly one $y$-intercept.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Find the $x$-intercepts of $y = x^2 + 2x - 15$ and use them
to locate the axis.
\step{The question asks where the graph meets the $x$-axis, which is the same
as solving $y = 0$ --- so factor rather than complete the square.}
\step{Two numbers with product $-15$ and sum $2$ are $5$ and $-3$:
$x^2 + 2x - 15 = (x+5)(x-3)$, so $x + 5 = 0$ or $x - 3 = 0$.}
$x$-intercepts: $\ans{x = -5 \text{ and } x = 3}$, whose midpoint puts the axis
at $x = -1$; $a$ is positive, so the curve opens up and that midpoint is a
minimum.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Find the $x$-intercepts of $y = x^2 - 6x + 8$, then say where
the axis of symmetry is.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{x = 2 \text{ and } x = 4}$, axis
$x = 3$}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} $y = (x+5)^2$ has its vertex at $x = -5$, not $x = 5$
--- the sign inside the parentheses always flips. And in $-\frac{b}{2a}$ the
sign of $a$ counts too: for $y = -x^2 + 6x - 5$ the axis is
$x = -\frac{6}{-2} = 3$, not $-3$.
\end{watchoutbox}
