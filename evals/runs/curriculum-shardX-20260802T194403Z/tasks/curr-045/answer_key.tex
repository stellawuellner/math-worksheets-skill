\noindent\small\textbf{Chart data used throughout:} Mia 5, Leo 3, Ana 6, Sam 4 stickers.
\textbf{Measuring strip:} crayon 4, marker 6, pencil 7, straw 9 paper clips.\normalsize

\problem{\textbf{How many stickers does Ana have?}\par
Find Ana's row, then touch and count each star once: 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6. Ana's row has six
stars, and the key says one star means one sticker, so the count \emph{is} the number of
stickers: $\ans{6}$.\par
\textit{Listen for:} a child who counts a star twice, or who reads the row above.}

\problem{\textbf{Write $>$ or $<$: pencil \_\_\_ crayon.}\par
The strip says the pencil is 7 paper clips and the crayon is 4 paper clips. Both were
measured with the \emph{same} paper clips, so the bigger count is the longer object.
Seven is more than four, and the symbol opens toward the bigger side: $\ans{>}$.\par
\textit{Say it out loud:} ``pencil is greater than crayon.''}

\problem{\textbf{Who has the most stickers?}\par
Compare the rows by length, not by name: Mia 5, Leo 3, Ana 6, Sam 4. The longest row of
stars is Ana's, with 6 --- more than 5, 4, and 3. So $\ans{\text{Ana}}$ has the most.}

\problem{\textbf{How many more stickers does Ana have than Leo?}\par
``How many more'' means compare the two rows and count the extra. Line them up: Ana 6,
Leo 3. Three of Ana's stars match Leo's three; the leftover stars are the extra.
$6 - 3 = 3$, so Ana has $\ans{3}$ more stickers.\par
\textit{Listen for:} answering ``6'' (the whole row) instead of the difference.}

\problem{\textbf{How many paper clips longer is the straw than the marker?}\par
Same ``how many more'' idea, now with lengths. Straw is 9 paper clips, marker is 6 paper
clips, and both used the same paper clip. Count on from 6 to 9: 7, 8, 9 --- that is three
counts. $9 - 6 = 3$, so the straw is $\ans{3 \text{ paper clips}}$ longer.}

\problem{\textbf{How many stickers do all four children have in all?}\par
``In all'' means put every row together. Add the four counts, making tens where you can:
$5 + 3 = 8$, then $8 + 6 = 14$, then $14 + 4 = 18$. Altogether they have $\ans{18}$
stickers.}

\problem{\textbf{Who has the fewest stickers?}\par
``Fewest'' asks for the \emph{shortest} row, which is the opposite of problem 3. Mia 5,
Leo 3, Ana 6, Sam 4: the shortest row is Leo's, with only 3 stars. So $\ans{\text{Leo}}$
has the fewest.}

\problem{\textbf{Order crayon, marker, and straw from shortest to longest.}\par
Read each length off the strip: crayon 4, marker 6, straw 9 paper clips. Because the same
unit was used every time, ordering the objects is just ordering the numbers, smallest
first: $4$, then $6$, then $9$. So the order is
$\ans{\text{crayon }(4),\ \text{marker }(6),\ \text{straw }(9)}$.}

\problem{\textbf{Is Mia right that Leo $+$ Sam beats Ana $+$ Mia?}\par
Do not judge by the claim --- get both totals from the chart first. Leo and Sam:
$3 + 4 = 7$ stickers. Ana and Mia: $6 + 5 = 11$ stickers. Now compare the two totals:
7 is less than 11, so Mia's claim is wrong and the correct symbol is $\ans{<}$.\par
\textit{Listen for:} a child who compares single children (Sam 4 vs Mia 5) instead of the
two totals.}

\problem{\textbf{Same straw: 9 paper clips but only 5 blocks --- which unit is longer?}
\quad (open response --- read the reasoning)\par
The straw never changed length; only the measuring unit changed. It took \emph{fewer}
blocks to cover the same straw, so each block must cover more of it than a paper clip
does. Fewer units to cover one length always means the units are bigger. Therefore
$\ans{\text{one block is longer}}$.\par
\textit{A full-credit answer includes a picture or a sentence such as:} ``The straw is the
same both times. It only took 5 blocks but 9 paper clips, so one block is longer than one
paper clip.'' A drawing showing 5 long blocks and 9 short clips under the same straw earns
the same credit. \textit{Not full credit:} ``9 is bigger than 5, so paper clips are
longer'' --- that compares the counts instead of the units.}
