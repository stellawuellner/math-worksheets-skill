\problem{Write the numeral for the number word \emph{thirteen}.}

\textbf{Solution:} Hear the word in two pieces: \emph{thir} is the ones part (three) and \emph{teen} is the ten. Put the ten down first and count the three ones on: $10 + 3$. \ans{13}

\problem{Write the numeral for the number word \emph{seventeen}.}

\textbf{Solution:} \emph{Seven} names the ones and \emph{teen} names the ten, so the numeral is $10 + 7$. The ones part is said first in the word but written second in the numeral. \ans{17}

\problem{Look at the numeral $15$. (a) $15$ is one ten and how many ones? (b) Write the number word for $15$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The leading digit stands for one whole ten, so take that ten out of the number to leave the ones: $15 - 10$. \ans{5}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The intended word is \emph{fifteen}. Full credit for a recognisable attempt at it, including invented spelling a reader can sound out (\emph{fifteen}, \emph{fiften}, \emph{fifteem}). A different teen word that keeps the \emph{-teen} ending, such as \emph{fourteen}, earns half credit. Digits written instead of a word earn no credit, because part (a) already asks for the digits.

\problem{Look at the numeral $16$. $16$ is one ten and how many ones?}

\textbf{Solution:} Again take out the ten the leading digit stands for: $16 - 10$. The word agrees --- \emph{six}-teen names those six ones. \ans{6}

\problem{Write the numeral for the number word \emph{eleven}.}

\textbf{Solution:} \emph{Eleven} is the odd one out: it does not say its ones part aloud. But it is still one ten and some ones, and it is the very next number after ten, so exactly one one is left over: $10 + 1$. \ans{11}

\problem{Think about the number word \emph{eighteen}. (a) Write its numeral. (b) Write how many ones that numeral has.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \emph{Eight} names the ones and \emph{teen} names the ten, so the numeral is $10 + 8$. \ans{18}

\textbf{Solution (b):} Take the ten back out to be sure the two halves agree: $18 - 10$. The digit on the right is the ones digit, and it matches the \emph{eight} you heard. \ans{8}

\problem{Look at the numeral $12$. (a) $12$ is one ten and how many ones? (b) Write the number word for $12$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Take out the ten: $12 - 10$. \ans{2}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The intended word is \emph{twelve}. Full credit for a recognisable attempt at it, including invented spelling a reader can sound out (\emph{twelve}, \emph{twelv}, \emph{twelfe}). \emph{Twenty} earns half credit --- it is the commonest confusion at this stage and worth naming aloud. Digits written instead of a word earn no credit, because part (a) already asks for the digits.

\problem{Two number words: \emph{fourteen} and \emph{nineteen}. (a) Write the numeral for \emph{fourteen}. (b) Write the numeral for \emph{nineteen}. (c) Write $<$, $>$, or $=$ between them.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \emph{Four} names the ones, \emph{teen} names the ten: $10 + 4$. \ans{14}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \emph{Nine} names the ones: $10 + 9$. \ans{19}

\textbf{Solution (c):} Both numerals hold exactly one ten, so the tens match and only the ones can decide. Four ones is fewer than nine ones, so the first numeral is the smaller one and the wide end of the symbol faces the second. \ans{14 < 19}

\problem{Ravi was asked for the numeral matching \emph{fifteen} and wrote $51$. (a) Write the correct numeral. (b) Explain what Ravi mixed up.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \emph{Fif} names five ones and \emph{teen} names the one ten, so the numeral is one ten and five ones: $10 + 5$. \ans{15}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} A correct response says the digits landed in the wrong places. Full credit for the idea that the word says the ones part first, but the numeral must write the ten first --- so $51$ reads as five tens and one one, which is a far bigger number than the one asked for. Accept the short form ``he swapped the digits'' provided the child says which digit stands for the ten. Correcting the numeral in words with no reason earns half credit. Repeating the correct numeral earns no credit, because part (a) already asks for it.

\problem{Write \emph{sixteen}, \emph{eleven}, \emph{fourteen} as numerals, in order from least to greatest.}

\textbf{Solution:} Turn each word into its numeral first --- sixteen is $10 + 6$, eleven is $10 + 1$, fourteen is $10 + 4$. All three hold one ten, so order them by their ones: one, then four, then six. \ans{11,\ 14,\ 16}
