\problem{\textbf{Differentiate} $A = \pi r^2$ \textbf{with respect to} $r$.

\textbf{Solution:} $\pi$ is a constant, so the power rule applies to $r^2$ alone:
$\dfrac{dA}{dr} = \pi \cdot 2r$. \ans{2\pi r}

\noindent This is the factor the chain rule attaches to $\dfrac{dr}{dt}$:
$\dfrac{dA}{dt} = 2\pi r \dfrac{dr}{dt}$. Note that it still contains $r$ --- that
is exactly why a numeric radius cannot be substituted before this step.}

\problem{\textbf{Cone with} $h = 2r$: \textbf{eliminate, then differentiate.}

\textbf{Solution:} Substitute the geometric constraint into the volume relation
first, so that only one variable remains:
\[ V = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 h = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 (2r) = \tfrac{2}{3}\pi r^3 \]
Now differentiate: $\dfrac{dV}{dr} = \tfrac{2}{3}\pi \cdot 3r^2$. \ans{2\pi r^2}

\noindent\textit{Why the order matters:} differentiating $\tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2h$
directly would need a product rule and a second unknown rate $\dfrac{dh}{dt}$.
Eliminating first is what makes the problem solvable with one given rate.}

\problem{\textbf{Balloon:} $\dfrac{dV}{dt} = 100$, \textbf{find} $\dfrac{dr}{dt}$
\textbf{at} $r = 5$.

\textbf{Solution:} Relation and differentiation come before any number:
\begin{align*}
  V &= \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3 \\
  \frac{dV}{dt} &= 4\pi r^2 \frac{dr}{dt} \\
  \frac{dr}{dt} &= \frac{dV/dt}{4\pi r^2}
\end{align*}
Only now substitute the instantaneous values $\dfrac{dV}{dt} = 100$ and $r = 5$:
$\dfrac{100}{4\pi (5)^2} = \dfrac{100}{100\pi} = \dfrac{1}{\pi}$. \ans{0.318}

\noindent\textit{Watch for:} $1.59$ comes from dividing by $4\pi r$ rather than
$4\pi r^2$ --- the derivative factor was taken from the wrong power. A quick
sanity check: the divisor must have the units of an area, and $4\pi r$ does not.}

\problem{\textbf{Ladder:} $10$ \textbf{ft, base at} $6$ \textbf{ft moving out at}
$2$ \textbf{ft/s.}

\textbf{Solution:} Let $x$ be the base distance and $y$ the wall height. The
ladder's length is the constant here, and $x$ and $y$ both vary:
\begin{align*}
  x^2 + y^2 &= 10^2 \\
  2x\frac{dx}{dt} + 2y\frac{dy}{dt} &= 0 \\
  \frac{dy}{dt} &= -\frac{x}{y}\cdot\frac{dx}{dt}
\end{align*}
Solve for the wanted rate symbolically first, then substitute. At the instant in
question $x = 6$, so $y = \sqrt{10^2 - 6^2} = 8$, and
$\dfrac{dy}{dt} = -\dfrac{6}{8}(2)$. \ans{-1.500}

\noindent The negative sign is part of the answer: the top slides down.
\textit{Watch for:} $-2.67$ is $-\dfrac{y}{x}\dfrac{dx}{dt}$, the inverted ratio.
Solving the differentiated equation symbolically before substituting is what
prevents it.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: the conical tank with a "fixed" radius.}

\textbf{Solution:} Writing $\dfrac{dV}{dt} = \tfrac{1}{3}\pi r^2 \dfrac{dh}{dt}$
with $r$ held at $3$ assumes the water's surface keeps the radius of the tank's
rim as the level falls. It does not: the surface is a cross-section of the cone,
so it shrinks with the depth. Similar triangles give the constraint
$\dfrac{r}{h} = \dfrac{3}{6}$, i.e. $r = \dfrac{h}{2}$. Substitute it, then
differentiate:
\begin{align*}
  V &= \tfrac{1}{3}\pi \left(\tfrac{h}{2}\right)^2 h = \frac{\pi h^3}{12} \\
  \frac{dV}{dt} &= \frac{\pi h^2}{4}\cdot\frac{dh}{dt} \\
  \frac{dh}{dt} &= \frac{4}{\pi h^2}\cdot\frac{dV}{dt}
\end{align*}
With $\dfrac{dV}{dt} = -12$ (draining) and $h = 4$:
$\dfrac{dh}{dt} = \dfrac{4(-12)}{\pi (4)^2} = -\dfrac{3}{\pi}$. \ans{-0.955}

\noindent\textit{Watch for:} $-1.27$ is what the student's fixed-radius setup
produces. It is too fast by a factor of $\tfrac{4}{3}$, and the sign of the error
is predictable: pretending the surface stays wide makes the level appear to drop
faster than it does.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: the pen against the barn.}

\textbf{Solution:} The constraint $2x + 2y = 200$ is the perimeter of a closed
rectangle, but the barn supplies one side, so only three sides are fenced. Let
$x$ be the side parallel to the barn and $y$ each of the two perpendicular sides.
The correct constraint is $x + 2y = 200$, giving $y = \dfrac{200 - x}{2}$.
Now write the area with one variable and differentiate:
\begin{align*}
  A(x) &= x\cdot\frac{200 - x}{2} = 100x - \frac{x^2}{2} \\
  A'(x) &= 100 - x
\end{align*}
Setting $A'(x) = 0$ gives the critical value. Since $A''(x) = -1 < 0$, it is a
maximum.
\[ \boxed{A'(x) = 100 - x \qquad x = 100} \]

\noindent The corresponding $y$ is $50$ and the maximum area is $5000$ square
feet. The student's constraint would have given $x = 50$ and an area of $2500$ ---
exactly half, because their pen spends fencing on a side the barn already
provides.}

\problem{\textbf{Open box from a} $24$ \textbf{by} $24$ \textbf{sheet.}

\textbf{Solution:} Cutting a square of side $x$ from each corner leaves a base of
side $24 - 2x$ and a height of $x$:
\begin{align*}
  V(x) &= x(24 - 2x)^2 = 4x^3 - 96x^2 + 576x \\
  V'(x) &= 12x^2 - 192x + 576 = 12(x^2 - 16x + 48) = 12(x - 4)(x - 12)
\end{align*}
\[ \boxed{V'(x) = 12x^2 - 192x + 576 \qquad x = 4 \quad x = 12} \]

\noindent Rejecting a critical value: the physical domain is $0 < x < 12$,
because two cuts of side $x$ must fit across a side of length $24$. At $x = 12$
the base has side $24 - 2(12) = 0$, so the box has no volume --- it is an endpoint
of the domain, not an interior maximum. The maximum is at $x = 4$, where
$V = 4(16)^2 = 1024$. Stating the domain \emph{as part of the setup} is what makes
this rejection automatic rather than an afterthought.}

\problem{\textbf{Explain: substituting before differentiating.}
\textit{(Open response --- flagged for manual review; a model answer follows.)}

\textbf{Model answer:} Substituting the instantaneous radius into
$V = \tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$ turns a function of a changing quantity into a single
number. Differentiating a number gives zero, so the conclusion "the rate is zero"
is a faithful consequence of the wrong first step, not an arithmetic slip. The
relation must hold at \emph{every} instant, not just the one of interest, because
differentiation compares nearby instants; freezing $r$ discards the very variation
the derivative measures.

\noindent The distinction to keep is between quantities that vary and quantities
that are genuinely constant. In the balloon, $r$ and $V$ both vary with time and
neither may be replaced by a number before differentiating. In the ladder problem,
the ladder's length is a true constant, which is why it may be written as $10$
from the start while $x$ and $y$ may not. A useful test: if the quantity has a
non-zero rate of change in the problem, it must stay a symbol.

\noindent Substitution becomes legal once differentiation is finished and the
equation has been solved for the desired rate. At that point the equation relates
rates \emph{at a general instant}, and putting in the values for one instant
evaluates it there.

\noindent\textit{What a reviewer should look for:} the response must separate
varying quantities from genuine constants and must state that substitution is
legal only after differentiating.}
