\problem{\textbf{Pattern A:} start at $0$, add $2$ each time.

\textbf{Solution:} Write the starting number as term $1$, then add the step
once for every move to the next term: $0$, $2$, $4$, $6$, $8$, $10$. Getting
from term $1$ to term $6$ is five steps of $2$, which is $0 + 2 \times 5$.
\ans{10}

Common slip to watch for: adding $2$ six times instead of five and answering
$12$. Term $1$ is the start, before any adding.}

\problem{\textbf{Pattern B:} start at $0$, add $6$ each time.

\textbf{Solution:} Same reasoning with a bigger step: $0$, $6$, $12$, $18$,
$24$, $30$. From term $1$ to term $6$ is five steps of $6$, so
$0 + 6 \times 5$. \ans{30}}

\problem{\textbf{Comparing A and B term by term.}

Lined up, the two patterns look like this:
\par\smallskip
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|l|c|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
Pattern A & $0$ & $2$ & $4$ & $6$ & $8$ & $10$ \\
\hline
Pattern B & $0$ & $6$ & $12$ & $18$ & $24$ & $30$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\par\smallskip
\textbf{(a)} Divide the fourth terms, B's over A's: $18 \div 6$. \ans{3}

The same answer comes from any matching pair: $6 \div 2 = 3$,
$12 \div 4 = 3$, $24 \div 8 = 3$.

\textbf{(b)} Because every B term is $3$ times the matching A term, and A's
sixth term is $10$, B's sixth term is $3 \times 10$. \ans{30}

That matches the sixth term found by adding in problem 2, which is the check.
The reason the relationship is exactly ``times $3$'' here is that B's step,
$6$, is $3$ times A's step, $2$, \emph{and} both patterns start at $0$.}

\problem{\textbf{Ordered pairs from A and B.}

\textbf{(a)} (Open response --- judge by reading.) Pair up corresponding
terms, A first: $(0, 0)$, $(2, 6)$, $(4, 12)$, $(6, 18)$. Plotted on the grid,
the four points climb from the origin in a straight line, going $2$ across and
$6$ up each time.

\textbf{(b)} Every pair's second number is $3$ times its first number, so with
$8$ as the first number the second number is $3 \times 8$. \ans{24}

Look for: pairs written first number from Pattern A, points plotted at the
correct crossings (not shifted by one square), and a straight-line arrangement
noticed or at least not contradicted.}

\problem{\textbf{Ana's jar:} $5$ dollars in week $1$, add $3$ dollars a week.

\textbf{Solution:} Weeks $1$ to $4$: $5$, $8$, $11$, $14$ dollars. Week $8$ is
seven steps after week $1$, so it is $5 + 3 \times 7 = 5 + 21$ dollars.
\ans{26}

Counting the steps rather than the weeks is the whole trick: eight weeks, seven
additions.}

\problem{\textbf{Ben's jar:} $10$ dollars in week $1$, add $6$ dollars a week.

\textbf{(a)} Week $8$ is again seven steps after week $1$:
$10 + 6 \times 7 = 10 + 42$ dollars. \ans{52}

\textbf{(b)} Divide Ben's week-$8$ total by Ana's: $52 \div 26$. \ans{2}

Ben's jar holds twice Ana's in every week, because Ben started with twice as
much \emph{and} adds twice as much each week. Both halves are needed.}

\problem{\textbf{Means of the two savers.}

\textbf{(a)} Ana: $5 + 8 + 11 + 14 + 17 = 55$ dollars in total, shared over five
weeks: $55 \div 5$. \ans{11}

\textbf{(b)} Ben: $10 + 16 + 22 + 28 + 34 = 110$ dollars in total, over the same
five weeks: $110 \div 5$. \ans{22}

\textbf{(c)} Yes: $22$ is twice $11$. Every one of Ben's weekly totals is twice
Ana's matching total, so the two totals added up are in the same ratio, and
dividing both by $5$ keeps it. A relationship that holds term by term is
inherited by the mean.}

\problem{\textbf{Patterns C and D --- checking Mia's claim.}

\textbf{Solution:} Write both patterns out to term $4$.
\par\smallskip
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|l|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
Pattern C (start $3$, add $2$) & $3$ & $5$ & $7$ & $9$ \\
\hline
Pattern D (start $3$, add $4$) & $3$ & $7$ & $11$ & $15$ \\
\hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\par\smallskip
Pattern D's fourth term is $3 + 4 \times 3 = 3 + 12$. \ans{15}

Mia answered $18$, which is twice Pattern C's fourth term of $9$. What her rule
missed: doubling the step does not double the terms unless the starting number
doubles too. Here both patterns start at the same $3$, so that shared $3$ is
never doubled --- Pattern D's term is always Pattern C's term plus the extra
steps, $2$ more for each step taken, and at term $4$ that is only $6$ extra,
not the $9$ that doubling would need. Term $1$ shows the claim failing at once:
$3$ and $3$ are equal, not double.}

\problem{\textbf{Patterns P and Q as ordered pairs.}

\textbf{(a)} (Open response --- judge by reading.) Pattern P: $0$, $3$, $6$,
$9$, $12$. Pattern Q: $0$, $5$, $10$, $15$, $20$. Paired up, P first:
$(0,0)$, $(3,5)$, $(6,10)$, $(9,15)$, $(12,20)$. On the grid the five points
lie on a straight line rising $5$ for every $3$ across.

\textbf{(b)} The pair whose first number is $6$ is the third pair, so read its
partner straight from the list: $6$ is P's third term and Q's third term is
$5 \times 2$. \ans{10}

Either route is fine --- reading the third pair, or noticing that each Q term is
$\frac{5}{3}$ of its P term and computing $\frac{5}{3} \times 6$.

Look for: pairs in the right order (P first), points at the correct crossings,
and an answer of $10$ justified from the pattern or from the graph rather than
guessed.}

\problem{\textbf{Write your own pair of rules.} (Open response --- judge by
reading.)

Model answer: Rule 1 --- start at $2$ and add $3$ each time: $2$, $5$, $8$,
$11$, $14$. Rule 2 --- start at $8$ and add $12$ each time: $8$, $20$, $32$,
$44$, $56$. Every term of the second pattern is four times the matching term of
the first: $8 = 4 \times 2$, $20 = 4 \times 5$, $32 = 4 \times 8$, and so on.

How the rules had to be chosen: the second pattern's starting number must be
four times the first pattern's starting number, and its step must be four times
the first pattern's step. Multiply both parts of the rule by $4$ and every term
is multiplied by $4$. Starting both patterns at $0$ also works, and then only
the step has to be quadrupled.

Look for: five terms listed for each pattern, the ``four times'' relationship
holding at \emph{every} term (not just the last one), and an explanation that
names both the starting number and the step --- a student who scales only the
step will produce a pattern that fails at term $1$, which is exactly the error
problem 8 planted.}
