\sstitleblock{Finding the Missing Factor}

\skillheading{Skill 1 --- Missing Factor in an Array}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{The idea:} an array or a set of equal groups shows
$(\text{number of rows}) \times (\text{size of one row}) = \text{total}$.\par
If one of the two factors is missing, \textbf{share the total into the part you know}:
$\text{missing factor} = \text{total} \div \text{known factor}$.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad $4$ equal rows of counters, $20$ counters in all. How many are in each row?
\step{The total is shared equally among the rows, so the missing factor is the size of one row --- that is a division: total divided by the number of rows.}
\step{$20 \div 4$: skip-count by $4$ --- $4, 8, 12, 16, 20$ --- that is four jumps of $4$ to reach $20$, so each row holds $5$.}

Check by multiplying back: $4 \times 5 = 20$ $\checkmark$ \quad each row holds $\ans{5}$
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad $4$ equal rows, $28$ counters in all. How many counters are in each row?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{7}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{Skill 2 --- Missing Factor from a Fact You Know}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Rule:} every multiplication fact hides a division fact.\par
If $a \times b = c$, then $c \div a = b$ and $c \div b = a$.\par
So to solve $a \times \square = c$, run through the multiples of $a$ until you land on $c$, and count
how many you used.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad Solve $8 \times \square = 40$
\step{Nothing is shared or drawn here, so use the times table for the factor I know: I want the number of eights that make the total.}
\step{The eights go $8, 16, 24, 32, 40$. The fifth one is $40$, so the missing factor is $5$.}

Related division fact: $40 \div 8 = 5$. \quad $\square = \ans{5}$
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad Solve $4 \times \square = 28$\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{7}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{Skill 3 --- Missing Factor in a Story}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{How to set it up:} find the size of one group and the total. Then\par
$(\text{size of one group}) \times (\text{number of groups}) = \text{total}$.\par
The answer's \textbf{unit} is whatever the missing factor counts --- boxes, trays, rows --- not the
items inside them.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad A bag holds $9$ marbles. There are $36$ marbles in full bags. How many bags?
\step{Each bag is one equal group of the same size, so the number of bags is the missing factor, and I find it by sharing the total into groups of that size.}
\step{$36 \div 9$: the nines go $9, 18, 27, 36$ --- four nines make $36$.}

The missing factor counts bags, so the answer is $\ans{4}$ bags.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad A shelf holds $6$ books. There are $30$ books on full shelves. How many shelves?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{5}$ shelves}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} the missing factor is never found by multiplying the two numbers you can see.
In $8 \times \square = 56$, multiplying gives $448$, which is far more than the total --- a sure sign
the wrong operation was used. Divide, or skip-count up to the total.
\end{watchoutbox}
