\noindent\textbf{How to use this key.} A step is legal when the new equation
has the same solution set as the old one. Credit the \emph{reason}, not just
the arithmetic: a student who reaches the right number while writing
``subtracted 7'' on a line where 7 was subtracted from one side only has not
yet learned this skill.

\problem{Solve $3x + 7 = 22$ with reasons, then check.

\textbf{Solution:} From $3x + 7 = 22$, subtract 7 from \emph{both} sides ---
Subtraction Property of Equality --- to get $3x = 15$. Then divide both sides
by 3, which is legal because 3 is not zero --- Division Property of Equality.
That gives $\ans{x = 5}$.

\textbf{Check:} substituting into the original left side,
$3(5) + 7 = 15 + 7 = \ans{22}$, which matches the right side, so the solution
set really was preserved. A student who divides first, turning 22 into
$\frac{22}{3}$ and then subtracting 7, lands at $x = \frac{22}{3} - 7$; putting
that back gives 8, not 22, which is exactly how the check catches an illegal
order.}

\problem{Solve $\dfrac{x}{4} - 3 = 5$ with reasons.

\textbf{Solution:} Add 3 to both sides --- Addition Property of Equality ---
giving $\dfrac{x}{4} = 8$. Multiply both sides by 4 --- Multiplication Property
of Equality --- giving $\ans{x = 32}$. The addition comes first because the
subtraction is the operation applied \emph{last} to $x$ on the left, and legal
steps undo operations in reverse order.}

\problem{Rewrite $2(x+5)$ and name the property.

\textbf{Solution:} The Distributive Property gives
$2(x+5) = 2 \cdot x + 2 \cdot 5$, that is $\ans{2x+10}$. This one is different
in kind from the others: nothing was done to \emph{both} sides. Only one side
was rewritten into a form equal to it for every value of $x$, so no equation
containing it can gain or lose a solution. That is the second family of legal
transformations --- rewriting a side using an identity.

\noindent
\begin{tabular}{|l|l|}
\hline
\textbf{Kind of step} & \textbf{Why it is legal} \\ \hline
Same operation on both sides & Both sides stay equal, so solutions are kept \\ \hline
Rewrite one side by an identity & That side's value is unchanged for every $x$ \\ \hline
\end{tabular}}

\problem{Devi: $5x - 3 = 12 \to 5x = 9 \to x = \frac{9}{5}$.

\textbf{Solution:} The second line is the illegal one. Devi subtracted 3 from
the right side only ($12 - 3 = 9$) instead of adding 3 to both sides. Nothing
was done to the left side at all, so the two sides were no longer changed
equally and the solution set moved.

Legal version: add 3 to both sides --- Addition Property of Equality --- giving
$5x = 15$; then divide both sides by 5 --- Division Property of Equality ---
giving $\ans{x = 3}$.

\textbf{Check:} $5(3) - 3 = 15 - 3 = \ans{12}$, matching the right side.
Devi's value fails the same check: $5 \cdot \frac{9}{5} - 3 = 6$, not 12.}

\problem{Solve $-2x < 8$.

\textbf{Solution:} Divide both sides by $-2$. Dividing an inequality by a
negative number reverses the direction of the symbol, because multiplying by a
negative reflects every number across zero and reverses their order. So
$-2x < 8$ becomes $\ans{x > -4}$. Testing a value confirms it: $x = 0$ gives
$0 < 8$, true, and 0 is in the solution set; $x = -10$ gives $20 < 8$, false,
and $-10$ is not.}

\problem{Solve $4(x-2) = 3x + 5$ with reasons.

\textbf{Solution:} Distributive Property on the left: $4x - 8 = 3x + 5$.
Subtract $3x$ from both sides --- Subtraction Property of Equality (a variable
term is a quantity like any other): $x - 8 = 5$. Add 8 to both sides ---
Addition Property of Equality: $\ans{x = 13}$. Every line kept the same
solution, so the last line's solution is the first line's solution.}

\problem{$x^2 = 4x$, divided by $x$.

\textbf{Solution:} The Division Property of Equality requires the divisor to be
\emph{nonzero}, and here the divisor is $x$ itself, which may be zero. Dividing
by it silently assumes $x \neq 0$ and throws that possibility away, so the step
is not legal as written; it loses a solution rather than gaining a false one.

Legal route: subtract $4x$ from both sides to get $x^2 - 4x = 0$, factor to
$x(x - 4) = 0$, and apply the zero-product property:
$\ans{x = 0 \text{ or } x = 4}$. The discarded root really was a solution, since
$0^2 = 4 \cdot 0$.}

\problem{Ravi: $2x + 6 = 10 \to x + 6 = 5$.

\textbf{Solution:} Dividing both sides by 2 would be legal, but Ravi divided
only \emph{some} terms: the $2x$ and the 10 were halved while the 6 was left
alone. A division property applies to each side as a whole, not term by term.

Ravi's equation solves to $x = -1$. Substituting that into the original left
side gives $2(-1) + 6 = \ans{4}$, and the original right side is 10, so
$x = -1$ is not a solution of the original equation. The two equations are
therefore not equivalent. Substituting into Ravi's own equation instead would
give 5 and hide the error --- always test a suspect step against the
\emph{original}.}

\problem{Solve $-3(x-2) \ge 9$.

\textbf{Solution:} Distributive Property: $-3x + 6 \ge 9$. Subtract 6 from both
sides --- Subtraction Property of Equality: $-3x \ge 3$. Divide both sides by
$-3$; this is the line where the symbol turns around, because dividing by a
negative reverses order. Result: $\ans{x \le -1}$. Check with $x = -5$:
$-3(-5-2) = 21 \ge 9$, true.}

\problem{One illegal line in the solution of $2(x+3) = 4x - 8$.

\textbf{Solution:} The illegal line is the second, $2x + 3 = 4x - 8$. It should
have used the Distributive Property, which multiplies the 2 by \emph{both}
terms inside the parentheses: $2(x+3) = 2x + 6$, not $2x + 3$. The third line
is then correct arithmetic applied to a wrong equation, which is why naming the
first bad line matters.

Correct solution: $2x + 6 = 4x - 8$; subtract $2x$ from both sides:
$6 = 2x - 8$; add 8 to both sides: $14 = 2x$; divide both sides by 2:
$\ans{x = 7}$.}
