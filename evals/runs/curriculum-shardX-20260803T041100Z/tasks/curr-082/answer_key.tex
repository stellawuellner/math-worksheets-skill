\problem{\textbf{Point $A$ on a line cut into halves.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Count the equal parts between $0$ and $1$. There are two of
them, so every part is one half and the bottom number is $2$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Count the parts travelled from $0$ to reach $A$: just one. So
the top number is $1$.

\textbf{Step 3.} One part out of two equal parts is $\ans{\tfrac{1}{2}}$.

\textit{Teaching note:} the tick at $A$ is also the halfway point of the whole
line, which is a good visual check before any counting starts.}

\problem{\textbf{Point $B$ on a line cut into fourths.}

\textbf{Step 1.} There are four equal parts between $0$ and $1$, so each part
is one fourth and the bottom number is $4$.

\textbf{Step 2.} From $0$, the parts travelled to reach $B$ are: one, two,
three. The top number is $3$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Three parts out of four is $\ans{\tfrac{3}{4}}$.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $\frac{3}{5}$ --- the five \emph{tick marks} were
counted instead of the four equal \emph{parts}. Have the child colour in the
parts, not the ticks.}

\problem{\textbf{Point $C$ on a line cut into thirds.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Three equal parts between $0$ and $1$ makes each part one
third, so the bottom number is $3$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Counting from $0$: one part, two parts --- that reaches $C$.
The top number is $2$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The point is $\ans{\tfrac{2}{3}}$.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $\frac{1}{3}$ --- the one part left \emph{after}
$C$ was counted. Counting always starts at $0$ and runs up to the point.}

\problem{\textbf{Placing $\frac{4}{6}$ and renaming it.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The line is cut into six equal parts, so each tick is one
sixth. To place $\frac{4}{6}$, count four parts from $0$: that is the fourth
tick after $0$. Mark it $P$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Now look at the same picture in bigger pieces: two sixths make
one third, so the six parts group into three equal thirds. Point $P$ lands
exactly on the boundary of the second group.

\textbf{Step 3.} Two thirds out of three, so $\frac{4}{6}$ and
$\ans{\tfrac{2}{3}}$ name the same point.

\textit{Listen for:} ``it moved'' --- the point does not move. Only the size of
the parts we count with changes.}

\problem{\textbf{Placing $\frac{8}{8}$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Each part on this line is one eighth, and there are eight of
them between $0$ and $1$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Counting eight parts from $0$ uses up every part, which lands
on the far end of the whole.

\textbf{Step 3.} The far end of the whole is the number $\ans{1}$.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $\frac{7}{8}$ --- a child who believes a fraction
can never quite reach a whole stops one tick short. Eight eighths \emph{is}
one.}

\problem{\textbf{Comparing $\frac{2}{3}$ with $\frac{2}{4}$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Both fractions travel the same \emph{number} of parts (two),
so the comparison is decided entirely by how long one part is.

\textbf{Step 2.} Cutting one whole into three parts makes longer parts than
cutting it into four. On the pictures, $\frac{2}{3}$ sits past the middle of
the line and $\frac{2}{4}$ sits exactly on the middle.

\textbf{Step 3.} $\frac{2}{3}$ is farther from $0$, so
$\frac{2}{3}$ $\ans{>}$ $\frac{2}{4}$.

\textit{Listen for:} ``four is more than three, so $\frac{2}{4}$ is bigger.''
The bottom number counts how many pieces the whole was broken into --- more
pieces means smaller pieces.}

\problem{\textbf{Comparing $\frac{5}{8}$ with $\frac{3}{4}$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The two fractions are written in different units, so put them
on one line first. Every fourth is two eighths.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\frac{3}{4}$ is therefore three groups of two eighths, which
is the sixth tick. $\frac{5}{8}$ is the fifth tick.

\textbf{Step 3.} The fifth tick comes before the sixth, so
$\frac{5}{8}$ $\ans{<}$ $\frac{3}{4}$.

\textit{Teaching note:} rewriting one fraction so both share a unit is the
whole idea behind common denominators, met here as a picture.}

\problem{\textbf{Point $D$ on a line that runs to $2$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Check the labels first: this line runs from $0$ to $2$, and
each whole is cut into halves, so every part is one half.

\textbf{Step 2.} Count halves from $0$ to $D$: one half, two halves (that is
$1$), three halves. The top number is $3$ and the bottom number is $2$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Point $D$ is $\ans{\tfrac{3}{2}}$, which is one whole and one
more half.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $\frac{3}{4}$ --- the line was read as if it
ended at $1$, so all four parts were treated as parts of a single whole. The
label under the middle tick says $1$, and that settles it.}

\problem{\textbf{Placing $\frac{5}{3}$ and measuring past $1$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Each part here is one third, and $1$ sits at the third tick.
To place $\frac{5}{3}$, count five thirds from $0$ --- that is the fifth tick.
Mark it $E$.

\textbf{Step 2.} The question asks how far $E$ sits \emph{past} $1$, so count
from the tick labelled $1$ instead of from $0$: fourth tick, fifth tick. That
is two parts.

\textbf{Step 3.} Two parts of a whole cut into thirds is $\ans{\tfrac{2}{3}}$
past $1$. (Written as a subtraction: $\frac{5}{3} - 1 = \frac{2}{3}$.)

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $\frac{5}{3}$ --- the whole distance from $0$ was
given instead of the distance past $1$.}

\problem{\textbf{What Ellie got wrong.} \emph{Open explanation --- flagged for
manual review; the model response below is what a full-credit answer contains.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Mark the points. On the thirds line, $\frac{1}{3}$ is the
first tick after $0$. On the halves line, $\frac{1}{2}$ is the first tick after
$0$, which is the middle of the whole.

\textbf{Step 2.} Compare the two distances. $\frac{1}{3}$ stops short of the
middle; $\frac{1}{2}$ lands on it. So $\frac{1}{2}$ is farther from $0$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Say why. Both fractions travel one part, but the parts are not
the same size: cutting a whole into $3$ pieces gives \emph{smaller} pieces than
cutting it into $2$. A bigger bottom number means more pieces, and more pieces
means each one is shorter --- so $\frac{1}{3} < \frac{1}{2}$.

\textit{Full credit:} the two points are marked in the right places \emph{and}
the sentence says that a larger denominator makes smaller parts. A sentence
that only says ``because I looked at the picture'' has the right answer without
the reason --- ask the child to finish the thought.}
