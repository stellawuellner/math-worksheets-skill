\problem{$f(x) = x^{2} - 7x + 10$

\textbf{Step 1.} Zeros are the solutions of $f(x) = 0$, and the polynomial is
monic, so look for a factor pair of $10$ that adds to $-7$: those are $-2$ and
$-5$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $f(x) = (x - 2)(x - 5)$, so the zero product property gives
the two zeros. \quad $\ans{x = 2,\ 5}$}

\problem{$g(x) = x^{3} - 4x$

\textbf{(a) Step 1.} Always take the greatest common factor first:
$g(x) = x(x^{2} - 4)$.

\textbf{Step 2.} The second factor is a difference of squares:
$x^{2} - 4 = (x - 2)(x + 2)$.

\textbf{(b)} Set each factor to zero. Three linear factors, three distinct
zeros --- the maximum a cubic can have.

$\ans{x(x - 2)(x + 2);\quad x = 0,\ \pm 2}$}

\problem{$p(x) = (x - 3)^{2}(x + 1)$

\textbf{Step 1.} The function is already factored, so read the zeros and their
exponents directly: $x = 3$ from $(x - 3)^{2}$ and $x = -1$ from $(x + 1)$.

\textbf{Step 2.} The exponent on a factor \emph{is} the multiplicity of that
zero: multiplicity $2$ at $x = 3$, multiplicity $1$ at $x = -1$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Even multiplicity means the factor never changes sign there,
so the graph touches and turns back; odd multiplicity means it changes sign,
so the graph crosses.

$\ans{x = 3\ (m = 2,\ \text{touch}),\ x = -1\ (m = 1,\ \text{cross})}$}

\problem{Degree-$5$ polynomial $h$.

\textbf{(a) Step 1.} A polynomial of degree $n$ has at most $n - 1$ turning
points, because between consecutive turning points the graph is monotonic and
the derivative --- a degree-$4$ polynomial here --- has at most $4$ roots.

\textbf{(b) Step 2.} It has at most $n$ real zeros, since a degree-$n$
polynomial has exactly $n$ roots counted with multiplicity over the complex
numbers, and real ones are a subset.

\textit{Both are ceilings.} $h(x) = x^{5}$ has degree $5$ with one zero and no
turning points at all.

$\ans{4 \text{ turning points};\ 5 \text{ zeros}}$}

\problem{$\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{3x^{2} - 5}{x^{2} + 4}$

\textbf{Step 1.} Numerator and denominator have the \emph{same} degree, so
divide every term by the highest power, $x^{2}$:
\[ \frac{3 - \dfrac{5}{x^{2}}}{1 + \dfrac{4}{x^{2}}}. \]

\textbf{Step 2.} As $x \to \infty$ both fraction terms go to $0$, leaving the
ratio of the leading coefficients, $3/1$.

\textbf{Step 3.} A finite limit at infinity is a horizontal asymptote.

$\ans{y = 3}$}

\problem{$f(x) = x^{3} - 2x^{2} - 5x + 6$

\textbf{Step 1.} By the Rational Root Theorem any rational zero divides the
constant term $6$, so test $\pm 1, \pm 2, \pm 3, \pm 6$. At $x = 1$:
$1 - 2 - 5 + 6 = 0$, so $(x - 1)$ is a factor.

\textbf{Step 2.} Divide: $f(x) = (x - 1)(x^{2} - x - 6)$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Factor the quadratic: $x^{2} - x - 6 = (x - 3)(x + 2)$.

\textbf{Step 4.} Three distinct real zeros, so the graph crosses three times
and must have exactly $2$ turning points.

$\ans{x = 1,\ -2,\ 3}$}

\problem{$q(x) = x^{4} - 6x^{3} + 9x^{2}$

\textbf{Step 1.} Common factor first: $q(x) = x^{2}(x^{2} - 6x + 9)$.

\textbf{Step 2.} The bracket is a perfect square:
$x^{2} - 6x + 9 = (x - 3)^{2}$, so $q(x) = x^{2}(x - 3)^{2}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Two distinct zeros, each of multiplicity $2$. Both
multiplicities are even, so the graph \emph{touches} at both and crosses the
$x$-axis \textbf{zero} times --- the whole graph sits on or above the axis.

$\ans{x = 0\ (m = 2),\ x = 3\ (m = 2)}$}

\problem{A graph with exactly $4$ turning points.

\textbf{Step 1.} Run the bound backwards. Degree $n$ allows at most $n - 1$
turning points, so $4$ turning points require $n - 1 \ge 4$, that is
$n \ge 5$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Degree $5$ is achievable --- a quintic with four turning
points exists --- so the smallest possible degree is exactly $5$.

\textit{Expected explanation:} ``A degree-$n$ polynomial has at most $n - 1$
turning points, so $4$ turning points need $n - 1 \ge 4$.''

$\ans{5}$}

\problem{$r(x) = x^{4} - 5x^{2} + 4$

\textbf{(a) Step 1.} This is quadratic in $x^{2}$. With $u = x^{2}$:
$u^{2} - 5u + 4 = (u - 1)(u - 4)$, so
$r(x) = (x^{2} - 1)(x^{2} - 4)$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Each bracket is a difference of squares, so factor again ---
``completely'' means down to linear factors here.

\textbf{(b) Step 3.} Four distinct real zeros. A quartic can have at most $3$
turning points, and four crossings force a turn between each consecutive pair,
so it has exactly $3$.

$\ans{(x - 1)(x + 1)(x - 2)(x + 2);\ x = \pm 1,\ \pm 2;\ 3 \text{ turning points}}$}

\problem{$s(x) = (x + 2)(x - 1)^{2}(x - 3)$

\textbf{(a) Step 1.} Read the zeros and exponents from the factored form:
$x = -2$ (multiplicity $1$), $x = 1$ (multiplicity $2$), $x = 3$
(multiplicity $1$).

\textbf{(b) Step 2.} Sketch checklist --- this is what to look for in the
student's graph:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2cm, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
\item Crosses the $x$-axis at $x = -2$ and at $x = 3$ (odd multiplicity).
\item Touches and turns back at $x = 1$ without crossing (even multiplicity).
\item Degree $4$ with positive leading coefficient, so both ends rise.
\item Three turning points --- the maximum for a quartic --- one of which is
the touch point at $x = 1$.
\item Below the axis on $(-2, 1)$ and on $(1, 3)$; above it outside
$[-2, 3]$.
\end{itemize}

The sketch itself is flagged for manual review: judge it on the five bullets
above, not on exact $y$-values.

$\ans{x = -2,\ 1\ (m = 2),\ 3}$}

\problem{$\displaystyle\lim_{x \to \infty} \frac{4x^{2} + 3}{2x^{2} - x}$

\textbf{Step 1.} Equal degrees again, so divide numerator and denominator by
$x^{2}$:
\[ \frac{4 + \dfrac{3}{x^{2}}}{2 - \dfrac{1}{x}}. \]

\textbf{Step 2.} Both fraction terms vanish as $x \to \infty$, leaving
$4/2$.

\textbf{Step 3.} So the graph levels off at the horizontal asymptote below.
(The vertical asymptotes at $x = 0$ and $x = \tfrac{1}{2}$ do not affect end
behaviour.)

$\ans{y = 2}$}

\problem{Why $5$ distinct real zeros and $2$ turning points is impossible.

\textbf{Model answer.} Suppose the graph crosses the $x$-axis at five
different places. Between two consecutive $x$-intercepts the function is zero
at both ends and nonzero in between, so it must rise and then come back down
(or fall and come back up) --- it has to turn at least once in each of those
gaps. Five distinct zeros create four gaps, so the graph has at least $4$
turning points, not $2$. A degree-$5$ polynomial is allowed at most $4$
turning points, so four is exactly what such a polynomial must have --- and
$2$ is impossible.

\textit{Grading note:} this is a justification, so it is flagged for manual
review rather than machine-checked. Full credit needs the ``must turn between
consecutive zeros'' idea; naming Rolle's theorem is not required.}
