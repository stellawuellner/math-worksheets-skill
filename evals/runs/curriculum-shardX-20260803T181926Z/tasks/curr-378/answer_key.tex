\problem{\textbf{Yeast culture: solve $3^x = 81$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Both sides can be written as powers of the same base, so no
logarithm is needed --- matching exponents is exact and faster.

\textbf{Step 2.} $81 = 3^4$, so the equation reads $3^x = 3^4$. An exponential
function is one-to-one, so the exponents must be equal.

\textbf{Step 3.} The culture reaches that size after \ans{4 \text{ hours}}

\textit{Check:} $3^4 = 81$, and the model says the size is a multiple of the
start, so the answer is a time, not a size.}

\problem{\textbf{Bacteria: solve $2^x = 5$ to the nearest thousandth.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Five is not a power of two, so the bases cannot be matched.
Take a logarithm of both sides and use the power law to bring the unknown down.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\log(2^x) = \log 5$ gives $x\log 2 = \log 5$, so
$x = \dfrac{\log 5}{\log 2}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Evaluating, $x = 2.32192\ldots$, so the population reaches
five times its start after \ans{2.322 \text{ days}}

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $2.5$, from dividing the two numbers instead of
their logarithms. $\log 5 \div \log 2$ is not $5 \div 2$; the logarithms must
be taken first.}

\problem{\textbf{Doubling-time model: solve $\log_2 n = 5$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} A logarithm is an exponent, so the fastest route is to read
the equation as its exponential twin rather than to manipulate it.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\log_2 n = 5$ means $2^5 = n$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $2^5 = 32$, so \ans{n = 32}

\textit{Teaching note:} say the sentence out loud --- ``two to the fifth is
$n$''. Students who narrate the rewrite stop reaching for a calculator on
problems that do not need one.}

\problem{\textbf{Expand $\log_3(9x^4)$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The argument is a product, and one factor carries a power, so
the product law comes first and the power law second.

\textbf{Step 2.} Product law: $\log_3(9x^4) = \log_3 9 + \log_3(x^4)$. Power
law on the second term: $\log_3(x^4) = 4\log_3 x$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $\log_3 9 = 2$ because $3^2 = 9$, so the expansion is
\ans{2 + 4\log_3 x}

\textit{Listen for:} $\log_3 9 = 3$. Ask what exponent turns $3$ into $9$, not
what number goes into it.}

\problem{\textbf{Half-life: solve $200\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/6} = 25$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Isolate the exponential factor before doing anything with the
exponent --- the constant in front is not part of the power.

\textbf{Step 2.} Dividing by $200$ gives
$\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/6} = \tfrac{1}{8}$, and
$\tfrac{1}{8} = \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^3$, so $\dfrac{t}{6} = 3$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Multiplying by $6$, the sample falls to that mass after
\ans{18 \text{ years}}

\textit{Sense check:} three half-lives take the sample $200 \to 100 \to 50 \to
25$, which is exactly three periods of $6$ years.}

\problem{\textbf{Account: solve $1500(1.04)^t = 2000$ to the nearest
hundredth.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Isolate the power, then take logarithms --- $1.04$ and the
ratio of the balances share no convenient common base.

\textbf{Step 2.} $(1.04)^t = \dfrac{2000}{1500}$, so
$t\log(1.04) = \log\!\left(\dfrac{2000}{1500}\right)$ and
$t = \dfrac{\log(2000/1500)}{\log(1.04)}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Evaluating gives $t = 7.33495\ldots$, so the balance reaches
that level after \ans{7.33 \text{ years}}

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $1.33$, the ratio of the balances. That ratio is
the growth \emph{factor} needed, not the time; the logarithm converts a factor
into a number of periods.}

\problem{\textbf{Solve $\log x + \log(x-3) = 1$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Two logarithms with the same base and a plus sign between them
condense by the product law, which leaves one logarithm to undo.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\log\!\left(x(x-3)\right) = 1$, and in exponential form that
is $x(x-3) = 10^1$. Expanding, $x^2 - 3x - 10 = 0$, which factors as
$(x-5)(x+2) = 0$, so the candidates are $x = 5$ and $x = -2$.

\textbf{Step 3.} A logarithm needs a positive argument. At $x = -2$ both
$\log x$ and $\log(x-3)$ are undefined, so that candidate is extraneous ---
introduced by condensing, not by the original equation. The solution is
\ans{x = 5}

\textit{Check:} $\log 5 + \log 2 = \log 10 = 1$, which confirms both the
condensing and the rejected root.}

\problem{\textbf{Condense $2\log_2 x - \log_2 3$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} A coefficient in front of a logarithm is a power inside it, so
clear the coefficient before touching the subtraction.

\textbf{Step 2.} Power law: $2\log_2 x = \log_2(x^2)$. The expression is now
$\log_2(x^2) - \log_2 3$, and a difference of logarithms with the same base is
the logarithm of a quotient.

\textbf{Step 3.} Quotient law gives \ans{\log_2\!\left(\dfrac{x^2}{3}\right)}

\textit{Why it matters:} once the whole side is a single logarithm, the
equation can be rewritten in exponential form in one move. Condensing is the
enabling step, not decoration.}

\problem{\textbf{Error analysis: $4^{x+1} = 32$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Dividing $32$ by $4$ treats the equation as if the base and
the power were multiplied together. They are not: $4^{x+1}$ means repeated
multiplication of $4$, so dividing by $4$ removes one factor, it does not
remove the exponent.

\textbf{Step 2.} Both $4$ and $32$ are powers of $2$: $4 = 2^2$ and
$32 = 2^5$. The equation becomes $\left(2^2\right)^{x+1} = 2^5$, that is
$2^{2x+2} = 2^5$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Matching exponents gives $2x + 2 = 5$, so
\ans{x = \tfrac{3}{2}}

\textit{Check:} $4^{5/2} = \left(\sqrt{4}\right)^{5} = 32$, and Dev's answer
would give $4^{8}$, which is far larger --- substituting once would have caught
it.}

\problem{\textbf{Solve $2\log x = \log(x + 6)$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Both sides are single logarithms with the same base once the
coefficient on the left is absorbed, and equal logarithms force equal
arguments.

\textbf{Step 2.} $2\log x = \log(x^2)$, so $\log(x^2) = \log(x+6)$ gives
$x^2 = x + 6$. Then $x^2 - x - 6 = 0$ factors as $(x-3)(x+2) = 0$, with
candidates $x = 3$ and $x = -2$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The original equation contains $\log x$, which is undefined
for a negative argument, so $x = -2$ is rejected and the solution is
\ans{x = 3}

\textit{Worth naming:} $x = -2$ does satisfy $x^2 = x + 6$. The extraneous root
appears because squaring inside the logarithm widened the domain --- the
rejection is a domain fact, not an arithmetic slip.}
