\problem{\textbf{Graph A. In the week when 26 cartons were sold, how many bottles
of sunscreen were sold?}

Step 1: find $26$ on the horizontal axis --- that axis carries the ice-cream
counts, which is the quantity the question gives.

Step 2: go straight up to the plotted point, then straight across to the
vertical axis, which carries the sunscreen counts.

Step 3: the point sits at height $12$:

\ans{12}

The ordered pair is $(26, 12)$: $26$ cartons, $12$ bottles.}

\problem{\textbf{Graph A. Dana compared the 10-carton week with the 38-carton
week and wrote ``sunscreen went up by 28''. How many more bottles of sunscreen
were really sold?}

Step 1: name Dana's mistake --- $38 - 10 = 28$ is the change in \emph{cartons},
read off the horizontal axis. The question asks about sunscreen, which lives on
the vertical axis.

Step 2: read both sunscreen values. The $10$-carton week has $4$ bottles; the
$38$-carton week has $18$ bottles.

Step 3: subtract the two \emph{vertical} values: $18 - 4$:

\ans{14}

Step 4: sanity check --- sunscreen sales cannot rise by more than the highest
value plotted on that axis, so $28$ was impossible from the start.}

\problem{\textbf{Graph A. Dana claims the plot proves ice-cream sales cause
sunscreen sales. Name a lurking variable and say why the plot cannot show cause.}
\emph{(Open response --- review by hand.)}

\emph{Model answer.} The lurking variable is the \textbf{weather} --- the
temperature, or how sunny the day is. Hot, sunny weeks send people out for both
ice cream and sunscreen, so both counts rise together without either one causing
the other. Graph A only records what happened alongside what; nobody changed the
ice-cream sales and then watched sunscreen respond. An association is a
\emph{pattern in the pairs}, and a pattern is consistent with three different
stories: ice cream drives sunscreen, sunscreen drives ice cream, or a third
variable drives both.

\emph{Look for:} a named third variable that plausibly moves \textbf{both}
quantities (heat, sunshine, summer holidays, tourist season --- not "people like
summer"), plus the idea that association alone does not pick between the
possible stories.}

\problem{\textbf{Table B. Ravi averaged all ten scores and got 78.5. Find the
correct mean for the group that used the app.}

Step 1: name Ravi's mistake --- he pooled both groups. A group mean uses only
that group's data; mixing in the non-users pulls the app group's mean down
toward the middle, which is exactly what $78.5$ is.

Step 2: add the five app scores: $88 + 79 + 95 + 84 + 74 = 420$.

Step 3: divide by how many scores there are, which is five: $420 \div 5$:

\ans{84}

Step 4: check the answer is believable --- $84$ sits inside the app group's range
from $74$ to $95$ $\checkmark$, while $78.5$ was being dragged down by scores
that do not belong to this group.}

\problem{\textbf{Table B. Find the mean score of the group that did not use the
app.}

Step 1: use only the second row of the table --- the same rule as problem 4,
applied to the other group.

Step 2: add: $72 + 66 + 81 + 70 + 76 = 365$.

Step 3: divide by five: $365 \div 5$:

\ans{73}

Step 4: compare the two group means: $84$ against $73$, a gap of $11$ points in
favour of the app users. The gap is real --- what problem 6 asks is what the gap
is \emph{evidence of}.}

\problem{\textbf{Table B. The advertisement claims the app raises scores by
eleven points. Why can this study not support that, and what would?}
\emph{(Open response --- review by hand.)}

\emph{Model answer.} The students \textbf{chose for themselves} whether to use
the app, so the two groups were not alike before the app was introduced. The
kind of student who downloads a tutoring app is often already the student who
studies more, sleeps enough, has a quiet place to work, or started the year
ahead. Any of those differences could produce an eleven-point gap on its own, so
the gap cannot be assigned to the app. This is a self-selected observational
study, and it can establish association only.

\emph{The fix:} \textbf{random assignment}. Take one group of students, and by
coin flip (or a random-number list) assign each one to use the app or not.
Random assignment makes the two groups alike on average in every respect ---
including things nobody thought to measure --- so a score gap that shows up
afterwards can be credited to the app. Keeping everything else the same for both
groups, and using enough students, is part of the same fix.

\emph{Look for:} the words self-selected / not randomly assigned / the groups
may already differ, at least one concrete pre-existing difference, and random
assignment (or a randomized controlled experiment) as the remedy.}

\problem{\textbf{Graph C. Sam reported the effect as 5 by subtracting television
hours. Going from the 1-hour student to the 6-hour student, what is the change
in hours of sleep?}

Step 1: name Sam's mistake --- $6 - 1 = 5$ is the change in \emph{television}
hours, the horizontal axis. Sleep is the vertical axis.

Step 2: read the two sleep values: the $1$-hour student sleeps $9$ hours, the
$6$-hour student sleeps $6.5$ hours.

Step 3: change means end value minus start value: $6.5 - 9$:

\ans{-2.5}

Step 4: read the sign --- it is negative, so sleep \emph{falls} by $2.5$ hours
across that range. That is a negative association, which is all the graph
reports.}

\problem{\textbf{Challenge --- Graph C. Rewrite Sam's headline as a claim the
graph supports, then describe the experiment that would test the cause claim.}
\emph{(Open response --- review by hand.)}

\emph{Model rewrite.} ``Among these six students, more television hours went with
fewer hours of sleep.'' Other acceptable forms: ``Television time and sleep are
negatively associated in this sample,'' or ``Students who watched more
television slept less on this night.'' What must go: \emph{makes}, \emph{causes},
\emph{leads to}, and any claim about students in general.

\emph{Model experiment.} Recruit a large group of similar students. Assign each
student at random --- coin flip or random-number list, not by preference --- to a
fixed television allowance for the evening: say a low-television group and a
high-television group. Hold bedtime routine, homework load and wake-up time the
same for everyone, measure each student's sleep, and repeat over several nights.
Because the television time was assigned rather than chosen, a difference in
sleep between the groups can be credited to television.

\emph{Look for:} a rewritten claim with no causal verb and scoped to the data
collected; and an experiment with (a) random assignment, (b) an assigned
television level rather than a self-reported one, and (c) other conditions held
the same. A plan that merely surveys more students still cannot show cause ---
that is the point of the whole sheet.}
