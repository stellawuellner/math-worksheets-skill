\problem{\textbf{$f(x) = 3x + a$ for $x < 2$, \ $x^2 + 1$ for $x \geq 2$. Find $a$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Both pieces are polynomials, so each is continuous on its own
side. The only place continuity can fail is the junction $x = 2$, and there the
test reduces to: left-hand limit $=$ right-hand limit $= f(2)$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 2^-} f(x) = 3(2) + a = 6 + a$,
\qquad $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 2^+} f(x) = 2^2 + 1 = 5 = f(2)$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Set them equal: $6 + a = 5$, so \ans{a = -1}

\textit{Check:} with $a = -1$ the left piece gives $3(2) - 1 = 5$, matching the
right piece.}

\problem{\textbf{$f(x) = \dfrac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3}$ for $x \neq 3$, \ $k$ at $x = 3$. Find $k$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Direct substitution gives $\frac{0}{0}$, an indeterminate
form, so the limit is not read off by plugging in --- factor and cancel the
common factor first.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\dfrac{x^2 - 9}{x - 3} = \dfrac{(x - 3)(x + 3)}{x - 3}
= x + 3$ for every $x \neq 3$, so
$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 3} f(x) = 3 + 3 = 6$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Continuity at $x = 3$ requires $f(3) = \lim_{x \to 3} f(x)$,
so \ans{k = 6}

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $0$ --- the wrong factor was cancelled and $x - 3$
was evaluated at $x = 3$. Cancel $(x-3)$, then substitute into what is left.}

\problem{\textbf{$T(t) = 4t + c$ on $0 \leq t < 5$, \ $32 - 2t$ for $t \geq 5$.
Find $c$ in degrees Celsius.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The model must not jump at the moment the two rules change
over, so the two one-sided limits at $t = 5$ minutes have to agree.

\textbf{Step 2.} From the left: $4(5) + c = 20 + c$ degrees Celsius. From the
right: $32 - 2(5) = 22$ degrees Celsius, which is also $T(5)$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $20 + c = 22$, so \ans{c = 2}

\textit{Interpretation:} the oven is already at $2^\circ$C above ambient when
the timer starts; without that offset the model would drop instantaneously by
$2^\circ$C at the five-minute mark.}

\problem{\textbf{$f(x) = x^2$ on $x \leq 1$, \ $ax + b$ on $1 < x < 4$, \ $10$ on
$x \geq 4$. Find $a$ and $b$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} There are two junctions, $x = 1$ and $x = 4$, and each one
gives one equation --- two equations for the two unknowns, so the system is
determined.

\textbf{Step 2.} At $x = 1$: the left piece gives $1^2 = 1$ and the middle
piece approaches $a(1) + b$, so $a + b = 1$.

\textbf{Step 3.} At $x = 4$: the middle piece approaches $a(4) + b$ and the
right piece is $10$, so $4a + b = 10$.

\textbf{Step 4.} Subtract the first equation from the second: $3a = 9$, so
$a = 3$, and then $b = 1 - 3 = -2$. \ans{a = 3, \ b = -2}

\textit{Check:} $3(1) - 2 = 1$ matches $x^2$ at $x = 1$, and $3(4) - 2 = 10$
matches the constant piece.}

\problem{\textbf{$f(x) = \dfrac{\sqrt{x + 7} - 3}{x - 2}$ for $x \neq 2$, \ $k$
at $x = 2$. Find $k$ exactly.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Substitution gives $\frac{0}{0}$ again, but nothing factors ---
with a radical in the numerator the tool is the conjugate, which converts the
difference of square roots into a difference of squares.

\textbf{Step 2.} Multiply numerator and denominator by $\sqrt{x + 7} + 3$:
\[
\frac{(\sqrt{x+7} - 3)(\sqrt{x+7} + 3)}{(x - 2)(\sqrt{x+7} + 3)}
= \frac{(x + 7) - 9}{(x - 2)(\sqrt{x+7} + 3)}
= \frac{x - 2}{(x - 2)(\sqrt{x+7} + 3)} .
\]

\textbf{Step 3.} Cancel $x - 2$ and substitute: the limit is
$\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{9} + 3} = \dfrac{1}{6}$, so \ans{k = \tfrac{1}{6}}

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $0$ --- the numerator tends to $0$, but so does
the denominator, and a $\frac{0}{0}$ form has to be resolved before any value
can be read off.}

\problem{\textbf{$f(x) = \dfrac{e^{4x} - 1}{2x}$ for $x \neq 0$, \ $k$ at
$x = 0$. Find $k$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Substitution gives $\frac{0}{0}$. The numerator is the
difference quotient shape for $e^{4x}$ at $x = 0$, so either the known limit
$\lim_{u \to 0} \frac{e^u - 1}{u} = 1$ or L'H\^opital's rule settles it.

\textbf{Step 2.} Write $u = 4x$: \quad
$\dfrac{e^{4x} - 1}{2x} = \dfrac{e^{u} - 1}{u} \cdot \dfrac{4x}{2x}
= \dfrac{e^{u} - 1}{u} \cdot 2 \to 1 \cdot 2$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The limit is $2$, and continuity forces $f(0)$ to equal it, so
\ans{k = 2}

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $4$ --- the exponent was read as the answer and
the $2$ in the denominator was ignored.}

\problem{\textbf{$f(x) = 2x + a$ for $x < 3$, \ $x^2 + b$ on $3 \leq x < 5$,
\ $4x + 1$ for $x \geq 5$. Find $a$ and $b$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Two junctions again, but they are not symmetric: the junction
at $x = 5$ involves only $b$, so solve that one first and carry the value into
the other equation.

\textbf{Step 2.} At $x = 5$: the middle piece approaches $5^2 + b = 25 + b$ and
the right piece gives $4(5) + 1 = 21$. So $25 + b = 21$ and $b = -4$.

\textbf{Step 3.} At $x = 3$: the left piece approaches $2(3) + a = 6 + a$ and
the middle piece gives $3^2 + b = 9 + b = 9 - 4 = 5$. So $6 + a = 5$.

\textbf{Step 4.} Therefore \ans{a = -1, \ b = -4}

\textit{Check:} the middle piece runs from $f(3) = 9 - 4 = 5$ up to
$5^2 - 4 = 21$, meeting both neighbours with no jump.}

\problem{\textbf{Why no $k$ makes $f(x) = \dfrac{1}{x - 3}$ (with $f(3) = k$)
continuous at $x = 3$. \ (Manual review --- open reasoning.)}

\textbf{Model answer.} Continuity at $x = 3$ requires
$\lim_{x \to 3} f(x)$ to exist \emph{and} to equal $f(3)$. Examine the two
one-sided limits.

\textbf{Step 1.} For $x$ slightly less than $3$, $x - 3$ is a small negative
number, so $\dfrac{1}{x - 3} \to -\infty$:
$\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 3^-} f(x) = -\infty$.

\textbf{Step 2.} For $x$ slightly greater than $3$, $x - 3$ is a small positive
number, so $\displaystyle \lim_{x \to 3^+} f(x) = +\infty$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Neither one-sided limit is a real number, so
$\lim_{x \to 3} f(x)$ does not exist. A value of $k$ can only supply $f(3)$; it
cannot create a limit that is not there. Hence \ans{\text{no such } k \text{ exists}}

\textbf{Step 4.} Because at least one one-sided limit is infinite, $x = 3$ is an
\emph{infinite} (vertical-asymptote) discontinuity, which is non-removable. The
student's $k = 5$ fixes nothing: $f$ still fails the limit condition.

\textit{Grading note:} full credit needs both one-sided limits stated (not just
``the function blows up''), the conclusion that the two-sided limit does not
exist, and the word \emph{infinite} or \emph{non-removable} for the
classification.}
