\problem{Circle $O$: central $\angle AOB = 110^\circ$, $C$ on the major arc.
Find $m\angle ACB$.}

\textbf{Solution:} $\angle ACB$ is inscribed in the circle and $\angle AOB$ is the
central angle standing on the \emph{same} arc $AB$, so the inscribed-angle theorem
applies: the inscribed angle is half the central angle.
$m\angle ACB = \tfrac{1}{2}(110^\circ)$.
\ans{m\angle ACB = 55^\circ}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$ with $m\angle A = 84^\circ$ and
$m\angle B = 71^\circ$. Find $m\angle C$ and $m\angle D$.}

\textbf{Solution:} In a quadrilateral inscribed in a circle the opposite angles are
supplementary, because each pair of opposite angles is inscribed on the two arcs
that together make the whole circle ($\tfrac{1}{2}(360^\circ) = 180^\circ$).
$\angle C$ is opposite $\angle A$, so $m\angle C = 180^\circ - 84^\circ$; $\angle D$
is opposite $\angle B$, so $m\angle D = 180^\circ - 71^\circ$.
\ans{m\angle C = 96^\circ}

Then, for the second pair:
\ans{m\angle D = 109^\circ}

\problem{Circle $O$, radius 9 cm, inscribed $\angle ABC = 40^\circ$. Find the length
of minor arc $AC$ to the nearest tenth of a centimetre.}

\textbf{Solution:} Two theorems, in order.
\emph{Inscribed angle:} the central angle on arc $AC$ is twice the inscribed angle,
so $m\angle AOC = 2(40^\circ) = 80^\circ$, and the minor arc $AC$ measures $80^\circ$.
\emph{Arc length:} an arc of $80^\circ$ is $\tfrac{80}{360}$ of the circumference,
\[
  \text{arc } AC = \frac{80}{360}\cdot 2\pi(9) = \frac{2}{9}\cdot 18\pi = 4\pi \approx 12.566
\]
\ans{12.6\ \text{cm}}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Chords $AB$ and $CD$ meet at interior point $P$ with $AP = 8$, $PB = x$,
$CP = 6$, $PD = 12$. Find $x$.}

\textbf{Solution:} The intersecting-chords theorem says the two products of the
pieces are equal: $AP \cdot PB = CP \cdot PD$, so
\[ 8x = 6 \cdot 12 = 72 \;\Longrightarrow\; x = \frac{72}{8} \]
Check: $8(9) = 72$ and $6(12) = 72$ $\checkmark$
\ans{x = 9}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Flowerbed of radius 10 cm; sector $AOB$ has central angle $144^\circ$.
Find the sector area to the nearest tenth of a square centimetre.}

\textbf{Solution:} A sector of $144^\circ$ is $\tfrac{144}{360} = \tfrac{2}{5}$ of the
whole disc, so its area is that fraction of $\pi r^2$:
\[
  \text{area} = \frac{144}{360}\cdot \pi (10)^2 = \frac{2}{5}(100\pi) = 40\pi \approx 125.664
\]
\ans{125.7\ \text{cm}^2}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$AB$ is a diameter of circle $O$, $C$ is on the circle, and
$m\angle ABC = 34^\circ$. Find $m\angle BAC$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Two theorems again.
\emph{Angle in a semicircle:} $\angle ACB$ is inscribed in a semicircle (it stands on
the diameter $AB$), so $m\angle ACB = 90^\circ$.
\emph{Triangle sum:} $m\angle BAC = 180^\circ - 90^\circ - 34^\circ$.
\ans{m\angle BAC = 56^\circ}

\problem{$x^2 + y^2 - 10x - 6y + 9 = 0$. (a) Centre-radius form, centre and radius.
(b) The $x$-values where the circle crosses the $x$-axis.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Group and complete the square in each variable. Half of $10$
is $5$ and half of $6$ is $3$, so add $5^2$ and $3^2$ to both sides:
\begin{align*}
  (x^2 - 10x) + (y^2 - 6y) &= -9 \\
  (x^2 - 10x + 25) + (y^2 - 6y + 9) &= -9 + 25 + 9 \\
  (x - 5)^2 + (y - 3)^2 &= 25
\end{align*}
The centre is $(h,k)$ read off with the opposite signs, and the radius is
$\sqrt{25}$.
\ans{\text{centre } (5,3),\quad r = 5}

\textbf{Solution (b):} A point on the $x$-axis has $y = 0$, so substitute $y = 0$
into the original equation:
\[ x^2 - 10x + 9 = 0 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x - 1)(x - 9) = 0 \]
Both crossings are real because the distance from the centre to the $x$-axis is
$3$, which is less than the radius $5$.
\ans{x = 1 \text{ and } x = 9}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Tangent $PT = 12$ from exterior point $P$; a secant through $P$ meets the
circle at $A$ then $B$, with $PA = 8$. Find $AB = x$.}

\textbf{Solution:} The tangent-secant (power of a point) theorem says
$PT^2 = PA \cdot PB$. The whole secant is $PB = PA + AB = 8 + x$, so
\begin{align*}
  12^2 &= 8(8 + x) \\
  144 &= 64 + 8x \\
  8x &= 80
\end{align*}
Check: $PB = 8 + 10 = 18$ and $8(18) = 144 = 12^2$ $\checkmark$
\ans{x = 10}

\problem{Circle $O$, radius 14 cm, $\angle AOB = 90^\circ$. Find the area of the
circular segment cut off by $AB$, to the nearest tenth of a square centimetre.}

\textbf{Solution:} The segment is what is left of the sector once the triangle is
removed, so this needs the sector formula \emph{and} the triangle area.
\emph{Sector:} $\dfrac{90}{360}\pi(14)^2 = \dfrac{1}{4}(196\pi) = 49\pi \approx 153.938$ cm$^2$.
\emph{Triangle:} $\angle AOB$ is a right angle between two radii, so the triangle is
right-angled with legs $14$ and $14$: area $= \tfrac{1}{2}(14)(14) = 98$ cm$^2$.
\emph{Segment:} $153.938 - 98 = 55.938$.
\ans{55.9\ \text{cm}^2}

\problem{Fountain $x^2 + y^2 - 8x + 6y - 11 = 0$ (metres). (a) Centre and radius.
(b) Area of a $120^\circ$ sector. (c) Explain the fraction.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Complete the square: half of $8$ is $4$, half of $6$ is $3$,
\[
  (x^2 - 8x + 16) + (y^2 + 6y + 9) = 11 + 16 + 9 \;\Longrightarrow\; (x-4)^2 + (y+3)^2 = 36
\]
so the radius is $\sqrt{36}$ and the centre is $(4,-3)$ --- note the sign flip on the
$y$ term: $(y+3)^2 = (y - (-3))^2$.
\ans{\text{centre } (4,-3),\quad r = 6\ \text{m}}

\textbf{Solution (b):} The watered region is a sector of the disc of radius $6$:
\[
  \text{area} = \frac{120}{360}\cdot \pi (6)^2 = \frac{1}{3}(36\pi) = 12\pi \approx 37.699
\]
\ans{37.7\ \text{m}^2}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- model answer (open response, review by hand):}
A full turn about the centre is $360^\circ$, and the sprinkler sweeps $120^\circ$ of
it. Sectors with the same radius have areas in the same ratio as their central
angles, because a sector of angle $\theta$ is exactly what you get by cutting the
disc into $\tfrac{360}{\theta}$ congruent pieces and keeping one. Since
$\tfrac{120}{360} = \tfrac{1}{3}$, the watered region is one third of the fountain's
area --- and that is why the formula multiplies $\pi r^2$ by $\tfrac{\theta}{360}$
rather than by $\theta$ itself.
