## What the worksheet covers

Halves, thirds, fourths, sixths and eighths, all on drawn number lines — every problem has its own
line on the page beside it.

The sheet ramps deliberately and no two problems share a skeleton:

- **1–3** read a marked point (fourths, thirds, then eighths, where the ticks get close together and
  counting has to replace guessing).
- **4–5** turn it around: the child places the dot and then says how many jumps of the unit fraction
  that is. Problem 5 crosses $1$ for the first time, with $\frac{3}{2}$ on a line of halves.
- **6** compares two points on one line of eighths — same jump size, different counts.
- **7** reads a point past the whole: $\frac{5}{3}$ on a line running $0$ to $2$.
- **8** compares $\frac{3}{4}$ with $\frac{3}{6}$ on two same-length lines, which is the problem that
  breaks the "bigger bottom number means bigger fraction" habit.
- **9–10** finish with placement on eighths and a synthesis item: how many eighths land exactly where
  $\frac{3}{4}$ lands, and why the two lines show it.

## What is verified, and what is not

- **10 of the 14 checks are machine-verified** with SymPy: every fraction named, every jump count,
  and both comparisons were recomputed independently of the answer key. **6 of the 10 problems are
  fully machine-checked.**
- **Four responses are instructor-judged**, and the key marks them `---` in the Quick Answers list
  rather than implying they were checked: the dot placements in 4(a), 5(a) and 9(a) (a machine
  cannot see where a pencil went) and the written explanation in 10(b). For each one the answer key
  states exactly what to look for — for the placements, which tick the dot must sit on and what is
  *not* credit; for 10(b), that the explanation must mention the two lines landing on the same point,
  not just repeat the number six.
- The key's "What is verified" note names problems 4, 5, 9 and 10, so nothing about the split is
  hidden in fine print.

## The study guide

Three sections: reading a point, placing a fraction by counting jumps, and comparing on a line. Each
carries a rule box, a worked example that opens by saying *why* that method applies, and a try-it with
its answer upside down inside the box. The watch-out box flags the single most common error on this
topic — counting tick marks instead of the spaces between them (fourths show five ticks, not four).

The grade level and the standards code (3.NF.A) appear on the answer key only.
