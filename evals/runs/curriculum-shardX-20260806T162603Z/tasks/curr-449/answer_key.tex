\problem{``Sales doubled --- up $100\%$!'' $4$ units last month, $8$ this month, out of $5000$
units sold. \ (a) share of total sales. \ (b) why is the headline misleading?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $\dfrac{8}{5000}$, and dividing both parts by $8$ gives
$\dfrac{1}{625}$. \ans{\tfrac{1}{625}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The doubling is real, but the base is tiny:
the flavour went from $0.08\%$ to $0.16\%$ of sales, four extra units out of five thousand. A
percentage change computed on a very small starting number is large almost automatically, so
quoting it without the base figure gives a false impression of importance. Full credit requires
naming the small base as the reason.

\problem{$3000$ members, $240$ replied to a website survey, $210$ liked the logo. \ (a)
proportion of respondents. \ (b) why not a claim about all members?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $\dfrac{210}{240}$, dividing both parts by $30$. \ans{\tfrac{7}{8}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The $240$ chose to answer --- a voluntary
response sample. Members with strong feelings are much likelier to reply than the $2760$ who
did not, so the respondents are not a random slice of the membership and the seven-eighths
figure describes only them. Full credit requires naming voluntary response, self-selection or
nonresponse bias.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Ice cream sales and pool rescues, $r = 0.90$. \ (a) $r^2$. \ (b) name the flaw.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $r^2 = 0.90^2$. \ans{0.81}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad Hot weather is a common cause: it
independently raises ice cream sales and the number of people swimming, and therefore the
number of rescues. Neither variable acts on the other, so however strong the correlation, it is
not evidence of causation. Full credit requires naming a plausible common cause, not merely
saying "correlation is not causation".

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{$60$ people leaving a gym, $45$ say they exercise daily. \ (a) sample proportion.
\ (b) name the bias and its direction.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $\dfrac{45}{60}$, dividing both parts by $15$. \ans{\tfrac{3}{4}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad This is a convenience sample taken at the one
place where daily exercisers congregate; being there is itself evidence of exercising. People
who never exercise had almost no chance of being asked, so the estimate is biased \emph{upward}
and cannot be reported for the town. Full credit requires naming the convenience or
undercoverage bias and the upward direction.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Course group $78, 80, 82, 84, 86$; no-course group $70, 72, 74, 76, 78$. \ (a) and
(b) the means. \ (c) does the course work?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $\dfrac{78+80+82+84+86}{5} = \dfrac{410}{5}$. \ans{82}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad $\dfrac{70+72+74+76+78}{5} = \dfrac{370}{5}$. \ans{74}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The arithmetic gap is $8$ points, but the
students \emph{chose} whether to take the course. The groups may already have differed in
motivation, prior achievement or family resources, and any of those confounding variables
explains the gap just as well as the course does. Only an experiment --- assigning students to
course or no course at random --- balances the unknown variables and would support the causal
claim. Full credit requires naming self-selection or confounding and proposing random
assignment.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Risk falls from $2$ per $1000$ to $1$ per $1000$, advertised as a $50\%$ cut.
\ (a) absolute change. \ (b) why misleading?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $\dfrac{2}{1000} - \dfrac{1}{1000}$. \ans{\tfrac{1}{1000}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad Both figures are correct descriptions of the
same result: the \emph{relative} reduction is $50\%$, the \emph{absolute} reduction is one case
per thousand people. The headline quotes the larger-sounding relative number and omits the base
rate, so a reader imagines a benefit far bigger than one person in a thousand. Full credit
requires both figures named and the point that the relative figure hides the base rate.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Fertilizer on the sunny half: $150$ of $200$ treated thrived, $100$ of $200$ untreated
thrived. \ (a) proportion thriving when treated. \ (b) flaw and fix.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $\dfrac{150}{200}$, dividing both parts by $50$. \ans{\tfrac{3}{4}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad Every treated plant was also a sunny plant,
so sunlight and fertilizer are completely confounded: the higher survival could be caused by
either, and the data cannot separate them. The fix is to assign the treatment at random across
the whole field, or --- better, since sun exposure is known to matter --- to \emph{block} by
half and randomize within each half, so both treatments appear in sun and in shade. Full credit
requires naming the confounding and proposing randomization or blocking.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{\textbf{Synthesis.} Admissions Nov $120$, Dec $210$, Jan $260$, Feb $190$; flu shots
run over the same months. \ (a) total. \ (b) busiest month. \ (c) diagnose the claim.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $120 + 210 + 260 + 190$. \ans{780}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad The largest of the four monthly figures is January's. \ans{260}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The two series rise together because winter
drives both: cold weather fills hospitals and is also when vaccination campaigns are scheduled.
The season is a common cause, so shared timing is no evidence at all of a causal link. The
direction could even run the other way, since people who feel at risk are the ones who both
seek a shot and seek care. Settling the question requires a randomized trial: assign people at
random to receive the vaccine or not and compare admission rates between the groups. Full
credit requires the common-cause diagnosis and a randomized comparison as the remedy; noting
the possible reverse direction is a strong bonus.
