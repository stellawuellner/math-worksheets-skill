Three PDFs are ready:

## What the worksheet covers

Every problem is built on a drawn area model, and every model is on the page with the problem it
belongs to, so nothing has to be read across a page.

- **Problems 1–3** name the unit fraction shaded in a bar, a circle and a grid — the same rule in
  three shapes, so the child learns that the *count of equal pieces* is what matters, not the shape.
- **Problem 4** adds the reverse question: one piece is $\frac{1}{8}$, so how many of those pieces
  rebuild the whole bar? That is the idea that makes fractions numbers rather than pictures.
- **Problem 5** is the equal-parts check. Two models are each cut into four pieces, but only one has
  four *equal* pieces. The child circles the right one and writes why the other is not fourths.
- **Problems 6–7** compare unit fractions using same-size wholes: one third against one sixth, then
  ordering one half, one fourth and one eighth from smallest to largest.
- **Problem 8** is the stretch item — name the unit fraction for one plot of a garden bed, then shade
  three plots and describe the shaded amount as copies of that unit fraction.

Difficulty ramps 1 → 5, and the models get less regular as the sheet goes on.

## What is verified, and what is not

- **8 of the 11 checks are machine-verified** with SymPy, covering **6 of the 8 problems fully**:
  every named fraction, the count of eighths in a whole, the comparison $\frac{1}{3} > \frac{1}{6}$,
  and the ordering $\frac{1}{8}, \frac{1}{4}, \frac{1}{2}$ were recomputed independently of the key.
- **Three responses are instructor-judged**, and the key marks them `---` rather than pretending
  otherwise: problem 5(a) *and* 5(b) (which model shows fourths, and why the other does not) and
  problem 8(b) (shade three plots and explain the amount). The answer key states exactly what a
  full-credit answer must contain for each — for 5(b) the explanation has to mention that the pieces
  are not the same size, not merely that there are four of them.
- The "What is verified" note on the key names problems 5 and 8 explicitly, so you can see at a
  glance where your judgement is needed.

One cosmetic note: in the key's at-a-glance Quick Answers list, a unit fraction is typeset by the
generator as $1 \cdot \frac{1}{4}$ rather than $\frac{1}{4}$. The value is right and the worked
solutions below it print the fraction normally; it is a formatting quirk of the answer-bank
generator, reported upstream.

## The study guide

Three sections: naming a unit fraction from a model, checking that the pieces are *equal* (the
misconception this topic lives or dies on), and comparing unit fractions on same-size wholes. Each
has a rule box, a worked example whose first step says why that method applies, and a try-it with
the answer printed upside down inside the box.

The grade level and standards code (3.NF.A) appear on the answer key only.
