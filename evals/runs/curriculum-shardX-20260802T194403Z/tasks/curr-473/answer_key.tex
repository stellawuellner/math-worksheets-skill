\problem{$\displaystyle g(x) = \int_1^{x}(t^3 + 2)\,dt$ has a constant lower limit and the
variable alone on top, so the FTC applies directly: $g'(x) = x^3 + 2$ --- the integrand with
$t$ replaced by $x$. Evaluating the integral first is legal but wasted work.
\[ g'(3) = 3^3 + 2 = 27 + 2 \]
\ans{g'(3) = 29}}

\problem{Here the question asks for accumulated volume, not a rate, so the integral must
actually be evaluated (FTC Part 2). An antiderivative of $6t + 4$ is $3t^2 + 4t$:
\[ V(5) = \int_0^5 (6t + 4)\,dt = \Big[3t^2 + 4t\Big]_0^5 = (75 + 20) - 0 \]
\ans{95 \ \text{L}}

\noindent{\small Units check: (litres per minute) $\times$ (minutes) $=$ litres, which is why
an area under a rate graph is a volume.}}

\problem{The upper limit is $x^2$, not $x$, so this is the chain-rule form:
if $g(x) = \int_a^{u(x)} f(t)\,dt$ then $g'(x) = f(u(x)) \cdot u'(x)$.
\begin{align*}
g'(x) &= \sqrt{1 + (x^2)^3} \cdot 2x = 2x\sqrt{1 + x^6}\\
g'(2) &= 2(2)\sqrt{1 + 2^6} = 4\sqrt{65} \approx 32.25
\end{align*}
\ans{g'(2) = 4\sqrt{65}}

\noindent{\small Forgetting the factor $u'(x) = 2x$ gives $\sqrt{65} \approx 8.06$, a
quarter of the correct value --- the single most common slip on this problem type.}}

\problem{\textbf{Open response --- graded on the justification.} A complete answer uses
$g'(x) = f(x)$ (FTC) three times:

\emph{Increasing.} On $[0,3]$ the table shows $f(t) \ge 0$ (values $4, 3, 2, 0$), so
$g'(x) = f(x) \ge 0$ there and $g$ is increasing on $[0,3]$.

\emph{Decreasing.} On $[3,7]$ the table shows $f(t) \le 0$ (values $0, -2, -3, -4, -5$), so
$g'(x) = f(x) \le 0$ there and $g$ is decreasing on $[3,7]$.

\emph{Maximum.} $g'$ changes sign from positive to negative at $x = 3$, and $g$ is
continuous, so by the first-derivative test $g$ has its absolute maximum on $[0,7]$ at
$x = 3$. (Because $g$ increases up to $x=3$ and decreases afterwards, no endpoint can beat
it.)

\noindent{\small Accept an answer that says ``$g$ is increasing where $f$ is above the
axis'' provided it names $g' = f$ as the reason. Do \emph{not} accept ``$g$ has a maximum at
$x = 3$ because $f$ has a zero there'' with no sign analysis: a zero of $f$ with no sign
change would give no extremum. Note also that $f$ decreasing means $g$ is concave down
throughout --- a good follow-up question.}}

\problem{The variable sits in the \textbf{lower} limit, so flip the limits first --- that
introduces a minus sign, and the FTC then applies to the flipped form:
\[ h(x) = \int_x^4 \ln(t^2+1)\,dt = -\int_4^x \ln(t^2+1)\,dt
   \;\Longrightarrow\; h'(x) = -\ln(x^2+1) \]
\[ h'(2) = -\ln(2^2 + 1) = -\ln 5 \approx -1.609 \]
\ans{h'(2) = -\ln 5}

\noindent{\small The sign is the whole point: as $x$ increases, the interval $[x,4]$ shrinks,
so an accumulation with a variable lower limit \emph{decreases} whenever the integrand is
positive.}}

\problem{\textbf{(a)} Accumulated volume needs the integral evaluated. An antiderivative of
$12 - t$ is $12t - \tfrac{1}{2}t^2$:
\[ V(10) = \Big[12t - \tfrac{1}{2}t^2\Big]_0^{10} = 120 - 50 \]
so $70$ litres are added in the first $10$ minutes. \ans{70}

\textbf{(b)} $V$ is an accumulation function of $r$, so the FTC gives $V'(x) = r(x)$ with no
work: $V'(10) = r(10) = 12 - 10$, i.e. $2$ litres per minute. At $t = 10$ minutes the
reservoir is still gaining water, but only at $2$ L/min --- the inflow rate has fallen to a
sixth of its starting value of $12$ L/min. \ans{2}}

\problem{\textbf{(a)} Evaluate with FTC Part 2. An antiderivative of $4t^3 - 6t$ is
$t^4 - 3t^2$, and it vanishes at $t = 0$:
\[ g(x) = \Big[t^4 - 3t^2\Big]_0^{x} = x^4 - 3x^2 - 0 \]
\ans{g(x) = x^4 - 3x^2}

\textbf{(b)} Differentiating that polynomial gives $4x^3 - 6x$, which is exactly the
integrand with $t$ replaced by $x$ --- precisely what the FTC predicted, since
$g(x) = \int_0^x f(t)\,dt$ forces $g' = f$. \ans{g'(x) = 4x^3 - 6x}

\noindent{\small This problem is the theorem in miniature: integrating and then
differentiating returns the original function.}}

\problem{\textbf{(a)} Chain-rule form with $u(x) = x^2$ and $f(t) = \dfrac{3}{t}$:
\[ g'(x) = f\!\left(x^2\right) \cdot \frac{d}{dx}\!\left(x^2\right)
        = \frac{3}{x^2} \cdot 2x = \frac{6x}{x^2} \]
which simplifies to \ans{g'(x) = \dfrac{6}{x}}

\textbf{(b)} $g'(2) = \dfrac{6}{2}$. \ans{g'(2) = 3}

\noindent{\small Worth showing the class: evaluating first gives
$g(x) = 3\ln(x^2) = 6\ln x$, whose derivative is again $6/x$. Two routes, one answer --- the
chain-rule form is simply the one that does not require the integral to be doable.}}
