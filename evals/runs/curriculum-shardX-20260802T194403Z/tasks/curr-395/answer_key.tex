\problem{\textbf{Evaluate $\dbinom{7}{3}$.}\\
\textbf{Step 1 (choose the tool).} A binomial coefficient counts unordered
selections, so use $\binom{n}{r} = \frac{n!}{r!(n-r)!}$ with $n = 7$, $r = 3$.
\textbf{Step 2 (cancel first).} $\dfrac{7!}{3!\,4!}$: cancel the $4!$ against
the tail of $7!$, leaving $\dfrac{7 \cdot 6 \cdot 5}{3 \cdot 2 \cdot 1}$.
\textbf{Step 3 (multiply).} $\dfrac{210}{6} = \ans{35}$. Cancelling before
multiplying keeps the arithmetic small enough to do without a calculator.}

\problem{\textbf{Evaluate $\dbinom{9}{2}$; a classmate answers 72.}\\
\textbf{Step 1 (name the classmate's count).} 72 is the number of \emph{ordered}
pairs that can be drawn from 9 objects --- the permutation count, in which
choosing A then B is different from choosing B then A.
\textbf{Step 2 (correct the count).} A binomial coefficient counts unordered
selections, so every pair has been counted twice; divide by $2! = 2$.
\textbf{Step 3 (compute).} $\dbinom{9}{2} = \dfrac{9!}{2!\,7!}
= \dfrac{9 \cdot 8}{2} = \ans{36}$.}

\problem{\textbf{Expand $(x+3)^{3}$.}\\
\textbf{Step 1 (set up the row).} With $n = 3$ the coefficients are
$1, 3, 3, 1$, and the terms run $x^{3}$, $x^{2}$, $x^{1}$, $x^{0}$ with the
matching powers of 3.
\textbf{Step 2 (build each term).} $1 \cdot x^{3}$, \ $3 \cdot x^{2} \cdot 3$,
\ $3 \cdot x \cdot 3^{2}$, \ $1 \cdot 3^{3}$.
\textbf{Step 3 (collect).}
\[ \boxed{x^{3}+9x^{2}+27x+27} \]}

\problem{\textbf{Coefficient of $x^{2}$ in $(x+4)^{5}$.}\\
\textbf{Step 1 (find $r$).} The general term is
$\binom{5}{r}x^{5-r}4^{r}$; the power of $x$ is $5-r$, so $x^{2}$ needs
$r = 3$.
\textbf{Step 2 (evaluate both pieces).} $\dbinom{5}{3} = 10$ and
$4^{3} = 64$ --- the constant's power is what most answers leave out.
\textbf{Step 3 (multiply).} $10 \cdot 64 = \ans{640}$. Reporting 10 alone would
give the count of terms of that shape, not the coefficient.}

\problem{\textbf{Sum of row 6 of Pascal's triangle.}\\
\textbf{Step 1 (write the row as a sum).} The row is
$\sum_{r=0}^{6}\binom{6}{r}$.
\textbf{Step 2 (use the theorem).} Substituting $p = 1$ and $q = 1$ into
$(p+q)^{6} = \sum \binom{6}{r}p^{6-r}q^{r}$ turns every power into 1, so the
expansion collapses to exactly that sum of coefficients, and the left side
becomes $2^{6}$.
\textbf{Step 3 (compute).} $2^{6} = \ans{64}$; adding the entries
$1+6+15+20+15+6+1$ gives 64 as a check.}

\problem{\textbf{Expand $(2x-1)^{4}$.}\\
\textbf{Step 1 (identify $p$ and $q$).} Here $p = 2x$ and $q = -1$, so both the
2 and the minus sign travel with their factor through every term.
\textbf{Step 2 (build the terms).} Coefficients $1, 4, 6, 4, 1$ multiply
$(2x)^{4}$, $(2x)^{3}(-1)$, $(2x)^{2}(-1)^{2}$, $(2x)(-1)^{3}$, $(-1)^{4}$,
giving $16x^{4}$, $-32x^{3}$, $24x^{2}$, $-8x$, $1$.
\textbf{Step 3 (collect).}
\[ \boxed{16x^{4}-32x^{3}+24x^{2}-8x+1} \]
The signs alternate because $q$ is negative and its power climbs by one each
term.}

\problem{\textbf{Coefficient of $y^{4}$ in $(3+y)^{6}$.}\\
\textbf{Step 1 (find $r$).} With $p = 3$ and $q = y$, the general term is
$\binom{6}{r}3^{6-r}y^{r}$, so $y^{4}$ needs $r = 4$.
\textbf{Step 2 (evaluate both pieces).} $\dbinom{6}{4} = 15$, and the exponent
on 3 is $6-4 = 2$, so the constant factor is $3^{2} = 9$.
\textbf{Step 3 (multiply).} $15 \cdot 9 = \ans{135}$. The exponent on 3 comes
from the \emph{other} factor of the term: the two exponents must always add
to 6.}

\problem{\textbf{Solve $\dfrac{n(n-1)}{2} = 45$.}\\
\textbf{Step 1 (clear the fraction).} $n(n-1) = 90$, so $n^{2}-n-90 = 0$.
\textbf{Step 2 (factor).} $(n-10)(n+9) = 0$, giving the two solutions
$\ans{n = 10}$ and $\ans{n = -9}$.
\textbf{Step 3 (interpret).} A count of teams must be a positive integer, so
$n = 10$ is the answer to the tournament question; $-9$ solves the equation but
not the situation. Check: $\dbinom{10}{2} = 45$ $\checkmark$.}

\problem{\textbf{Sum of the coefficients of $(1+2x)^{5}$.}\\
\textbf{Step 1 (name the substitution).} The sum of the coefficients of any
polynomial in $x$ is its value at $x = 1$, because every power of 1 is 1.
\textbf{Step 2 (substitute).} $(1+2 \cdot 1)^{5} = 3^{5}$.
\textbf{Step 3 (compute).} $3^{5} = \ans{243}$. The familiar $2^{n}$ row sum is
only the special case where the second term is 1; here it is 2, so the base
is 3.}

\problem{\textbf{Expand $(a+2b)^{3}$.}\\
\textbf{Step 1 (identify $p$ and $q$).} $p = a$ and $q = 2b$, so each term
carries a power of 2 as well as a power of $b$.
\textbf{Step 2 (build the terms).} Coefficients $1, 3, 3, 1$ multiply
$a^{3}$, $a^{2}(2b)$, $a(2b)^{2}$, $(2b)^{3}$, giving $a^{3}$, $6a^{2}b$,
$12ab^{2}$, $8b^{3}$.
\textbf{Step 3 (collect).}
\[ \boxed{a^{3}+6a^{2}b+12ab^{2}+8b^{3}} \]}

\problem{\textbf{Coefficient of $x^{3}y^{2}$ in $(2x+y)^{5}$.}\\
\textbf{Step 1 (find $r$).} The general term is
$\binom{5}{r}(2x)^{5-r}y^{r}$, and $y^{2}$ needs $r = 2$, which leaves
$(2x)^{3}$.
\textbf{Step 2 (evaluate both pieces).} $\dbinom{5}{2} = 10$ and
$(2x)^{3} = 8x^{3}$, so the numeric factor is $10 \cdot 8$.
\textbf{Step 3 (multiply).} The coefficient is $\ans{80}$. It is not the
Pascal's-triangle entry 10, because the first term of the binomial is $2x$, not
$x$, and that 2 is raised to the third power along with $x$.}

\problem{\textbf{Synthesis: sum of the coefficients of $(3x-2)^{4}$.}\\
\textbf{Step 1 (substitute).} Evaluate the polynomial at $x = 1$:
$(3 \cdot 1 - 2)^{4} = (3-2)^{4}$.
\textbf{Step 2 (compute).} $(3-2)^{4} = 1^{4} = \ans{1}$.
\textbf{Step 3 (interpret).} The sum of the coefficients \emph{is} the value of
the polynomial at $x = 1$, so this expansion is worth 1 there. The individual
coefficients are large --- 81, $-216$, 216, $-96$, 16 --- but they alternate in
sign, so they very nearly cancel. Ignoring the minus sign and computing
$(3+2)^{4} = 625$ answers a different question entirely: it is the sum of the
absolute values of the coefficients.}
