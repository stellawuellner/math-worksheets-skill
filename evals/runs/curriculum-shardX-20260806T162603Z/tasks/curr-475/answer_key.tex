\problem{Right Riemann sum, $n = 4$, for $\int_0^2 x^2dx$.}

\textbf{Solution.} $\Delta x = \dfrac{2 - 0}{4} = \dfrac{1}{2}$ and the right
endpoints are $\tfrac{1}{2}, 1, \tfrac{3}{2}, 2$, so the sum is
\[ \frac{1}{2}\left(\frac{1}{4} + 1 + \frac{9}{4} + 4\right)
   = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{15}{2}. \]
\[ \boxed{\frac{15}{4}} \]
That is $3.75$, an overestimate of the exact $\tfrac{8}{3}$ because $x^2$ is
increasing here.

\problem{$r(t) = 4t + 3$ L/min. Total volume in the first $6$ minutes.}

\textbf{Solution.} The total is the accumulated rate:
$\int_0^6 (4t + 3)\,dt = \left[2t^2 + 3t\right]_0^6 = 72 + 18$.
\[ \boxed{90\ \text{L}} \]

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$G(x) = \int_2^x (t^3 - 1)\,dt$. Find $G'(3)$.}

\textbf{Solution.} By the Fundamental Theorem $G'(x) = x^3 - 1$, so
$G'(3) = 27 - 1$.
\[ \boxed{G'(3) = 26} \]
No antiderivative was needed --- that is the theorem doing the work.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$v(t) = 3t^2$ m/s. Displacement from $t = 1$ to $t = 4$.}

\textbf{Solution.} Displacement is the integral of velocity:
$\int_1^4 3t^2\,dt = \left[t^3\right]_1^4 = 64 - 1$.
\[ \boxed{63\ \text{m}} \]

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\int_1^3 \frac{1}{x}dx$. (a) Left sum, $n = 4$. (b) Over or under?}

\textbf{Solution (a).} $\Delta x = \tfrac{1}{2}$ and the left endpoints are
$1, \tfrac{3}{2}, 2, \tfrac{5}{2}$:
\[ \frac{1}{2}\left(1 + \frac{2}{3} + \frac{1}{2} + \frac{2}{5}\right)
   = \frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{77}{30}. \]
\[ \boxed{\frac{77}{60}} \]

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} $\dfrac{1}{x}$ is decreasing on
$[1,3]$, so on each subinterval the left endpoint is the highest point: every
rectangle pokes out above the curve and the sum overestimates. (The exact value
is $\ln 3 \approx 1.0986$, well below $\tfrac{77}{60} \approx 1.2833$.) Full
credit needs the link from ``decreasing'' to ``rectangles too tall'', not just
the word overestimate.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$H(x) = \int_1^{x^3}(2t+1)\,dt$. Find $H'(2)$.}

\textbf{Solution.} The upper limit is $x^3$, so the chain rule supplies $3x^2$:
\[ H'(x) = \left(2x^3 + 1\right)\cdot 3x^2 . \]
At $x = 2$: $\left(16 + 1\right)\cdot 12 = 17\cdot 12$.
\[ \boxed{H'(2) = 204} \]

\problem{Speed table at $t = 0, 2, 4, 6, 8$: $v = 0, 5, 9, 12, 14$.
(a) Trapezoidal estimate of $\int_0^8 v\,dt$. (b) Units and meaning.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} Each subinterval is $2$ wide, and each trapezoid has area
$\tfrac{1}{2}(\text{left} + \text{right})\cdot 2$:
\[ 2\left(\frac{0+5}{2} + \frac{5+9}{2} + \frac{9+12}{2} + \frac{12+14}{2}\right)
   = 2\left(2.5 + 7 + 10.5 + 13\right). \]
\[ \boxed{66} \]

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The rate is in metres per second
and the widths are in seconds, so the product is in metres; the estimate is the
distance the cart travelled during those eight seconds. Full credit needs both
the unit and the physical reading --- a unit with no interpretation, or an
interpretation with no unit, is half an answer.

\problem{Reservoir, $r(t) = 10 - 2t$ thousand litres per hour, $40$ thousand
litres at $t = 0$. (a) Net change. (b) Amount at $t = 8$. (c) Time of the
maximum.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} $\int_0^8 (10 - 2t)\,dt
= \left[10t - t^2\right]_0^8 = 80 - 64$.
\[ \boxed{16} \]
Thousand litres.

\textbf{Solution (b).} Starting amount plus net change: $40 + 16$.
\[ \boxed{56} \]

\textbf{Solution (c).} The amount grows while $r > 0$ and shrinks after, so the
peak is where $10 - 2t = 0$.
\[ \boxed{t = 5} \]
There the reservoir holds $40 + 25 = 65$ thousand litres, more than at $t = 8$.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$F(x) = \int_0^x f$, $f(t) = t^2 - 6t + 8$, on $[0, 6]$.
(a) Local extrema of $F$. (b) Which is the maximum?}

\textbf{Solution (a).} $F' = f$, and $f(t) = (t-2)(t-4)$ vanishes at
\[ \boxed{x = 2 \text{ or } x = 4} \]

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} $f$ is positive on $[0,2)$,
negative on $(2,4)$ and positive on $(4,6]$. Since $F' = f$, that makes $F$ rise,
fall, and rise again: $x = 2$ is a local maximum and $x = 4$ a local minimum.
Full credit requires the sign change of $f$ --- $F$ itself is never computed
here, which is the point of the problem.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$r(t) = 6\sqrt{t}$ L/min on $[0,9]$, $A(x) = \int_0^x 6\sqrt{t}\,dt$.
(a) $A(9)$. (b) $A'(4)$. (c) What each one answers.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} $\int 6\sqrt{t}\,dt = 4t^{3/2}$, so
$A(9) = 4\cdot 27 - 0$.
\[ \boxed{A(9) = 108\ \text{L}} \]

\textbf{Solution (b).} By the Fundamental Theorem $A'(x) = 6\sqrt{x}$, so
$A'(4) = 6\cdot 2$.
\[ \boxed{A'(4) = 12} \]

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} $A(9)$ is a \emph{total}: the
litres that arrived over the whole nine minutes, found by evaluating an
antiderivative at the two limits. $A'(4)$ is a \emph{rate}: the litres per
minute arriving at the single instant $t = 4$, which the Fundamental Theorem
hands back as the integrand itself. Full credit needs the total-versus-rate
distinction with the right unit on each.
