\problem{\textbf{12 stickers shared by 3 friends.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Name the three parts before writing anything. The total in all
is 12 and the number of groups is 3; what is unknown is how many are in each
group. That is the sharing shape of division.

\textbf{Step 2.} Write it as total $\div$ groups: $12 \div 3$, and count out
12 into 3 equal piles --- 4 in each pile.
\ans{4}

\textit{Check with multiplication:} $3 \times 4 = 12$, which is the same fact
read the other way.}

\problem{\textbf{The picture of 20 counters in 4 equal groups.}

\textbf{Step 1.} A drawing already shows two of the three parts: count the total
(20 counters) and count the boxes (4 groups). The unknown is the size of one
group.

\textbf{Step 2.} $20 \div 4$: each box holds the same number, and counting one
box gives 5.
\ans{5}

\textit{Matching multiplication:} $4 \times 5 = 20$ --- 4 groups of 5.}

\problem{\textbf{$3 \times \square = 18$ and its division partner.}

\textbf{Step 1.} A missing \emph{factor} is exactly what division finds, so
turn the multiplication around instead of guessing.

\textbf{Step 2.} The partner sentence is $18 \div 3 = \square$. Skip-count by
threes to 18: 3, 6, 9, 12, 15, 18 --- that is 6 threes.
\ans{6}

\textit{Fact family:} $3 \times 6 = 18$, $6 \times 3 = 18$, $18 \div 3 = 6$,
$18 \div 6 = 3$.}

\problem{\textbf{24 crayons packed 6 to a box.}

\textbf{Step 1.} This story gives the total (24) and the size of each group
(6 in a box). The unknown is the \emph{number} of groups, so it is the
measurement shape of division, not the sharing shape.

\textbf{Step 2.} $24 \div 6$: take 6 away at a time --- 24, 18, 12, 6, 0. That
is 4 boxes.
\ans{4}

\textit{Watch out:} a student who reads ``24 and 6'' without reading the story
may subtract and write 18. Subtraction takes 6 away \emph{once}; division takes
6 away until nothing is left.}

\problem{\textbf{Which story matches $15 \div 5 = \square$?}

\textbf{Step 1.} Read the equation out loud first: ``15 in all, split into 5
equal parts.'' Now look for the story that starts with 15 as the total.

\textbf{Step 2.} Story \textbf{A} is the match --- 15 shells split into 5 equal
piles. Story B makes 15 the size of each group, so its total would be much
larger than 15. Answering A: $15 \div 5 = 3$ shells in each pile.
\ans{3}

\textit{Language clue:} ``put into 5 equal piles'' names groups; ``15 in each''
names group size.}

\problem{\textbf{The picture of 18 counters in 3 equal groups.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Read the same two facts off the drawing: 18 counters in all,
3 boxes. The unknown is the size of one group.

\textbf{Step 2.} $18 \div 3$: each box holds the same amount, and one box holds
6.
\ans{6}

\textit{Matching multiplication:} $3 \times 6 = 18$. The same picture tells both
sentences --- multiplication puts the groups together, division breaks them
apart.}

\problem{\textbf{The fact family of 7, 4 and 28.}

\textbf{Step 1.} A missing factor again, so use the division partner rather than
guessing and checking.

\textbf{Step 2.} $28 \div 7$: count by sevens --- 7, 14, 21, 28. That is 4
sevens.
\ans{4}

\textit{All four sentences:} $7 \times 4 = 28$, $4 \times 7 = 28$,
$28 \div 7 = 4$, $28 \div 4 = 7$. Every one uses the same three numbers.}

\problem{\textbf{32 apples in baskets of 8.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Decide which number is the total before writing the equation.
``A farm stand has 32 apples'' is the whole amount; ``8 apples in each basket''
is the group size. The total is always the number you divide.

\textbf{Step 2.} $32 \div 8$: count by eights --- 8, 16, 24, 32. That is 4
baskets.
\ans{4}

\textit{Watch out:} multiplying gives 256, which is more apples than the stand
ever had. If an answer is bigger than the total, the operation was wrong.}

\problem{\textbf{Which story matches $42 \div 6 = \square$?}

\textbf{Step 1.} Say the equation in words: ``42 in all, put into groups of 6.''
The total must be 42.

\textbf{Step 2.} Story \textbf{A} matches --- 42 muffins with 6 on each tray.
Story B says 42 muffins are on \emph{each} of 6 trays, so 42 is a group size
there and the total would be far more than 42. Answering A: $42 \div 6 = 7$
trays.
\ans{7}

\textit{Why B fails:} B describes multiplication, $6 \times 42$, not division.}

\problem{\textbf{The array of 36 counters in 4 rows.}

\textbf{Step 1.} An array gives the total (36) and the number of rows (4). The
question asks for the size of a row, so divide the total by the number of rows.

\textbf{Step 2.} $36 \div 4$: count by fours to 36 --- 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28,
32, 36. That is 9, so each row holds 9 counters.
\ans{9}

\textit{The trap:} answering 4 would be reporting the number of \emph{rows},
which the picture already told you. Read the question, then decide which part of
the array is unknown.}

\problem{\textbf{$45 \div \square = 9$ --- the divisor is missing.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Here the total (45) and the answer (9) are known, so rewrite
the sentence as multiplication: $\square \times 9 = 45$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Count by nines --- 9, 18, 27, 36, 45 --- which takes 5 nines.
\ans{5}

\textit{A story it could tell:} 45 marbles are shared equally among 5 children,
and each child gets 9.}

\problem{\textbf{Challenge: 5 packs of 6 pencils shared by 3 tables.}

\textbf{Step 1.} You cannot divide yet, because the total is not given --- only
the packs are. Find the total first, then share it.

\textbf{Step 2.} Total pencils: $5 \times 6 = 30$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Now share the total among the tables: $30 \div 3 = 10$ pencils
for each table.
\ans{10}

\textit{The trap:} dividing $6 \div 3$ gives 2 and only shares \emph{one} pack.
A picture of 3 groups with 10 pencils in each shows the whole 30.}
