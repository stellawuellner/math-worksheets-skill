\sstitleblock{Factoring Special Forms: Squares}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Difference of squares:} $A^2 - B^2 = (A - B)(A + B)$ --- two terms,
subtraction, both perfect squares.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Perfect-square trinomial:} $A^2 + 2AB + B^2 = (A + B)^2$ and
$A^2 - 2AB + B^2 = (A - B)^2$ --- three terms, ends both squares, middle equal
to twice the product of their roots.\\[3pt]
\textbf{No pattern for a sum of squares:} $A^2 + B^2$ does not factor over the
integers. Recognising that saves time on every ``factor completely''.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Squares worth knowing on sight:} $4, 9, 16, 25, 36, 49, 64, 81, 100,
121, 144$; and $4x^2 = (2x)^2$, $9x^2 = (3x)^2$, $x^4 = (x^2)^2$.
\end{formulabox}

\skillheading{1. Difference of two squares}

\begin{formulabox}
Two terms and a minus sign is the trigger. Name $A$ and $B$ as the square roots
of the two terms, then write $(A - B)(A + B)$. The check is quick: expanding
always cancels the middle terms.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Factor $x^2 - 36$.
\step{Two terms, subtraction, and both terms are perfect squares --- that is
exactly the difference-of-squares trigger, so no trial and error is needed.}
\step{$A = x$ because $x^2 = (x)^2$, and $B = 6$ because $36 = (6)^2$.}
$x^2 - 36 = \ans{(x-6)(x+6)}$
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Factor $x^2 - 121$.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{(x-11)(x+11)}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{2. Perfect-square trinomial}

\begin{formulabox}
Three terms with square ends. Take the roots of the first and last terms, then
\emph{test} the middle: it must equal $2AB$. If it does not, the trinomial is
not a perfect square and needs ordinary factoring instead. The sign of the
middle term becomes the sign inside the bracket.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Factor $x^2 + 6x + 9$.
\step{Three terms whose first and last are squares --- test the perfect-square
pattern before trying factor pairs.}
\step{$A = x$, $B = 3$, and the test is $2AB = 2 \cdot x \cdot 3 = 6x$, which
matches the middle term. The middle sign is positive, so the bracket holds a
sum.}
$x^2 + 6x + 9 = \ans{(x+3)^2}$
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Factor $x^2 - 8x + 16$.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{(x-4)^2}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{3. Common factor first, then the pattern}

\begin{formulabox}
Before testing either pattern, ask whether every term shares a factor. A
coefficient that is not a perfect square ($2x^2$, $3x^2$, $18x^2$) is the usual
sign that a common factor is hiding the pattern. Take it out, factor what is
left, and keep the common factor in the final answer.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Factor $3x^2 - 27$ completely.
\step{$3x^2$ is not a perfect square, so the difference-of-squares pattern
cannot apply yet --- but both terms are divisible by $3$, so remove that first.}
\step{$3x^2 - 27 = 3(x^2 - 9)$, and $x^2 - 9 = (x - 3)(x + 3)$.}
$3x^2 - 27 = \ans{3(x-3)(x+3)}$
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Factor $4x^2 - 100$ completely.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{4(x-5)(x+5)}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} $x^2 - 9$ is \emph{not} $(x - 3)^2$. Squaring a binomial
always creates a middle term, so $(x - 3)^2 = x^2 - 6x + 9$. Count the terms
first: two terms means difference of squares, three terms means look for a
perfect square. And ``factor completely'' means keep going --- $x^4 - 81$
splits twice.
\end{watchoutbox}
