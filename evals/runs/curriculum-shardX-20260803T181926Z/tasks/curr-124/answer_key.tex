\problem{\textbf{Compare $\dfrac{3}{8}$ and $\dfrac{5}{8}$.}

Step 1. Check what matches. Both bottoms are $8$, so both fractions are built
from eighths --- pieces of exactly the same size.

Step 2. When the pieces are the same size, counting them is a fair comparison:
three eighths against five eighths.

Step 3. Five pieces beat three pieces, so
$\ans{\dfrac{3}{8} < \dfrac{5}{8}}$

Step 4. Why the sentence matters: this is the ONE case where looking at the top
numbers alone is safe, and knowing why keeps it from being used everywhere else.}

\problem{\textbf{Compare $\dfrac{3}{5}$ and $\dfrac{3}{8}$.}

Step 1. Check what matches. This time the TOPS match: three pieces in each
fraction. The bottoms differ, so the pieces are different sizes.

Step 2. Cutting a whole into $8$ pieces makes smaller pieces than cutting it
into $5$. So a fifth is bigger than an eighth.

Step 3. Three of the bigger pieces beat three of the smaller ones:
$\ans{\dfrac{3}{5} > \dfrac{3}{8}}$

Step 4. This is the pattern that trips students up: with the same top number,
the fraction with the BIGGER bottom number is the SMALLER fraction.}

\problem{\textbf{Ben: $\dfrac{2}{9} > \dfrac{2}{4}$ because ``nine is bigger
than four''.}

Step 1. Name the error. Ben compared the bottom numbers as if they were amounts.
A bottom number is not an amount --- it counts how many pieces the whole was cut
into.

Step 2. More pieces means each piece is smaller, so a ninth is smaller than a
quarter.

Step 3. Both fractions hold two pieces, so the one made of smaller pieces is
less: $\ans{\dfrac{2}{9} < \dfrac{2}{4}}$

Step 4. A picture settles it for a doubtful student: two slices of a pizza cut
into nine is clearly less food than two slices of the same pizza cut into four.}

\problem{\textbf{Cara: $\dfrac{3}{5}$ rewritten as $\dfrac{3}{40}$.}

Step 1. Find what was done to the bottom: $5$ became $40$, and
$40 \div 5 = 8$, so the bottom was multiplied by $8$.

Step 2. An equivalent fraction needs the SAME multiplication on top. Cara did it
to only one number, which does not make an equal fraction --- it makes a
completely different one.

Step 3. Multiply the top by $8$ as well: $3 \times 8 = 24$, so the correct top
number is $\ans{24}$ and $\dfrac{3}{5} = \dfrac{24}{40}$.

Step 4. Check the size: $\dfrac{3}{5}$ is a little more than half, and
$\dfrac{24}{40}$ is a little more than half too. Cara's $\dfrac{3}{40}$ is
nowhere near half, which is the quickest way to catch this error.}

\problem{\textbf{Which is greater, $\dfrac{3}{4}$ or $\dfrac{5}{7}$? Rewrite
both with $28$ on the bottom.}

Step 1. Neither the tops nor the bottoms match, so no shortcut applies. Find a
bottom number both can be rewritten with: $4 \times 7 = 28$ works.

Step 2. Rewrite the first fraction. The bottom went from $4$ to $28$, a
multiplication by $7$, so the top does the same:
$3 \times 7 = 21$, giving $\dfrac{21}{28}$. The top number is $\ans{21}$

Step 3. Rewrite the second. Its bottom went from $7$ to $28$, a multiplication
by $4$, so $5 \times 4 = 20$, giving $\dfrac{20}{28}$.

Step 4. Now the pieces are the same size, so count them: $21$ beats $20$, and
$\ans{\dfrac{3}{4} > \dfrac{5}{7}}$

Step 5. Notice what the shortcut would have said: $5$ is bigger than $3$, so a
top-numbers-only reader picks the wrong fraction --- by a single
twenty-eighth.}

\problem{\textbf{Write $\dfrac{6}{8}$ in simplest form.}

Step 1. Look for a number that divides both the top and the bottom. Both $6$ and
$8$ are even, so $2$ works.

Step 2. Divide both by the same number: $6 \div 2 = 3$ and $8 \div 2 = 4$.

Step 3. $\ans{\dfrac{3}{4}}$ --- and nothing divides $3$ and $4$ together except
$1$, so this is as simple as it gets.

Step 4. Same rule as problem 4, run backwards: equivalent fractions come from
doing the same thing to BOTH numbers, whether that is multiplying or dividing.}

\problem{\textbf{Order $\dfrac{2}{3}$, $\dfrac{3}{5}$, $\dfrac{7}{10}$ from
smallest to largest.}

Step 1. The bottoms are $3$, $5$ and $10$, and all three divide into $30$, so
rewrite everything in thirtieths.

Step 2. $\dfrac{2}{3} = \dfrac{20}{30}$ (top and bottom $\times 10$);
$\dfrac{3}{5} = \dfrac{18}{30}$ ($\times 6$);
$\dfrac{7}{10} = \dfrac{21}{30}$ ($\times 3$).

Step 3. Same-sized pieces at last, so order the tops: $18$, then $20$, then
$21$.

Step 4. Written as the original fractions:
$\ans{\dfrac{3}{5} < \dfrac{2}{3} < \dfrac{7}{10}}$

Step 5. A top-numbers-only reader would answer $2/3$, $3/5$, $7/10$ --- wrong at
the very first step --- and a bottom-numbers-only reader would answer
$7/10$, $3/5$, $2/3$, which is exactly backwards.}

\problem{\textbf{(a) Why ``just look at the top numbers'' fails. (b) Compare
$\dfrac{5}{6}$ and $\dfrac{7}{9}$ over $18$.}

\textbf{(a)} \emph{Open response --- flagged for manual review. Model answer
below; accept any equivalent reasoning with a working example.}\\
The top number only counts pieces; it says nothing about how big a piece is. The
bottom number sets the size, and more pieces means smaller ones. So two
fractions can only be compared by their tops when their bottoms already match.
Example: $\dfrac{2}{9}$ and $\dfrac{2}{4}$ have the same top, so the shortcut
calls them equal, but $\dfrac{2}{4}$ is a half and $\dfrac{2}{9}$ is much less.
Any example with a matching or larger top over a much larger bottom works.\\
\emph{What a full-credit answer must contain:} the idea that the bottom number
sets the size of the pieces, and one example where the shortcut gives the wrong
answer.

\textbf{(b)} Step 1. Neither number matches, and both $6$ and $9$ divide into
$18$, so rewrite both fractions in eighteenths.

Step 2. The first bottom goes from $6$ to $18$, a multiplication by $3$, so the
top does the same: $5 \times 3 = 15$. The top number is $\ans{15}$

Step 3. The second bottom goes from $9$ to $18$, a multiplication by $2$, so
$7 \times 2 = 14$, giving $\dfrac{14}{18}$.

Step 4. Fifteen eighteenths beats fourteen eighteenths, so
$\ans{\dfrac{5}{6} > \dfrac{7}{9}}$

Step 5. The shortcut fails here in both directions: the tops say $7 > 5$ and the
bottoms say $9 > 6$, and each ``reason'' points at the smaller fraction.}
