\problem{\textbf{Complete the factor pair $4 \times \underline{\hspace{1cm}}
= 24$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} A factor pair is two numbers that multiply to the target, so
the question is really ``four groups of what make $24$?''

\textbf{Step 2.} Count by fours: $4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24$ --- six fours, or
$24 \div 4$.

\ans{6}

\textit{Teaching note:} the full pair list for $24$ is
$1 \times 24$, $2 \times 12$, $3 \times 8$, $4 \times 6$ --- worth writing out
once, because it is what makes $24$ composite.}

\problem{\textbf{Is $342$ divisible by $3$?}

\textbf{Step 1.} Long division would answer it, but the digit-sum rule is
faster: a number is divisible by $3$ exactly when the sum of its digits is.

\textbf{Step 2.} $3 + 4 + 2 = 9$.

\ans{9}

\textbf{Step 3.} $9$ is divisible by $3$ (it is $3 \times 3$), so $342$ is
divisible by $3$ as well. \emph{Yes.}

\textit{Check if you like:} $342 \div 3 = 114$ $\checkmark$}

\problem{\textbf{Ari says $51$ is prime. Show that it is composite.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Prime means \emph{exactly two} factors, $1$ and itself. So one
factor pair other than $1 \times 51$ settles the question.

\textbf{Step 2.} The digits of $51$ add to $5 + 1 = 6$, which is divisible by
$3$, so $3$ is a factor. Then $51 \div 3$ gives the partner.

\ans{17}

\textbf{Step 3.} $51 = 3 \times 17$, so $51$ has four factors ($1, 3, 17, 51$)
and is composite.

\textit{Why Ari was fooled:} odd numbers are simply numbers not divisible by
$2$. Every prime above $2$ is odd, but plenty of odd numbers are not prime.}

\problem{\textbf{Pattern A (adds $4$) against pattern B (adds $6$): 5th
numbers.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Each pattern is a skip-count, so its $5$th number is five
steps of its own size --- no need to write out either list in full.

\textbf{Step 2.} Pattern A: $4 \times 5 = 20$. Pattern B: $6 \times 5 = 30$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $20$ is less than $30$.

\ans{<}

\textit{Teaching note:} the same reasoning says pattern B's $n$th number is
always larger, because it takes bigger steps from a bigger start.}

\problem{\textbf{First common multiple of $6$ and $8$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Skip-count both lists and watch for the first number that
appears in both --- that is what ``common multiple'' means.

\textbf{Step 2.} Sixes: $6, 12, 18, 24$. Eights: $8, 16, 24$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $24$ appears in both, and nothing smaller does. It is the
fourth multiple of $6$ and the third multiple of $8$.

\ans{6 \times 4 = 24}

\ans{8 \times 3 = 24}

\textit{Listen for:} $48$ --- a common multiple, but not the first one.}

\problem{\textbf{Is $63$ a multiple of $5$?}

\textbf{Step 1.} Multiples of $5$ end in $0$ or $5$, and $63$ ends in $3$, so
the answer is almost certainly no --- the skip-count shows why.

\textbf{Step 2.} Counting by fives passes $60$ ($5 \times 12$) and then jumps
to $65$. So $5 \times 12 + 3 = 63$.

\ans{63}

\textbf{Step 3.} $63$ sits $3$ past a multiple of $5$ and $2$ short of the
next one, so it is \emph{not} a multiple of $5$. \emph{No.}

\textit{Teaching note:} the leftover $3$ is the remainder --- the same $3$
appears in $63 \div 5 = 12$ remainder $3$.}

\problem{\textbf{Write $60$ as a product of primes.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Split $60$ into any factor pair, then keep splitting any
factor that is still composite until only primes are left.

\textbf{Step 2.} $60 = 6 \times 10$; then $6 = 2 \times 3$ and
$10 = 2 \times 5$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Collect the leaves in order: $2, 2, 3, 5$.

\ans{2 \times 2 \times 3 \times 5 = 60}

\textit{Worth saying:} starting with $60 = 4 \times 15$ or $60 = 5 \times 12$
gives exactly the same four primes. That is not luck --- every number has one
prime recipe.}

\problem{\textbf{Pattern $3, 7, 11, 15, \ldots$ --- the $8$th number.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Find the step size first: $7 - 3 = 4$, and the same jump takes
$11$ to $15$, so the pattern adds $4$ each time.

\textbf{Step 2.} Getting from the $1$st number to the $8$th takes $7$ jumps,
not $8$ --- the first number is already there.

\textbf{Step 3.} $3 + 7 \times 4 = 3 + 28 = 31$.

\ans{31}

\textit{Listen for:} $35$, from taking eight jumps instead of seven.}

\problem{\textbf{Riddle: in the thirties, divisible by $3$ and by $4$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} A number divisible by both $3$ and $4$ must be a multiple of
$3 \times 4 = 12$, so only the multiples of $12$ need checking.

\textbf{Step 2.} Multiples of $12$: $12, 24, 36, 48$. The only one in the
thirties is $36$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Check it: $36 = 3 \times 12$ and $36 = 4 \times 9$, so both
divisions come out whole.

\ans{36}

\textit{Why nothing else works:} $30$ and $33$ and $39$ are divisible by $3$
but not by $4$; $32$ is divisible by $4$ but not by $3$.}

\problem{\textbf{Bells every $8$ minutes and every $12$ minutes.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Each bell rings at its own multiples, so they meet again at
the first common multiple of $8$ and $12$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Eights: $8, 16, 24$. Twelves: $12, 24$. They meet at $24$.

\ans{24 \text{ minutes}}

\textbf{Step 3.} Check both: $8 \times 3 = 24$ and $12 \times 2 = 24$, so both
bells really do ring at minute $24$.

\textit{Listen for:} $96$, from multiplying $8 \times 12$. That is \emph{a}
common multiple, but the bells meet sooner.}

\problem{\textbf{Ravi says $91$ is prime.}

\textbf{Step 1.} To disprove a prime claim, one factor pair is enough, so test
the small primes in turn: $2$ (no, odd), $3$ (digits add to $10$, no), $5$ (no,
ends in $1$), then $7$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $91 \div 7 = 13$, so $7$ is a factor and its partner is $13$.

\ans{13}

\textbf{Step 3.} $91 = 7 \times 13$, so $91$ is composite.

\textit{Model answer for (b):} being odd only means $2$ is not a factor --- it
says nothing about $3, 5, 7, 11, \ldots$ You have to keep testing primes until
the test prime multiplied by itself passes the number: $7 \times 7 = 49$ and
$11 \times 11 = 121$, so for $91$ the testing stops after $7$ works (and would
have stopped at $11$ if it had not).

\textit{Manual review:} part (b) is the written half.}

\problem{\textbf{Challenge: exactly three factors, the middle one is $7$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The three factors are $1$, $7$, and the number itself. Factors
come in pairs that multiply to the number, and $7$ must pair with something.

\textbf{Step 2.} If $7$ paired with a different number, that number would be a
fourth factor. So $7$ has to pair with itself: the number is
$7 \times 7$.

\ans{49}

\textbf{Step 3.} Check: the factors of $49$ are $1, 7, 49$ --- exactly three.

\textit{Model answer for (b):} every factor pairs with a partner, so factors
usually come two at a time and the count is even. The count is odd only when
one factor is its own partner, i.e. the number is a square. And if that square
root had any factor other than $1$ and itself, the number would pick up extra
factors --- so the root must be prime. Exactly three factors means
prime $\times$ the same prime.

\textit{Manual review:} part (b) is the written half; $4 = 2 \times 2$ and
$25 = 5 \times 5$ are good examples to check the reasoning against.}
