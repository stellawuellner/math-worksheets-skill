\problem{$\int 2x(x^2+7)^5dx$, table supplied.}

\textbf{Solution.} With $u = x^2 + 7$ and $du = 2x\,dx$ the integral becomes
$\int u^5\,du = \dfrac{u^6}{6} + C$. Converting back to $x$:
\[ \boxed{\frac{\left(x^2 + 7\right)^6}{6} + C} \]
Check by differentiating: $6\cdot\dfrac{(x^2+7)^5}{6}\cdot 2x$ returns the
integrand, which is what substitution promises.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\int x^2 e^{x^3}dx$.}

\textbf{Solution.} $u = x^3$, $du = 3x^2\,dx$, so $x^2\,dx = \tfrac{1}{3}du$:
\[ \frac{1}{3}\int e^u\,du = \frac{1}{3}e^u + C. \]
\[ \boxed{\frac{e^{x^3}}{3} + C} \]

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\int_0^1 2x(x^2+1)^3dx$, $u = x^2+1$. (a) Lower $u$-limit.
(b) Upper $u$-limit. (c) Value.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} $u = 0^2 + 1$.
\[ \boxed{u = 1} \]

\textbf{Solution (b).} $u = 1^2 + 1$.
\[ \boxed{u = 2} \]

\textbf{Solution (c).} With $du = 2x\,dx$ the whole integral converts:
\[ \int_1^{2} u^3\,du = \left[\frac{u^4}{4}\right]_1^{2} = \frac{16 - 1}{4}. \]
\[ \boxed{\frac{15}{4}} \]
Note the limits moved with the variable --- nothing is converted back to $x$.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\int \dfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+9}}dx$.}

\textbf{Solution.} $u = x^2 + 9$, $du = 2x\,dx$, so $x\,dx = \tfrac{1}{2}du$:
\[ \frac{1}{2}\int u^{-1/2}du = \frac{1}{2}\cdot 2u^{1/2} + C = \sqrt{u} + C. \]
\[ \boxed{\sqrt{x^2 + 9} + C} \]

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\int_0^{\pi/4}\tan x\sec^2x\,dx$, $u = \tan x$. (a) Upper $u$-limit.
(b) Value.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} $\tan\dfrac{\pi}{4}$.
\[ \boxed{u = 1} \]

\textbf{Solution (b).} $du = \sec^2 x\,dx$, and the lower limit becomes
$\tan 0 = 0$:
\[ \int_0^{1} u\,du = \left[\frac{u^2}{2}\right]_0^{1}. \]
\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{2}} \]

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\int \dfrac{(\ln x)^3}{x}dx$. (a) Evaluate. (b) Why the $1/x$ matters.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} $u = \ln x$, $du = \dfrac{dx}{x}$:
\[ \int u^3\,du = \frac{u^4}{4} + C. \]
\[ \boxed{\frac{\left(\ln x\right)^4}{4} + C} \]

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The substitution $u = \ln x$ forces
$du = \dfrac{dx}{x}$, and the integrand already carries that $\dfrac{1}{x}$, so
the differential is consumed exactly. Without it, an unconverted $x$ would be
left over that cannot be written in terms of $u$, and the substitution would
stall. Full credit needs the $du = dx/x$ step, not just the remark that it works.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\int_1^{e}\dfrac{\ln x}{x}dx$, $u = \ln x$. (a) Upper $u$-limit.
(b) Value.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} $\ln e$.
\[ \boxed{u = 1} \]

\textbf{Solution (b).} The lower limit becomes $\ln 1 = 0$, and
$du = \dfrac{dx}{x}$:
\[ \int_0^{1} u\,du = \left[\frac{u^2}{2}\right]_0^{1}. \]
\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{2}} \]

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\int_0^{b}xe^{-x^2}dx$, $u = x^2$. (a) $b = 2$. (b) The limit.
(c) What it means.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} $du = 2x\,dx$, so $x\,dx = \tfrac{1}{2}du$, and $u$ runs
from $0$ to $b^2$:
\[ \frac{1}{2}\int_0^{b^2}e^{-u}du = \frac{1}{2}\left(1 - e^{-b^2}\right). \]
At $b = 2$ this is
\[ \boxed{\frac{1 - e^{-4}}{2}} \]

\textbf{Solution (b).} As $b \rightarrow \infty$, $e^{-b^2} \rightarrow 0$:
\[ \boxed{\frac{1}{2}} \]

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} The region under
$y = xe^{-x^2}$ stretches forever to the right, yet its total area settles on
$\tfrac{1}{2}$: the tail contributes less and less because $e^{-b^2}$ collapses
to zero. The improper integral therefore \emph{converges}, and its value is one
half. Full credit needs the word converges or an equivalent statement that the
area approaches a finite value rather than growing without bound.
