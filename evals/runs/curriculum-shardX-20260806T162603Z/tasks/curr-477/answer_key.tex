\problem{$\int x e^x dx$.}

\textbf{Solution.} $u = x$, $dv = e^x dx$, so $du = dx$ and $v = e^x$:
\[ \int x e^x dx = x e^x - \int e^x dx . \]
\[ \boxed{x e^{x} - e^{x} + C} \]
Differentiating $xe^x - e^x$ gives $e^x + xe^x - e^x = xe^x$, as it must.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\int \ln x\,dx$.}

\textbf{Solution.} There is only one factor, so take $u = \ln x$ and
$dv = dx$; then $du = \dfrac{dx}{x}$ and $v = x$:
\[ \int \ln x\,dx = x\ln x - \int x\cdot\frac{1}{x}\,dx = x\ln x - \int dx . \]
\[ \boxed{x\ln x - x + C} \]

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\int x\sin x\,dx$.}

\textbf{Solution.} $u = x$, $dv = \sin x\,dx$, so $v = -\cos x$:
\[ \int x\sin x\,dx = -x\cos x + \int \cos x\,dx . \]
\[ \boxed{\sin x - x\cos x + C} \]

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\int x\ln x\,dx$.}

\textbf{Solution.} The logarithm is the factor that improves when
differentiated, so $u = \ln x$ and $dv = x\,dx$, giving
$v = \dfrac{x^2}{2}$:
\[ \int x\ln x\,dx = \frac{x^2\ln x}{2} - \int \frac{x^2}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{x}\,dx
   = \frac{x^2\ln x}{2} - \frac{1}{2}\int x\,dx . \]
\[ \boxed{\frac{x^2\ln x}{2} - \frac{x^2}{4} + C} \]

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\int_0^1 x e^{2x}dx$.}

\textbf{Solution.} $u = x$, $dv = e^{2x}dx$, so $v = \dfrac{e^{2x}}{2}$:
\[ \left[\frac{x e^{2x}}{2}\right]_0^1 - \frac{1}{2}\int_0^1 e^{2x}dx
   = \frac{e^{2}}{2} - \left[\frac{e^{2x}}{4}\right]_0^1
   = \frac{e^{2}}{2} - \frac{e^{2}}{4} + \frac{1}{4}. \]
\[ \boxed{\frac{e^{2} + 1}{4}} \]

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\int x^2 e^x dx$. (a) Evaluate. (b) Why twice?}

\textbf{Solution (a).} First pass: $u = x^2$, $dv = e^x dx$, giving
$x^2e^x - 2\int xe^x dx$. The remaining integral is problem 1, so
\[ \int x^2 e^x dx = x^2e^x - 2\left(xe^x - e^x\right). \]
\[ \boxed{x^{2}e^{x} - 2xe^{x} + 2e^{x} + C} \]

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} Each application of parts
differentiates the polynomial factor once, lowering its degree by one:
$x^2 \rightarrow 2x \rightarrow 2$. After the first pass the leftover integral
still contains an $x$, so it is the same shape one power down; after the second
pass the polynomial is a constant and the integral is elementary. Full credit
needs that degree-lowering idea, not just ``the first pass didn't finish''.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\int \arctan x\,dx$.}

\textbf{Solution.} $u = \arctan x$, $dv = dx$, so
$du = \dfrac{dx}{1 + x^2}$ and $v = x$:
\[ \int \arctan x\,dx = x\arctan x - \int\frac{x}{1 + x^2}\,dx , \]
and the leftover integral is a substitution, $w = 1 + x^2$, giving
$\tfrac{1}{2}\ln\!\left(1 + x^2\right)$.
\[ \boxed{x\arctan x - \frac{\ln\!\left(1 + x^{2}\right)}{2} + C} \]

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\int e^x \sin x\,dx$.}

\textbf{Solution.} Call the integral $I$. With $u = \sin x$, $dv = e^x dx$:
$I = e^x\sin x - \int e^x\cos x\,dx$. Applying parts again to the new integral
(same choice of $dv$) gives $\int e^x\cos x\,dx = e^x\cos x + I$, so
\[ I = e^x\sin x - e^x\cos x - I \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 2I = e^x(\sin x - \cos x). \]
\[ \boxed{\frac{e^{x}\left(\sin x - \cos x\right)}{2} + C} \]
The integral came back unchanged, so it could be solved for algebraically ---
that is the whole trick here.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\int_0^{\pi} x\cos x\,dx$.}

\textbf{Solution.} $u = x$, $dv = \cos x\,dx$, so $v = \sin x$:
\[ \left[x\sin x\right]_0^{\pi} - \int_0^{\pi}\sin x\,dx
   = 0 + \left[\cos x\right]_0^{\pi} = -1 - 1 . \]
\[ \boxed{-2} \]

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$\int_0^b x e^{-x}dx$. (a) $b = 3$. (b) The limit. (c) The strategic
choice.}

\textbf{Solution (a).} $u = x$, $dv = e^{-x}dx$, so $v = -e^{-x}$:
\[ \left[-xe^{-x}\right]_0^{b} + \int_0^{b}e^{-x}dx
   = -be^{-b} + \left[-e^{-x}\right]_0^{b} = 1 - (b+1)e^{-b}. \]
At $b = 3$ this is
\[ \boxed{1 - 4e^{-3}} \]

\textbf{Solution (b).} As $b \rightarrow \infty$ the exponential beats the
linear factor, so $(b+1)e^{-b} \rightarrow 0$:
\[ \boxed{1} \]

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} Differentiating $x$ removes it
completely after one pass, while $e^{-x}$ integrates back to itself (up to a
sign) and never becomes harder. Swapping the roles would differentiate the
exponential --- which stays exponential --- and integrate $x$ into
$\tfrac{x^2}{2}$, leaving an integral worse than the original. Full credit needs
the reason, not just the correct assignment.
