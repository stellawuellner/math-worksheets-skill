\noindent\small\textbf{What this sheet is teaching.} A rigid motion is defined by what it
\emph{preserves}, not by what it looks like: distance survives, and therefore so do angle
measure and congruence. The sheet keeps the algebra and the picture side by side on every
problem so the student can catch a slip in either one. The distance-formula checks on
problems 4, 5 and 6 are not busywork --- they are the evidence for the congruence claim the
student is asked to make in problem 8 without measuring anything.\normalsize

\problem{\textbf{Translate a point.} $P(-4, 1)$ under $(x, y) \rightarrow (x + 6,\, y - 3)$.

\par\smallskip
Apply the rule coordinate by coordinate:
\par
$x' = -4 + 6 = \ans{2}$, \qquad $y' = 1 - 3 = \ans{-2}$.
\par
So $P'(2, -2)$: six units right and three units down from $P$.
\par
\textbf{Check on the grid.} Counting from $P$, six squares right and three squares down
lands in the fourth quadrant, which is where the arithmetic put it. A student whose plotted
point disagrees with their algebra has almost always counted the negative direction the
wrong way.}

\problem{\textbf{Reflect a point over the $y$-axis.} $Q(6, -2)$.

\par\smallskip
The $y$-axis is a vertical mirror, so it can only move a point left or right: the height
above or below the $x$-axis is untouched. The rule is
$(x, y) \rightarrow (-x,\, y)$.
\par
$x' = -6$, so $x' = \ans{-6}$; \qquad $y' = \ans{-2}$, unchanged.
\par
So $Q'(-6, -2)$.
\par
\textbf{The distance check.} $Q$ is $6$ units right of the $y$-axis and $Q'$ is $6$ units
left of it. That equal-distance-on-opposite-sides property \emph{is} the definition of a
reflection; the coordinate rule is just its shorthand.
\par
\textbf{Common slip:} negating both coordinates. That is a $180^\circ$ rotation, not a
reflection, and it is the same confusion problem 7 is built around.}

\problem{\textbf{Rotate $R(2, 7)$ by $90^\circ$ counterclockwise about the origin.}

\par\smallskip
The rule is $(x, y) \rightarrow (-y,\, x)$. Swap the coordinates, then negate the new
first one:
\par
$x' = -7$, so $x' = \ans{-7}$; \qquad $y' = \ans{2}$.
\par
So $R'(-7, 2)$.
\par
\textbf{Two checks the student can run alone.} The spoke $OR$ and the spoke $OR'$ must be
the same length: $\sqrt{2^2 + 7^2} = \sqrt{53}$ both times, because swapping and negating
does not change the sum of the squares. And they must be perpendicular --- the slopes are
$7/2$ and $-2/7$, whose product is $-1$.
\par
\textbf{Direction matters.} A clockwise quarter turn is $(x, y) \rightarrow (y,\, -x)$ and
would give $(7, -2)$. If a student produces that, they have the mechanism right and the
direction wrong; ask which way the dashed spoke has to sweep.}

\problem{\textbf{Translate the segment $A(-5, 2)$, $B(-1, 5)$ by
$(x, y) \rightarrow (x + 4,\, y - 6)$.}

\par\smallskip
\textbf{The image points.}
\par
$A' = (-5 + 4,\ 2 - 6) = (\ans{-1}, \ans{-4})$, \qquad
$B' = (-1 + 4,\ 5 - 6) = (\ans{3}, \ans{-1})$.
\par
\textbf{The length check.} Using the distance formula on the image:
\[ A'B' = \sqrt{(3 - (-1))^2 + (-1 - (-4))^2} = \sqrt{4^2 + 3^2} = \sqrt{25} = \ans{5}. \]
And on the pre-image: $AB = \sqrt{(-1-(-5))^2 + (5-2)^2} = \sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$. The two
agree, as they must.
\par
\textbf{Why it had to work.} Both endpoints moved by the same vector, so the difference
between them --- which is all the distance formula sees --- did not change at all. This is
the cleanest of the three rigid motions to argue: the horizontal gap of $4$ and the
vertical gap of $3$ are literally the same numbers before and after.}

\problem{\textbf{Reflect the segment $C(1, 4)$, $D(5, 7)$ over the line $y = x$.}

\par\smallskip
\textbf{The image points.} The rule $(x, y) \rightarrow (y,\, x)$ swaps the coordinates:
\par
$C' = (\ans{4}, \ans{1})$, \qquad $D' = (\ans{7}, \ans{5})$.
\par
\textbf{The length check.}
\[ C'D' = \sqrt{(7 - 4)^2 + (5 - 1)^2} = \sqrt{9 + 16} = \sqrt{25} = \ans{5}, \]
and $CD = \sqrt{(5-1)^2 + (7-4)^2} = \sqrt{16 + 9} = 5$. Equal.
\par
\textbf{Look at what the swap did to the formula.} The horizontal gap and the vertical gap
traded places --- $4$ and $3$ became $3$ and $4$ --- and the distance formula squares both
and adds, so the order cannot matter. That is a proof, not a coincidence, and it works for
any segment reflected in $y = x$.
\par
\textbf{The picture check.} The mirror line should be the perpendicular bisector of
$\overline{CC'}$. Its midpoint is $(2.5, 2.5)$, which lies on $y = x$, and the segment from
$(1,4)$ to $(4,1)$ has slope $-1$, perpendicular to the mirror's slope of $1$.}

\problem{\textbf{Rotate the segment $E(-3, 5)$, $F(-3, 1)$ by $180^\circ$ about the
origin.}

\par\smallskip
\textbf{The image points.} A half turn negates both coordinates,
$(x, y) \rightarrow (-x,\, -y)$:
\par
$E' = (\ans{3}, \ans{-5})$, \qquad $F' = (3, \ans{-1})$.
\par
\textbf{The length check.} Both image points share the $x$-coordinate $3$, so the segment
is vertical and the length is just the gap in $y$:
\[ E'F' = \sqrt{(3-3)^2 + (-1 - (-5))^2} = \sqrt{0 + 16} = \ans{4}. \]
The pre-image $\overline{EF}$ is also vertical, from $y = 1$ to $y = 5$, so $EF = 4$.
\par
\textbf{The quadrant answer.} $\overline{EF}$ sits in the second quadrant and its image
sits in the fourth --- diagonally opposite, which is what a half turn about the origin
always does. Note that the segment stayed vertical: a $180^\circ$ rotation preserves every
direction up to reversal, which is why it is the one rotation students can do in their
heads.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix the mistake.} Reflect $G(-6, 3)$ over the $y$-axis; Sam
wrote $G'(-6, -3)$.

\par\smallskip
\textbf{What Sam did.} Sam left the $x$-coordinate alone and negated the $y$-coordinate.
That is the rule $(x, y) \rightarrow (x,\, -y)$ --- a reflection over the \textbf{$x$-axis},
not the $y$-axis.
\par
\textbf{The correct image.} Reflecting over the $y$-axis uses
$(x, y) \rightarrow (-x,\, y)$:
\par
$x' = -(-6) = \ans{6}$, \qquad $y' = \ans{3}$ (unchanged).
\par
So $G'(6, 3)$.
\par
\textbf{How to remember which is which.} Name the mirror and ask which way it points. The
$y$-axis is vertical, so it flips a point \emph{sideways}, and sideways is the
$x$-coordinate. The $x$-axis is horizontal, so it flips a point \emph{up and down}, which
is the $y$-coordinate. The axis you reflect over is the one coordinate that does
\emph{not} change --- students who memorise a pair of rules with no picture attached
reliably swap them under time pressure.
\par
\textbf{On the grid.} $G$ sits in the second quadrant. Sam's answer stays on the left, in
the third quadrant; the correct image crosses to the right, into the first. A quick plot
would have caught it.}

\problem{\textbf{Name the motion, then justify the congruence.} (Open description ---
read the reasoning, not just the name.)

\par\smallskip
\textbf{(a) The motion.} It is a $90^\circ$ \textbf{counterclockwise rotation about the
origin}, coordinate rule $(x, y) \rightarrow (-y,\, x)$. Describing it as a $270^\circ$
clockwise rotation about the origin is the same motion and equally correct.
\par
\textbf{(b) Checking every vertex.}
\begin{itemize}[label={$\bullet$}, leftmargin=1.1cm, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item $J(2, 1) \rightarrow (-1, 2) = J'$
  \item $K(6, 1) \rightarrow (-1, 6) = K'$
  \item $L(6, 4) \rightarrow (-4, 6) = L'$
\end{itemize}
All three land on the marked image points, so one rule accounts for the whole triangle ---
which is what ``a single rigid motion'' means.
\par
\textbf{(c) The congruence argument.} A rotation is a rigid motion, and a rigid motion
preserves distance by definition. So $JK = J'K'$, $KL = K'L'$ and $LJ = L'J'$ without
computing any of them, and the triangles are congruent by SSS. The point of the question
is that congruence follows from the \emph{kind} of transformation, not from an inventory of
measurements.
\par
\textbf{Marking guide.} Full credit needs all three parts: the motion named (rotation,
$90^\circ$, counterclockwise, about the origin), the rule verified on each vertex, and a
justification that appeals to rotations preserving distance. Partial credit for a correct
name with the vertices checked but the congruence argued by measuring all three sides ---
correct mathematics, but it is the argument the question asked them to replace. Not
sufficient: ``they look the same size and shape''.
\par
\textbf{If a student says translation:} ask them what single slide could take a triangle
with a horizontal base to one with a vertical base. Orientation of the sides, not position,
is what rules a translation out here.
\par
Open description and justification --- $\ans{\text{open response}}$.}
