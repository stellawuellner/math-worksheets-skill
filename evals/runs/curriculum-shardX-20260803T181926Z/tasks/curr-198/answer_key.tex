\problem{\textbf{Raffle drum:} $20$ tickets, $8$ blue, one drawn.

\textbf{Solution:} Every ticket is the same size and shape, so the $20$
outcomes are equally likely and the probability model is
favourable over possible. Favourable outcomes: the $8$ blue tickets.
$P(\text{blue}) = \dfrac{8}{20}$, and dividing both parts by $4$ puts it in
lowest terms. \ans{\frac{2}{5}}

Sense check: $\frac{2}{5}$ is less than half, and fewer than half the tickets
are blue.}

\problem{\textbf{Homework sample:} $15$, $20$, $25$, $30$, $20$, $35$, $20$,
$15$ minutes.

\textbf{(a)} The mean is the total shared equally among the eight students.
Total: $15 + 20 + 25 + 30 + 20 + 35 + 20 + 15 = 180$ minutes. Divide by the
number of values: $180 \div 8$. \ans{22.5}

\textbf{(b)} The mode is the value that appears most often. Counting: $15$
appears twice, $25$, $30$ and $35$ once each, and one value appears three
times. \ans{20}

Reading the pair together: a typical student in this sample worked about
$22.5$ minutes, and the single most common time was $20$ minutes.}

\problem{\textbf{Tagged fish:} $12$ tagged and released, sample of $40$
contains $6$ tagged.

\textbf{(a)} The fraction of the sample that is tagged is
$\dfrac{6}{40}$; dividing both parts by $2$ gives lowest terms.
\ans{\frac{3}{20}}

\textbf{(b)} A random sample stands in for the pond, so about $\frac{3}{20}$ of
all the fish should be tagged. Exactly $12$ fish are tagged, and $12$ has to be
$\frac{3}{20}$ of the pond. Writing that as $\dfrac{12}{N} = \dfrac{6}{40}$ and
cross-multiplying gives $6N = 12 \times 40 = 480$, so $N = 480 \div 6$.
\ans{80}

Sense check: $\frac{3}{20}$ of $80$ is $12$ $\checkmark$. Estimate, not a
count --- another netting would give a slightly different number.}

\problem{\textbf{Prize spinner:} five equal sections --- two red, one blue, two
green.

\textbf{(a)} The five sections are equal, so the five outcomes are equally
likely. ``Not red'' happens on the blue section and the two green ones, which
is three of the five sections. \ans{\frac{3}{5}}

\textbf{(b)} Green covers two of the five sections. \ans{\frac{2}{5}}

Check: $P(\text{red}) = \frac{2}{5}$, and
$\frac{2}{5} + \frac{3}{5} = 1$ --- an event and its complement always total
one whole.}

\problem{\textbf{Pets per household:} $0$, $0$, $1$, $1$, $1$, $2$, $2$, $2$,
$3$, $3$, $4$, $5$.

\textbf{(a)} The values are already in order, and there are twelve of them, so
the median sits between the sixth and the seventh. Both of those values are
$2$, so the median is their average. \ans{2}

\textbf{(b)} The total is the sum of all twelve values:
$0 + 0 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 2 + 3 + 3 + 4 + 5$. Group them:
$(1+1+1) = 3$, $(2+2+2) = 6$, $(3+3) = 6$, then $3 + 6 + 6 + 4 + 5$.
\ans{24}}

\problem{\textbf{Walkers:} random sample of $50$ from $900$ students, $14$
walk.

\textbf{(a)} The sample fraction is $\dfrac{14}{50}$; dividing both parts by
$2$ gives lowest terms. \ans{\frac{7}{25}}

\textbf{(b)} The sample was drawn at random from the enrolment list, so it
represents the school: apply the same fraction to all $900$ students.
$\dfrac{7}{25} \times 900 = 7 \times 36$. \ans{252}

Sense check: $\frac{7}{25}$ is a little more than a quarter, and a quarter of
$900$ is $225$, so $252$ is the right size.}

\problem{\textbf{Number cube:} fair cube, one roll, then $300$ rolls.

\textbf{(a)} A fair cube has six equally likely faces and exactly one of them
shows a four. \ans{\frac{1}{6}}

\textbf{(b)} In the long run each face turns up about $\frac{1}{6}$ of the
time, so the predicted count is the probability times the number of rolls:
$\dfrac{1}{6} \times 300$. \ans{50}

Say it carefully: this is a prediction, not a guarantee. A real run of $300$
rolls landing on four somewhere near fifty times is exactly what the model
expects.}

\problem{\textbf{April temperatures:} $12$, $15$, $15$, $18$, $22$, $27$, $31$
degrees Fahrenheit.

\textbf{(a)} Seven values in order, so the median is the fourth one --- three
below it and three above it. \ans{18}

\textbf{(b)} The interquartile range measures the middle half. Split the
sample at the median. Lower half $12$, $15$, $15$ has median
$Q_1 = 15$; upper half $22$, $27$, $31$ has median $Q_3 = 27$. Then
$\text{IQR} = 27 - 15$. \ans{12}

The classmate's $19$ is the \emph{range}: $31 - 12$, the spread from the
coldest day to the warmest. The range uses only the two most extreme days, so
one unusual day moves it; the IQR describes the middle half and ignores both
tails.}

\problem{\textbf{Skate park survey:} $60$ asked at the skate park, $45$ say
skateboarding; school has $800$ students.

\textbf{(a)} $\dfrac{45}{60}$, and dividing both parts by $15$ gives lowest
terms. \ans{\frac{3}{4}}

\textbf{(b)} Applying that fraction to the whole school gives
$\dfrac{3}{4} \times 800$. \ans{600}

\textbf{(c)} (Open response --- judge by reading.) Model answer: the sample was
not random. Students at a skate park after school are far more likely to prefer
skateboarding than students in general, so the sample is biased toward the
answer being measured and $600$ is almost certainly too high. The arithmetic in
(b) is right; the sample it rests on is not.

A trustworthy method: number all $800$ students on the enrolment list and use a
random number generator to choose $60$ of them, then ask those students ---
every student has the same chance of being picked, whatever they do after
school.

Look for: the student names the bias (where and who was sampled) rather than
only saying ``too few people'', and describes a selection method that gives
every student an equal chance.}

\problem{\textbf{Simulation design.} (Open response --- judge by reading.)

Model answer: the four prize cards are equally likely, so I need a device with
four equally likely outcomes. Use a spinner with four equal sections, or a
random number generator producing the digits $1$ to $4$, or a bag with four
identical slips numbered $1$ to $4$.

Let outcome $1$ stand for the card I want and $2$, $3$, $4$ stand for the other
three cards. One trial is one spin, one generated digit, or one slip drawn and
put back --- one trial models buying one box.

Run at least $100$ trials, recording each result, then estimate the probability
as the number of trials that came out $1$ divided by the number of trials. With
many trials that fraction should settle near $\frac{1}{4}$, the theoretical
probability.

Look for: a device with four equally likely outcomes, an explicit rule linking
outcomes to the event, one trial clearly defined as one box, replacement (the
card in the next box does not depend on the last), and a plan to divide
successes by total trials.}
