\problem{\textbf{$\frac{1}{4}$ of $24$ crayons.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The bottom number is $4$, so break the $24$ crayons into $4$
equal groups. On the picture, three rows of eight ring neatly as four groups of
six --- any grouping works as long as the groups match.

\textbf{Step 2.} $24 \div 4 = 6$, so one group holds $6$ crayons.

\textbf{Step 3.} The top number is $1$, so Ravi takes one of those groups.

\ans{6 \text{ crayons}}

\textit{Teaching note:} say the sentence back --- ``one fourth of twenty-four is
six'' --- and check it the other way: four groups of six rebuild $24$.}

\problem{\textbf{$\frac{1}{3}$ of $21$ stickers.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The bottom number is $3$: break the $21$ stickers into $3$
equal groups. The picture is three rows of seven, so each row is already one
group.

\textbf{Step 2.} $21 \div 3 = 7$, so one group holds $7$ stickers.

\textbf{Step 3.} One group goes to her brother.

\ans{7 \text{ stickers}}

\textit{Listen for:} counting the rows instead of the stickers in a row. The
answer is how many are \emph{in} one group, not how many groups there are.}

\problem{\textbf{$\frac{2}{3}$ of $18$ marbles.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Divide first. The bottom number $3$ splits the bar into $3$
equal parts, and $18 \div 3 = 6$, so each part is worth $6$ marbles.

\textbf{Step 2.} Multiply second. The top number $2$ says to shade $2$ of those
parts: $2 \times 6$.

\ans{12 \text{ marbles}}

\textbf{Step 3.} Check on the picture: two shaded parts of $6$ and one unshaded
part of $6$ rebuild the $18$ $\checkmark$

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $27$ --- divided by the top number and multiplied
by the bottom one ($18 \div 2 \times 3$). The bottom number always makes the
groups.}

\problem{\textbf{$\frac{3}{4}$ of $32$ pages.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Divide: $32 \div 4 = 8$, so each of the $4$ equal parts of the
book is $8$ pages.

\textbf{Step 2.} Multiply: Dev has read $3$ of those parts, $3 \times 8$.

\ans{24 \text{ pages}}

\textbf{Step 3.} Check: the one part he has not read is $8$ pages, and
$24 + 8 = 32$ $\checkmark$

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $8$ --- the groups were made correctly and then
only one was counted. The top number is the instruction to take three.}

\problem{\textbf{Working backwards: one fifth of the bag is $8$ grapes.}

\textbf{Step 1.} This time the \emph{part} is known and the whole is missing.
The bottom number $5$ says the bag is $5$ equal parts, and one of them is $8$
grapes.

\textbf{Step 2.} Write $8$ in every part of the bar --- all five parts are the
same size, which is what ``equal parts'' means.

\textbf{Step 3.} The whole bag is all five parts: $5 \times 8$.

\ans{40 \text{ grapes}}

\textbf{Step 4.} Check forwards: one fifth of $40$ is $40 \div 5 = 8$
$\checkmark$

\textit{Teaching note:} forwards you divide, backwards you multiply. Drawing
the bar first is what keeps the two directions straight.}

\problem{\textbf{$\frac{5}{6}$ of a $42$-minute period.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Divide: $42 \div 6 = 7$, so each of the $6$ equal parts of the
period is $7$ minutes.

\textbf{Step 2.} Multiply: the project takes $5$ of those parts,
$5 \times 7$.

\ans{35 \text{ minutes}}

\textbf{Step 3.} Check: the leftover part is $7$ minutes of tidying, and
$35 + 7 = 42$ $\checkmark$

\textit{Teaching note:} nothing changes when the ``set'' is time instead of
objects --- $42$ minutes still breaks into six equal sevens.}

\problem{\textbf{$\frac{1}{8}$ of a $2.4$ litre jug.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The bottom number $8$ still means equal groups, so split the
$2.4$ litres into $8$ equal parts.

\textbf{Step 2.} $2.4 \div 8 = 0.3$, and the top number is $1$, so one part is
the answer.

\ans{0.3 \text{ L}}

\textbf{Step 3.} Check: eight glasses of $0.3$ litres is
$8 \times 0.3 = 2.4$ litres $\checkmark$

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $19.2$ --- the jug was multiplied by $8$ instead
of split into $8$ parts. A quick sense check kills it: one glass cannot hold
more than the whole jug.}

\problem{\textbf{$28$ students, $\frac{3}{7}$ ride the bus --- how many do
not?}

\textbf{Step 1.} Find the part the fraction names first. Divide:
$28 \div 7 = 4$, so each of the $7$ equal groups is $4$ students.

\textbf{Step 2.} Multiply: $3 \times 4 = 12$ students ride the bus.

\textbf{Step 3.} The question asks for the rest, so subtract from the whole
class: $28 - 12$.

\ans{16 \text{ students}}

\textbf{Step 4.} Check: $12 + 16 = 28$ $\checkmark$ (The students who do not
ride are $\frac{4}{7}$ of the class, and $4 \times 4 = 16$ as well.)

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $12$ --- the fraction was computed correctly and
the last sentence of the question was not read. Worth underlining the word
\emph{not} together.}

\problem{\textbf{Working backwards: $\frac{3}{8}$ of the theatre is $96$
people.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The shaded part of the bar is $3$ eighths, and those $3$ parts
together hold $96$ people.

\textbf{Step 2.} Find one part: $96 \div 3 = 32$ seats in one eighth.

\textbf{Step 3.} The whole theatre is $8$ of those parts: $8 \times 32$.

\ans{256 \text{ seats}}

\textbf{Step 4.} Check forwards: $256 \div 8 = 32$ and $3 \times 32 = 96$
$\checkmark$

\textit{Common wrong answer:} dividing $96$ by $8$ to get $12$. The $96$ covers
three parts, not one, so it has to be shared between three before the whole is
rebuilt.}

\problem{\textbf{What Sam got right and what he forgot.}

\textbf{Step 1.} What he did right: the bottom number is $5$, so splitting the
$45$ counters into five equal groups is exactly the first move, and
$45 \div 5 = 9$ counters per group is correct.

\textbf{Step 2.} What he forgot: the top number. $\frac{3}{5}$ asks for
\emph{three} of those groups, and Sam stopped after pointing at one. His $9$ is
the answer to $\frac{1}{5}$ of $45$, not $\frac{3}{5}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Finish the job: $3 \times 9$.

\ans{27 \text{ counters}}

\textbf{Step 4.} Check on the picture: three groups of $9$ are shaded and two
groups of $9$ are not, and $27 + 18 = 45$ $\checkmark$

\textit{Full credit on the sentence:} it has to name both halves --- the equal
groups were made correctly, and the top number was never used. ``He got it
wrong'' does not show that the student can see where the method broke.}
