\problem{\textbf{Round 48 to the nearest ten.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Find the two tens 48 sits between. Counting by tens:
$40, 50$. So the left end is $40$ and the right end is $50$.

\textbf{Step 2.} The middle mark is halfway between them, at $45$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $48$ is past the middle mark, so it is closer to the right
end. \ans{50}

\textit{Why the picture helps:} the student can see that $48$ is only two hops
from $50$ but eight hops from $40$.}

\problem{\textbf{Round 83 to the nearest ten.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The two tens around $83$ are $80$ and $90$, so those are the
ends of the line.

\textbf{Step 2.} The middle mark is $85$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $83$ has not reached the middle mark yet, so it stays with the
left end. \ans{80}

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $90$, from rounding ``up'' out of habit. Rounding
always goes to the \emph{nearer} end, and on this line the nearer end is
behind.}

\problem{\textbf{Round 260 to the nearest hundred.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Counting by hundreds, $260$ sits between $200$ and $300$.
Those are the ends.

\textbf{Step 2.} The middle mark is $250$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $260$ is past $250$, so it is closer to the right end.
\ans{300}

\textit{Same picture, longer hops:} each short tick is now worth ten instead of
one, but the question is still ``which end is closer?''}

\problem{\textbf{Round 418 to the nearest hundred.}

\textbf{Step 1.} $418$ sits between $400$ and $500$.

\textbf{Step 2.} The middle mark is $450$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $418$ is barely past the left end and nowhere near the middle,
so it stays at the left end. \ans{400}

\textit{Note:} the ones digit $8$ is a distraction here. When rounding to the
nearest hundred, only the position between $400$ and $500$ matters.}

\problem{\textbf{Write $<$ or $>$ between 536 and 563.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Both numbers have $5$ hundreds, so the hundreds place does not
decide it. Compare the next place along: tens.

\textbf{Step 2.} $536$ has $3$ tens; $563$ has $6$ tens. Three tens is less than
six tens, so $536$ sits to the left of $563$ on the number line.

\textbf{Step 3.} The number on the left is the smaller one. \ans{536 < 563}

\textit{Reading the symbol:} the small point of the symbol aims at the smaller
number.}

\problem{\textbf{Round 365 to the nearest ten.}

\textbf{Step 1.} $365$ sits between $360$ and $370$, so those are the ends.

\textbf{Step 2.} The middle mark is $365$ --- the number lands exactly on it.

\textbf{Step 3.} A tie needs a rule, and the agreed rule is to take the bigger
end. \ans{370}

\textit{Say the rule out loud:} ``exactly halfway rounds up.'' It is a
convention, not something the picture decides --- both ends really are five
hops away.}

\problem{\textbf{Order 307, 370 and 273 from least to greatest.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Compare hundreds first. $273$ has $2$ hundreds; the other two
have $3$ hundreds. So $273$ is the smallest and goes first.

\textbf{Step 2.} $307$ and $370$ both have $3$ hundreds, so compare tens:
$0$ tens against $7$ tens. $307$ comes before $370$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Reading the number line from left to right:
\ans{273,\ 307,\ 370}

\textit{Why these numbers:} they use the same digits in different places, so
the only way through is to compare place by place.}

\problem{\textbf{Round 650 to the nearest hundred.}

\textbf{Step 1.} $650$ sits between $600$ and $700$.

\textbf{Step 2.} The middle mark is $650$, so this is another exact tie.

\textbf{Step 3.} By the halfway rule, take the bigger end. \ans{700}

\textit{Check the pattern:} it matches problem 6 --- exactly halfway always
goes to the larger benchmark, whether the benchmarks are tens or hundreds.}

\problem{\textbf{Mia wrote $409 > 490$. Correct her.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Comparing starts at the biggest place, not the last digit.
Both numbers have $4$ hundreds, so move to the tens.

\textbf{Step 2.} $409$ has $0$ tens; $490$ has $9$ tens. So $409$ lands just
past $400$ on the line, while $490$ sits far to the right, near $500$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The number further left is smaller. \ans{409 < 490}

Mia compared the ones digits first. Comparing should start with the hundreds,
then the tens, and only then the ones --- a single one is worth far less than a
single ten.}

\problem{\textbf{472 between two tens: (a) hops past 470, (b) hops short of
480, (c) round to the nearest ten.}

\textbf{Step 1 (a).} From $470$, count on: $471, 472$ --- two hops past the
lower ten. \ans{2}

\textbf{Step 2 (b).} From $472$ up to $480$: $473, 474, 475, 476, 477, 478,
479, 480$ --- eight hops short of the upper ten. \ans{8}

\textbf{Step 3 (c).} Two hops is a shorter trip than eight hops, so $472$ is
closer to the lower ten. \ans{470}

\textit{This is what rounding means:} not a digit trick, but ``which benchmark
am I nearer to''. The digit rule (look at the ones place) is just a shortcut
for this count.}

\problem{\textbf{Order 418, 481, 184 and 148 from greatest to least.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Sort by hundreds first: $418$ and $481$ have $4$ hundreds;
$184$ and $148$ have $1$ hundred. So both $4$-hundreds numbers come first.

\textbf{Step 2.} Within the $400$s, compare tens: $1$ ten against $8$ tens, so
$481$ is greater than $418$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Within the $100$s, compare tens: $8$ tens against $4$ tens, so
$184$ is greater than $148$.

\textbf{Step 4.} Greatest to least: \ans{481,\ 418,\ 184,\ 148}

\textit{Why it is a challenge:} every number uses the digits $1$, $4$ and $8$,
so only place value tells them apart.}

\problem{\textbf{Challenge: Room A 287 cans, Room B 312 cans, Room C 296 cans.}

\textbf{Step 1 (a).} Compare hundreds: $287$ and $296$ have $2$ hundreds,
$312$ has $3$. So $312$ is the greatest. Between the other two, compare tens:
$8$ tens against $9$ tens, so $287$ is less than $296$.

\textbf{Step 2 (a).} Least to greatest: \ans{287,\ 296,\ 312}

\textbf{Step 3 (b).} Round each to the nearest hundred. $287$ and $296$ are
both past the middle mark $250$ on the $200$-to-$300$ line, and $312$ has not
reached the middle mark $350$ on the $300$-to-$400$ line, so all three counts
land on the same hundred. \ans{300}

\textbf{Step 4 (c).} \textit{Sample answer (accept any equivalent
explanation):} ``All three counts are close enough to 300 that rounding hides
the difference between them. Rounding tells you about how many, so you have to
go back to the exact numbers to say who collected the most --- Room B, with
312.''

\textit{Grading note for part (c):} the sentence should say that rounding
throws away the detail that separates the rooms, or that equal rounded values
can come from different exact values. Marking this part is a judgement call ---
it is the one item on the sheet that is not machine-checked.}
