\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: $\displaystyle\int_{5}^{2} 2x\,dx$.}\\
Step 1: name the slip. The FTC says $\int_a^b f = F(b) - F(a)$, with $a$ the
\emph{written} lower bound. Here $a = 5$ and $b = 2$, so the subtraction is
$F(2) - F(5)$. Jae silently reordered the bounds into increasing order.\\
Step 2: with $F(x) = x^2$, evaluate in the order the integral was written:
$F(2) - F(5) = 4 - 25$.\\
Step 3: the value is $\ans{-21}$.\\
Step 4: check with the reversal rule --- $\int_2^5 2x\,dx = 25 - 4 = 21$, and
$\int_5^2 = -\int_2^5 = -21$ $\checkmark$.\\
\emph{Watch for:} $21$, the right magnitude with the wrong sign. A backwards
integral of a positive function must come out negative.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: $\displaystyle\int_{1}^{3} (3x^2 - 4)\,dx$.}\\
\textbf{(a)} Step 1: the antiderivative is
$F(x) = \ans{x^3 - 4x}$, since differentiating it returns $3x^2 - 4$.\\
\textbf{(b)} Step 2: name the slip. Ravi lost the parentheses around $F(1)$. The
FTC subtracts the \emph{whole} value $F(1) = 1 - 4 = -3$, so the minus sign must
reach both terms: $-(1 - 4) = +3$, not $-1 - 4$.\\
Step 3: $F(3) - F(1) = (27 - 12) - (1 - 4) = 15 - (-3) = 15 + 3$, so the integral
is $\ans{18}$.\\
Step 4: check by splitting the integrand:
$\int_1^3 3x^2\,dx = 27 - 1 = 26$ and $\int_1^3 4\,dx = 8$, and
$26 - 8 = 18$ $\checkmark$.\\
\emph{Watch for:} $10$, exactly $8$ low --- twice the term that lost its sign.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2} (3t^2 + 2)\,dt$.}\\
Step 1: name the slip. Noor substituted the bound into the \emph{integrand}. The
bounds are only used after antidifferentiation; the integrand is what gets
integrated, never what gets evaluated.\\
Step 2: antidifferentiate with respect to $t$: $F(t) = t^3 + 2t$.\\
Step 3: $F(2) - F(0) = (8 + 4) - 0 = \ans{12}$.\\
Step 4: sanity check by size --- on $[0,2]$ the integrand runs from $2$ up to
$14$, so the integral must lie between $2 \times 2 = 4$ and $2 \times 14 = 28$;
$12$ fits and is nearer the low end, as it should be for a curve that stays small
until late $\checkmark$.\\
\emph{Watch for:} $14$. That is the integrand's own value at $t = 2$, which is
the tell-tale of this error: the answer equals $f$(upper bound).}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: $\displaystyle\int_{6}^{4} f(x)\,dx$ from
$\int_1^6 f = 10$ and $\int_1^4 f = 3$.}\\
\textbf{(a)} Step 1: additivity gives
$\int_1^4 f + \int_4^6 f = \int_1^6 f$, so $\int_4^6 f = 10 - 3 = 7$. The
student's $7$ is that integral --- correct, but for the bounds in
\emph{increasing} order, which is not what was asked.\\
\textbf{(b)} Step 2: the requested integral runs from $6$ down to $4$, so it is
the negative of the one just found: $\int_6^4 f = -\int_4^6 f = -(10 - 3)$.\\
Step 3: $\int_6^4 f(x)\,dx = \ans{-7}$.\\
Step 4: check by chaining the reversal directly:
$\int_6^4 f = \int_6^1 f + \int_1^4 f = -10 + 3 = -7$ $\checkmark$.\\
\emph{Watch for:} $7$. Whenever the answer differs from the right one only in
sign, look at the bound order before looking at the arithmetic.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{\pi/3} \sin x\,dx$.}\\
Step 1: name the slip. The antiderivative of $\sin x$ is $-\cos x$, because
$\frac{d}{dx}(-\cos x) = \sin x$ while $\frac{d}{dx}(\cos x) = -\sin x$. Lena's
version is the antiderivative of $-\sin x$, so her answer comes out with every
sign reversed.\\
Step 2: evaluate the correct antiderivative:
$\bigl[-\cos x\bigr]_0^{\pi/3} = -\cos(\pi/3) + \cos(0)$.\\
Step 3: $= -\dfrac{1}{2} + 1 = \ans{0.5}$.\\
Step 4: check by sign reasoning --- $\sin x > 0$ on $(0, \pi/3)$, so the integral
must be positive. Lena's $-0.5$ is negative and can be rejected before any
arithmetic is examined $\checkmark$.\\
\emph{Watch for:} $-0.5$. Always run the positive-integrand test on a trig
integral: it catches a flipped antiderivative in one line.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: $\displaystyle G(x) = \int_{x}^{5} (t^2 + 1)\,dt$,
find $G'(2)$.}\\
\textbf{(a)} Step 1: FTC Part 1 differentiates an accumulation whose
\emph{upper} bound is the variable. Here $x$ is the lower bound, so flip it
first: $G(x) = -\displaystyle\int_{5}^{x} (t^2 + 1)\,dt$. The reversal
contributes the minus sign Kwame dropped.\\
Step 2: differentiate the flipped form: $G'(x) = -(x^2 + 1)$.\\
\textbf{(b)} Step 3: $G'(2) = -(4 + 1) = \ans{-5}$.\\
Step 4: check by computing $G$ explicitly:
$G(x) = \bigl[\frac{t^3}{3} + t\bigr]_x^5 = \frac{140}{3} - \frac{x^3}{3} - x$,
so $G'(x) = -x^2 - 1$, giving $-5$ at $x = 2$ $\checkmark$. It also makes sense:
raising $x$ shrinks the interval $[x, 5]$, so $G$ must decrease.\\
\emph{Watch for:} $5$. The magnitude is right, so the only thing to check is
which bound the variable occupies.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: $\displaystyle\int_{0}^{2} x(x^2+1)^3\,dx$ by
substitution.}\\
\textbf{(a)} Step 1: with $u = x^2 + 1$ we get $du = 2x\,dx$, so
$x\,dx = \frac{1}{2}du$. The bounds are $x$-values and must be converted:
$x = 0 \Rightarrow u = 1$ and $x = 2 \Rightarrow u = 5$. The new interval is
$u$ from $1$ to $5$.\\
Step 2: name the slip. Ines evaluated a $u$-antiderivative at $x$-bounds ---
two different variables, one pair of numbers.\\
\textbf{(b)} Step 3: $\displaystyle\int_{1}^{5}\frac{u^3}{2}\,du
= \Bigl[\frac{u^4}{8}\Bigr]_1^5 = \frac{625}{8} - \frac{1}{8} = \frac{624}{8}$,
so the integral is $\ans{78}$.\\
Step 4: check by expanding instead of substituting:
$x(x^2+1)^3 = x^7 + 3x^5 + 3x^3 + x$, whose integral over $[0,2]$ is
$32 + 32 + 12 + 2 = 78$ $\checkmark$.\\
\emph{Watch for:} $2$. Unconverted bounds usually produce an answer far too
small, because $u$-values are shifted away from the $x$-values that were used.}

\problem{\textbf{Explain: reversal, dummies, and substitution.}\\
\emph{Open response --- flagged for manual review. Model answer below; accept any
equivalent reasoning.}\\
\textbf{(a)} By the FTC, $\int_a^b f = F(b) - F(a)$ and
$\int_b^a f = F(a) - F(b)$. These two expressions are negatives of each other for
every $F$, so swapping the bounds negates the integral. Geometrically, the
definite integral is a \emph{signed} area with a direction built in: traversing
the interval right-to-left reverses the sign of every $dx$, so the same region is
counted negatively. The magnitude of the area is unchanged; only the orientation
is.\\
\textbf{(b)} In $\int_a^b f(t)\,dt$ the variable $t$ is bound by the integral: it
takes every value between $a$ and $b$ and is gone by the time the value is
reported. The answer depends on $f$, $a$ and $b$ only, so writing $t$ in it is
meaningless --- $\int_0^2 3t^2\,dt$ and $\int_0^2 3s^2\,ds$ are the same number.
An accumulation function is different because its \emph{bound} is the variable:
in $F(x) = \int_a^x f(t)\,dt$ the letter $t$ is still consumed, but $x$ survives
as the input, and FTC Part 1 gives $F'(x) = f(x)$ --- the integrand's rule
evaluated at the moving bound.\\
\textbf{(c)} A substitution replaces $x$ by $u$ everywhere, and the bounds are
$x$-values. Leaving them attached to a $u$-antiderivative evaluates the right
function at the wrong two numbers, which is not a small error --- it silently
answers a different question. Two habits prevent it: write the converted bounds
onto the integral sign \emph{at the moment} you write $u = \ldots$, so the
integral is never in a half-converted state; or skip conversion entirely by
back-substituting to $x$ before evaluating.\\
\emph{What a full-credit answer must contain:} the $F(b) - F(a)$ argument (or an
equivalent signed-area argument) in (a), an explicit statement that the variable
of integration is bound/consumed while the bound variable of an accumulation
function is not in (b), and in (c) an account of evaluating a $u$-expression at
$x$-numbers plus a concrete preventive habit.}
