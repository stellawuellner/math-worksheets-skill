\sstitleblock{Circles and Parabolas in Standard Form}

\skillheading{Skill 1 --- Completing the square on a circle}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Standard (centre-radius) form:} $(x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2$, with centre $(h,k)$ and
radius $r$. The number on the right is $r^2$, never $r$.\par
\smallskip
\textbf{The move:} $x^2 + bx$ becomes a perfect square when you add $\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2$,
and it then equals $\left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^2$.\par
\smallskip
\textbf{The method:} group the $x$ terms and the $y$ terms, move the constant to the right,
complete both squares, and add each new number to the right-hand side as well. If the squared
terms carry a coefficient, divide the whole equation by it first.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad Write $x^2 + y^2 + 2x - 8y + 8 = 0$ in standard form.
\step{The equation has both squares with coefficient $1$ and no way to read a centre off it, so
group each variable and complete the square twice.}
\step{$(x^2 + 2x) + (y^2 - 8y) = -8$; half of $2$ is $1$ so add $1$, half of $-8$ is $-4$ so add
$16$, and both go on the right: $-8 + 1 + 16 = 9$.}

$\Rightarrow\quad \ans{(x+1)^2 + (y-4)^2 = 9}$, \quad centre $(-1, 4)$, radius $3$.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad Write $x^2 + y^2 - 4x - 6y - 3 = 0$ in standard form.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{(x-2)^2 + (y-3)^2 = 16}$, centre $(2,3)$, radius $4$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{Skill 2 --- Completing the square on a quadratic}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Vertex form:} $y = a(x-h)^2 + k$, with vertex $(h,k)$; the parabola opens up when
$a > 0$ and down when $a < 0$.\par
\smallskip
\textbf{The balancing is different from a circle.} There is only one expression here, not two
sides, so the number you add must be subtracted again straight away: $x^2 + bx + c$ becomes
$\left(x + \frac{b}{2}\right)^2 - \left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2 + c$.\par
\smallskip
If a coefficient sits in front of $x^2$, factor it out of the $x$ terms \emph{only} --- then
whatever you add inside the bracket is multiplied by that coefficient before it is subtracted
back off. Completing the square in $y$ instead of $x$ works identically and gives a parabola
lying on its side.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad Write $y = x^2 + 10x + 18$ in vertex form.
\step{Nothing about the vertex is visible in this form, and the coefficient of $x^2$ is $1$, so
complete the square directly on the $x$ terms.}
\step{Half of $10$ is $5$ and $5^2 = 25$, so add $25$ and take it away again:
$y = (x^2 + 10x + 25) - 25 + 18$.}

$\Rightarrow\quad \ans{y = (x+5)^2 - 7}$, \quad vertex $(-5, -7)$.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad Write $y = x^2 - 6x + 2$ in vertex form.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{y = (x-3)^2 - 7}$, vertex $(3,-7)$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{Skill 3 --- Reading the graph off the finished form}

\begin{formulabox}
Once a circle is in standard form you can read the centre and radius straight off, and that is
what makes every other question answerable.\par
\smallskip
\textbf{Signs:} $(x+4)^2$ is $(x-(-4))^2$, so $h = -4$. Flip the printed sign.\par
\smallskip
\textbf{Is a point on the circle?} Measure its distance $d$ from the centre,
\[ d = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2} \]
and compare it with $r$: less means inside, equal means on the circle, greater means outside.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad For $(x-4)^2 + (y+2)^2 = 36$, give the radius and decide where the point
$(4, 4)$ lies.
\step{The form gives the centre $(4,-2)$ and $r^2 = 36$ for free, so the question becomes a
distance comparison rather than anything algebraic.}
\step{Radius: $\sqrt{36}$. Distance from $(4,-2)$ to $(4,4)$: the two points share an
$x$-coordinate, so it is just $4 - (-2)$.}

$\Rightarrow\quad r = \ans{6}$ and the distance is $\ans{6}$, so the point sits exactly
\emph{on} the circle.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad For $(x+3)^2 + (y-1)^2 = 25$, how far is $(1, 4)$ from the centre, and
where does it lie?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: distance $\ans{5}$, equal to the radius, so it is on the
circle}
\end{tryitbox}
