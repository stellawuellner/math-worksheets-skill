\wstitleblock{Quadrilaterals: Which Way Does the Implication Go?}{High School Geometry --- Polygons}{}

\noindent\small\textbf{Skills practised:} Testing one-way implications with algebra, side lengths,
diagonals, and counterexamples.
\smallskip

\noindent Every square is a rhombus, but not every rhombus is a square: the implication runs one
way only. Each problem below contains a claim that runs the implication backwards, or asks you to
decide how far the evidence actually reaches. \textbf{Answer the computational part first, then
write the diagnosis in a full sentence} --- naming the property that was assumed and the property
that was actually established.\normalsize

\problem[6cm]{$MNPQ$ is a rhombus. One interior angle measures $(4x + 10)^\circ$ and a consecutive
interior angle measures $(6x + 20)^\circ$.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{(a)} Find $x$. \quad \textbf{(b)} Must $MNPQ$ be a square? Justify your answer using the
angle measures you found.}

\problem{Quadrilateral $ABCD$ has vertices $A(1,1)$, $B(5,3)$, $C(7,7)$ and $D(3,5)$, and all four
sides are congruent. Priya writes: \emph{``Four congruent sides, so $ABCD$ is a square --- and
therefore its diagonals are congruent, both about $8.49$.''}
\par\smallskip
\textbf{(a)} Compute the lengths of \emph{both} diagonals $AC$ and $BD$, to two decimal places.
\quad \textbf{(b)} Which of Priya's two claims is the one that fails, and what is the most specific
correct name for $ABCD$?

\problem{For each statement, write \textbf{always true} or \textbf{not always true}. When it is not
always true, describe one counterexample precisely enough that a classmate could draw it.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{(a)} If a quadrilateral is a rectangle, then it is a parallelogram.
\par\vspace{1.5cm}
\textbf{(b)} If a quadrilateral is a parallelogram, then it is a rectangle.
\par\vspace{1.5cm}
\textbf{(c)} If the diagonals of a quadrilateral are perpendicular, then it is a rhombus.
\par\vspace{1.5cm}
\textbf{(d)} If a quadrilateral is a square, then it is a rhombus.
\par\vspace{1.5cm}
\noansline}

\problem{Quadrilateral $ABCD$ has vertices $A(0,6)$, $B(3,0)$, $C(0,-2)$ and $D(-3,0)$. Sam writes:
\emph{``One diagonal is vertical and the other is horizontal, so the diagonals are perpendicular ---
that makes $ABCD$ a rhombus, with every side about $6.71$.''}
\par\smallskip
\textbf{(a)} Compute $AB$ and $BC$, to two decimal places. \quad \textbf{(b)} Sam's diagonal
observation is correct. Explain exactly what is missing from his argument, and name $ABCD$.

\problem[6cm]{$PQRS$ is a parallelogram whose diagonals measure $PR = 3x - 5$ and $QS = x + 11$.
Sam sets the diagonals equal, writes $x = 3$, and concludes: \emph{``congruent diagonals, so
$PQRS$ must be a square.''}
\par\smallskip
\textbf{(a)} Find and fix Sam's arithmetic slip: what is the correct value of $x$?
\quad \textbf{(b)} With the corrected value, what is the most specific name $PQRS$ is guaranteed to
have --- and what extra piece of information would be needed to reach ``square''?}

\problem{\textbf{Challenge.} Quadrilateral $WXYZ$ has vertices $W(-1,2)$, $X(3,4)$, $Y(5,0)$ and
$Z(1,-2)$.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{(a)} Compute the side length $WX$ and both diagonals $WY$ and $XZ$, to two decimal places.
(The other three sides are congruent to $WX$ --- you may take that as given.)
\quad \textbf{(b)} Give the most specific name for $WXYZ$ and state which implication justifies it.
Which single test would have been enough to rule out ``rhombus but not square''?
