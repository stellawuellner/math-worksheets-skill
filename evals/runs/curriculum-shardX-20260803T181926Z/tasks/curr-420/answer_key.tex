\problem{\textbf{Evaluate Curve G at $t = 2$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The input is given, so this is a substitution --- and the
angle must be read in radians, which is what $\pi t/4$ produces.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\dfrac{\pi(2)}{4} = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$, and
$\sin\dfrac{\pi}{2} = 1$ from the unit circle.

\textbf{Step 3.} $h(2) = 5(1) + 9 = \ans{14}$, which is the highest point on
Curve G --- the graph agrees.}

\problem{\textbf{Amplitude of Curve G.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Amplitude is the distance from the midline to a peak, which is
\emph{half} the total rise from the lowest value to the highest --- not the
whole rise.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\dfrac{14 - 4}{2} = \dfrac{10}{2}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The amplitude is $\ans{5}$.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $10$ --- the full vertical distance between the
minimum and the maximum, which is twice the amplitude.}

\problem{\textbf{Midline of Curve G.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The midline sits exactly halfway between the highest and
lowest values, so it is their average, not their difference.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\dfrac{14 + 4}{2} = \dfrac{18}{2}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The midline is $y = \ans{9}$.

\textit{Teaching note:} together problems 2 and 3 recover $A$ and $D$ without
knowing the equation. That is the whole idea of reading parameters off a
graph.}

\problem{\textbf{Period and $B$ for Curve G.}

\textbf{(a) Step 1.} One period is the horizontal distance between two
\emph{successive} maxima --- peak to peak, not peak to trough.

\textbf{Step 2.} The maxima sit at $t = 2$ and $t = 10$, so the period is
$10 - 2 = \ans{8}$.

\textbf{(b) Step 3.} $B$ is fixed by the period through
$\text{period} = \dfrac{2\pi}{B}$, so $B = \dfrac{2\pi}{8}$.

\textbf{Step 4.} $B = \ans{\frac{\pi}{4}}$, which matches the
$\sin(\pi t/4)$ in the equation of Curve G.}

\problem{\textbf{Building the Ferris wheel model.}

\textbf{(a) Step 1.} The rider travels around a circle, so the height swings a
full diameter from bottom to top; the amplitude is the radius.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\dfrac{40}{2} = \ans{20}$ metres.

\textbf{(b) Step 3.} One full turn is one period, and a turn takes $12$
minutes, so $B = \dfrac{2\pi}{12}$.

\textbf{Step 4.} $B = \ans{\frac{\pi}{6}}$.

\textit{Completing the model:} the lowest point is $3$ metres up, so the
midline is $3 + 20 = 23$, and boarding at the bottom means starting at the
minimum --- hence the leading minus sign in
$h(t) = -20\cos(\pi t/6) + 23$.}

\problem{\textbf{Height after four minutes.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The time is given, so substitute --- and keep the calculator
in radian mode, because $\pi t/6$ is a radian measure.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\dfrac{\pi(4)}{6} = \dfrac{2\pi}{3}$, and
$\cos\dfrac{2\pi}{3} = -\dfrac{1}{2}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $h(4) = -20\left(-\dfrac{1}{2}\right) + 23 = 10 + 23 = \ans{33}$
metres.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} about $3.05$ --- the cosine was taken in degrees,
which returns a number near the bottom of the wheel and looks plausible. Radian
mode is not optional here.}

\problem{\textbf{When the rider is level with the centre.}

\textbf{Step 1.} $h(t) = 23$ makes the cosine term zero, so the question
reduces to $\cos(\pi t / 6) = 0$ --- and cosine is zero at more than one place
per cycle, so expect more than one answer.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\cos\theta = 0$ when $\theta = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ or
$\theta = \dfrac{3\pi}{2}$ within one revolution.

\textbf{Step 3.} Setting $\dfrac{\pi t}{6} = \dfrac{\pi}{2}$ gives $t = 3$;
setting $\dfrac{\pi t}{6} = \dfrac{3\pi}{2}$ gives $t = 9$.

\textbf{Step 4.} Both lie in $0 \le t < 12$, so the times are
$\ans{t = 3 \text{ and } t = 9}$ minutes --- once on the way up, once on the
way down.

\textit{Grading note:} an answer of $t = 3$ alone is the error this problem is
built to catch. A horizontal line through the midline cuts one cycle of the
curve twice.}

\problem{\textbf{Building the harbour tide model.}

\textbf{(a) Step 1.} High and low water are the maximum and minimum, so the
amplitude is half their difference: $\dfrac{12 - 4}{2}$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $A = \ans{4}$.

\textbf{(b) Step 3.} The midline is their average: $\dfrac{12 + 4}{2}$, so
$D = \ans{8}$.

\textbf{(c) Step 4.} High tide at $t = 3$ and the next low at $t = 9$ are
\emph{half} a period apart, so half a period is $6$ hours and the full period
is $12$ hours.

\textbf{Step 5.} $B = \dfrac{2\pi}{12} = \ans{\frac{\pi}{6}}$, giving
$d(t) = 4\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi(t-3)}{6}\right) + 8$.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $B = \pi/3$, from treating the six hours between
high and low as a whole period. Peak to trough is always half.}

\problem{\textbf{Parameters of a described sinusoid.}

\textbf{(a) Step 1.} Same three formulas as Curve G, now with the maximum and
minimum given in words instead of drawn.

\textbf{Step 2.} Amplitude $= \dfrac{18 - 2}{2} = \ans{8}$.

\textbf{(b) Step 3.} Midline $= \dfrac{18 + 2}{2} = \ans{10}$.

\textbf{(c) Step 4.} The maximum at $x = 1$ and the \emph{next} minimum at
$x = 7$ are half a period apart, so half the period is $6$ and the period is
$2(6) = \ans{12}$.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $6$ --- the maximum-to-minimum gap read as a full
period. Sketching one arch is the fastest way to see the factor of two.}

\problem{\textbf{Depth at $t = 5$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The model is built, so this is substitution: put $t = 5$ into
$d(t) = 4\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi(t-3)}{6}\right) + 8$.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\dfrac{\pi(5 - 3)}{6} = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$, and
$\cos\dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{1}{2}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $d(5) = 4\left(\dfrac{1}{2}\right) + 8 = 2 + 8 = \ans{10}$
feet.}

\problem{\textbf{All times when the depth is $10$ feet.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Subtract the midline first so the cosine stands alone:
$4\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi(t-3)}{6}\right) = 2$, so
$\cos\!\left(\dfrac{\pi(t-3)}{6}\right) = \dfrac{1}{2}$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Cosine equals $\frac{1}{2}$ at $\theta = \pm\dfrac{\pi}{3}$
within one revolution --- two angles, because cosine is symmetric about
$\theta = 0$.

\textbf{Step 3.} $\dfrac{\pi(t-3)}{6} = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$ gives $t - 3 = 2$, so
$t = 5$. \ $\dfrac{\pi(t-3)}{6} = -\dfrac{\pi}{3}$ gives $t - 3 = -2$, so
$t = 1$.

\textbf{Step 4.} Both lie in $0 \le t < 12$, so the depth is $10$ feet at
$\ans{t = 1 \text{ and } t = 5}$ hours.

\textbf{Step 5.} How the graph predicts the count: $y = 10$ is a horizontal
line above the midline but below the maximum of $12$, so it cuts each single
cycle of the curve exactly twice --- once as the tide rises to high water at
$t = 3$ and once as it falls again.}

\problem{\textbf{Synthesis: two models of one wheel.}

\textbf{(a) Step 1.} Ana's model at $t = 5$: $\dfrac{\pi(5)}{6} = \dfrac{5\pi}{6}$
and $\cos\dfrac{5\pi}{6} = -\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, so
$h(5) = -20\left(-\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) + 23$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Ben's model at $t = 5$: $\dfrac{\pi(5-3)}{6} = \dfrac{\pi}{3}$
and $\sin\dfrac{\pi}{3} = \dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}$, so
$h(5) = 20\left(\dfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\right) + 23$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Both give $\ans{10\sqrt{3} + 23}$ metres, about $40.3$ metres.

\textbf{(b) Step 4.} \textit{(Open response --- flagged for manual review; read
the student's argument.)} A full-credit explanation contains all of this.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2cm, itemsep=2pt, topsep=3pt]
\item Sine and cosine are the same wave a quarter period apart:
$\sin\!\big(\theta - \frac{\pi}{2}\big) = -\cos\theta$. Ben's shift of $3$
minutes is exactly a quarter of the $12$-minute period, so his curve and Ana's
are the same curve.
\item A graph pins down the amplitude, the midline and the period uniquely ---
those are measurements of the picture and cannot be argued with.
\item A graph does \emph{not} pin down the horizontal shift $C$ or the choice
of sine against cosine, and it does not pin down the sign of $A$ on its own:
those three trade off against one another. Infinitely many
$(A, C)$ pairs describe one curve, because shifting by a whole period changes
nothing and shifting by half a period is the same as flipping the sign of $A$.
\end{itemize}

\textit{Grading note:} accept any argument that identifies amplitude, midline
and period as determined and the phase/form as not. ``They are both right
because they both work'' is the answer to push back on --- ask \emph{why} they
both work.}
