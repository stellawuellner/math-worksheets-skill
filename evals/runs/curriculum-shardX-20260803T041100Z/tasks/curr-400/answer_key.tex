\problem{\textbf{Is $(7, 1)$ on the circle with center $(3, -2)$, radius 5?}
\par
The center-radius form subtracts the center coordinates, and a $k$ of $-2$
makes that subtraction into $y - (-2) = y + 2$:
\[ (7 - 3)^2 + (1 + 2)^2 = 4^2 + 3^2 = 16 + 9 = 25 \]
Since $25 = r^2 = 5^2$, the point is exactly $5$ units from the center, so yes,
it lies on the circle.
\par
$\ans{25}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Common wrong answer 101:} the center coordinates were added instead of
subtracted, giving $(7+3)^2 + (1-2)^2$. The sign inside the bracket is the
opposite of the sign of the coordinate --- a center of $(3, -2)$ produces
$(x-3)$ and $(y+2)$.}

\problem{\textbf{On $x^2 + y^2 = 25$, find both $y$ when $x = 3$.}
\par
Substitute and solve; a circle is not a function, so a vertical line at
$x = 3$ meets it twice and both roots are real answers.
\begin{align*}
  3^2 + y^2 &= 25 \\
  y^2 &= 16 \\
  y &= \pm 4
\end{align*}
\par
$\ans{y = -4 \text{ or } y = 4}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Grading note:} reporting only $y = 4$ loses the point $(3, -4)$. The
$\pm$ is not decoration; it is the second intersection.}

\problem{\textbf{Parabola with focus $(0, 3)$ and directrix $y = -3$.}
\par
The focus sits $p = 3$ above the vertex and the directrix sits $3$ below it,
so the standard form $x^2 = 4py$ becomes $x^2 = 12y$. Solve for $y$ and
substitute $x = 6$:
\[ y = \frac{x^2}{4p} = \frac{6^2}{12} = \frac{36}{12} = 3 \]
\par
$\ans{3}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Common wrong answer 12:} the equation was written as $x^2 = py$, so the
student divided by $3$ instead of by $12$. The focal distance is $p$; the
coefficient in the equation is $4p$.}

\problem{\textbf{On $x^2 = 12y$, find both $x$ when $y = 12$.}
\begin{align*}
  x^2 &= 12(12) = 144 \\
  x &= \pm 12
\end{align*}
\par
$\ans{x = -12 \text{ or } x = 12}$
\par\smallskip
A parabola opening upward is symmetric about the $y$-axis, so a horizontal
line above the vertex always meets it twice.}

\problem{\textbf{Line $y = x + 1$ meets circle $x^2 + y^2 = 25$.}
\par
The line is already solved for $y$, so substitute it into the circle --- one
equation in one variable is the whole point of substitution:
\begin{align*}
  x^2 + (x + 1)^2 &= 25 \\
  2x^2 + 2x + 1 &= 25 \\
  2x^2 + 2x - 24 &= 0 \\
  x^2 + x - 12 &= 0 \\
  (x - 3)(x + 4) &= 0
\end{align*}
So $x = 3$ or $x = -4$. Substitute each back into the \emph{line} (the easier
equation): $y = 3 + 1 = 4$ and $y = -4 + 1 = -3$.
\par
$\ans{(3,\ 4) \text{ and } (-4,\ -3)}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Check:} $9 + 16 = 25$ $\checkmark$ and $16 + 9 = 25$ $\checkmark$}

\problem{\textbf{Complete the square: $x^2 + y^2 - 6x + 8y - 11 = 0$.}
\par
Group each variable and move the constant across:
\[ (x^2 - 6x) + (y^2 + 8y) = 11 \]
Half of $-6$ is $-3$, and $(-3)^2 = 9$; half of $8$ is $4$, and $4^2 = 16$.
Add both to \emph{each} side:
\[ (x^2 - 6x + 9) + (y^2 + 8y + 16) = 11 + 9 + 16 \]
\[ (x - 3)^2 + (y + 4)^2 = 36 \]
Read the center off the brackets (opposite signs) and the radius as
$\sqrt{36}$:
\par
$\ans{\text{center } (3,\ -4), \quad r = 6}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Common wrong answer $r \approx 3.32$:} the constant $11$ was taken as
$r^2$ and the two added constants were never carried to the right-hand side.
Whatever you add on the left must appear on the right, and here that turns
$11$ into $36$.}

\problem{\textbf{Circle $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ meets parabola $y = x^2 - 5$.}
\par
The parabola gives $x^2 = y + 5$, and substituting that into the circle
eliminates $x$ entirely --- a much cleaner substitution than solving for $x$:
\begin{align*}
  (y + 5) + y^2 &= 25 \\
  y^2 + y - 20 &= 0 \\
  (y + 5)(y - 4) &= 0
\end{align*}
So $y = 4$ or $y = -5$.
\par
If $y = 4$: $x^2 = 4 + 5 = 9$, so $x = \pm 3$.
\par
If $y = -5$: $x^2 = -5 + 5 = 0$, so $x = 0$ only --- the parabola's vertex
touches the bottom of the circle.
\par
$\ans{(3,\ 4),\ (-3,\ 4) \text{ and } (0,\ -5)}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Why three and not four:} each $y$ usually gives two $x$ values, but
$x^2 = 0$ gives a repeated root, so that intersection is a single tangential
point.}

\problem{\textbf{Line $y = 2x - 3$ meets parabola $y = x^2 - 2x + 1$.}
\par
Both are solved for $y$, so set them equal:
\begin{align*}
  x^2 - 2x + 1 &= 2x - 3 \\
  x^2 - 4x + 4 &= 0 \\
  (x - 2)^2 &= 0
\end{align*}
The discriminant is $16 - 16 = 0$: one repeated root, $x = 2$. Then
$y = 2(2) - 3 = 1$.
\par
$\ans{(2,\ 1)}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{What the count tells you:} a repeated root means the line touches the
parabola at exactly one point without crossing it --- the line is
\emph{tangent} at $(2, 1)$. Two roots would mean a secant, and no real roots
would mean the line misses the curve entirely.}

\problem{\textbf{Locus equidistant from $(0, 5)$ and the line $y = -5$.}
\par
(a) Model derivation. The distance from $P(x, y)$ to the point is
$\sqrt{x^2 + (y-5)^2}$; the distance to the horizontal line $y = -5$ is the
vertical gap $y + 5$. Setting them equal and squaring:
\begin{align*}
  x^2 + (y - 5)^2 &= (y + 5)^2 \\
  x^2 + y^2 - 10y + 25 &= y^2 + 10y + 25 \\
  x^2 &= 20y
\end{align*}
Every $y^2$ and every constant cancels --- that cancellation is what makes the
locus a parabola rather than a circle. Comparing with $x^2 = 4py$ gives
$p = 5$, which matches the focus $(0, 5)$ and directrix $y = -5$.
\par
(b) Set $y = 5$: $\;x^2 = 20(5) = 100$, so $x = \pm 10$, and the points are
$(-10, 5)$ and $(10, 5)$.
\par
$\ans{x = -10 \text{ or } x = 10}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Manual review:} part (a) is a written derivation, read by a teacher;
part (b) is machine-checked. Credit (a) when the squaring step is shown and the
$y^2$ terms are seen to cancel.}

\problem{\textbf{Circle $x^2 + y^2 = 25$ meets hyperbola $xy = 12$.}
\par
From the hyperbola, $y = \dfrac{12}{x}$ (and $x \neq 0$). Substitute:
\begin{align*}
  x^2 + \frac{144}{x^2} &= 25 \\
  x^4 - 25x^2 + 144 &= 0 \\
  (x^2 - 9)(x^2 - 16) &= 0
\end{align*}
Treat it as a quadratic in $x^2$: $x^2 = 9$ or $x^2 = 16$, so
$x = \pm 3$ or $x = \pm 4$. Each $x$ gives one $y = 12/x$:
\par
$x = 3 \Rightarrow y = 4$; \quad $x = 4 \Rightarrow y = 3$; \quad
$x = -3 \Rightarrow y = -4$; \quad $x = -4 \Rightarrow y = -3$.
\par
$\ans{(3,\ 4),\ (4,\ 3),\ (-3,\ -4),\ (-4,\ -3)}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Sanity check:} $xy = 12$ is positive, so $x$ and $y$ share a sign --- no
intersection can lie in the second or fourth quadrant, and none of the four
does. Multiplying by $x^2$ was safe because $x = 0$ is not on the hyperbola.}

\problem{\textbf{Locus: $PA = 2 \cdot PB$ with $A(6,0)$ and $B(0,0)$.}
\par
Write both distances, then square immediately --- the factor of $2$ must be
squared as well, which is where this problem is usually lost:
\begin{align*}
  \sqrt{(x-6)^2 + y^2} &= 2\sqrt{x^2 + y^2} \\
  (x-6)^2 + y^2 &= 4\left(x^2 + y^2\right) \\
  x^2 - 12x + 36 + y^2 &= 4x^2 + 4y^2 \\
  0 &= 3x^2 + 3y^2 + 12x - 36 \\
  x^2 + y^2 + 4x - 12 &= 0
\end{align*}
Complete the square in $x$: half of $4$ is $2$ and $2^2 = 4$, so
\[ (x + 2)^2 + y^2 = 12 + 4 = 16 \]
\par
$\ans{\text{center } (-2,\ 0), \quad r = 4}$
\par\smallskip
\emph{Worth naming:} ``twice as far from $A$ as from $B$'' produces a circle,
not a line --- the perpendicular bisector only appears when the ratio is $1$.
This family is the Apollonius circle. Sanity check with a point on the
$x$-axis: $P(2,0)$ gives $PA = 4$ and $PB = 2$ $\checkmark$.}

\problem{\textbf{Synthesis --- dish $x^2 = 8y$ and ring $x^2 + y^2 = 20$.}
\par
(a) The dish equation already isolates $x^2$, so substitute it into the ring:
\begin{align*}
  8y + y^2 &= 20 \\
  y^2 + 8y - 20 &= 0 \\
  (y + 10)(y - 2) &= 0
\end{align*}
So $y = 2$ or $y = -10$. Only $y = 2$ is usable: $y = -10$ gives
$x^2 = 8(-10) = -80$, which has no real solution --- the parabola never reaches
below its vertex at the origin. With $y = 2$: $x^2 = 16$, so $x = \pm 4$.
\par
$\ans{(4,\ 2) \text{ and } (-4,\ 2)}$
\par
(b) Take $(4, 2)$. Distance to the focus $(0, 2)$:
\[ \sqrt{(4-0)^2 + (2-2)^2} = \sqrt{16} = 4 \]
Distance to the directrix $y = -2$ is the vertical gap
$2 - (-2) = 4$.
\par
$\ans{4}$
\par
(c) Model answer: the two distances are equal, and that is not a coincidence
about this one point --- it is the definition of the parabola. Every point of
the dish is exactly as far from the focus $(0, 2)$ as it is from the directrix
$y = -2$. That property is why a dish shaped this way sends every signal
arriving parallel to its axis to the same focus point.
\par\smallskip
\emph{Manual review:} part (c) is written reasoning, read by a teacher; parts
(a) and (b) are machine-checked. Credit (c) when the student states the
equal-distance property as holding for \emph{every} point of the curve.}
