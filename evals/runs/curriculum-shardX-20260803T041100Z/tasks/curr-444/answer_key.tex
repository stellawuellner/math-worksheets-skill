\noindent\small\textbf{Two habits this sheet builds.} (1) A sigma sum is the summand
\emph{and} its limits: change one, change the other, and check that the number of terms
survived. (2) The geometric sum formula is a conditional statement --- name $a$ and $r$,
verify $|r| < 1$, and only then divide.\normalsize

\problem{\textbf{Evaluate $\sum_{n=1}^{5}(2n+1)$.}\par
The index runs over the integers from 1 to 5, so there are $5 - 1 + 1 = 5$ terms. Write
them out by substituting each value of $n$ into $2n+1$:
\[ 3 + 5 + 7 + 9 + 11 \]
Pairing the outside terms, $3+11 = 14$ and $5+9 = 14$, leaves $7$ in the middle:
$14 + 14 + 7$.\par
The sum is $\ans{35}$.\par
\textit{Watch for:} 36, which comes from starting at $n = 0$ and picking up the extra
term $1$. The lower limit is part of the definition, not a formality.}

\problem{\textbf{How many terms are in $\sum_{k=4}^{19} a_k$?}\par
Counting the integers from 4 to 19 inclusive is a fencepost count: subtracting gives the
number of \emph{gaps}, and there is always one more post than gap. So add one after
subtracting:
\[ 19 - 4 + 1 \]
The sum has $\ans{16 \text{ terms}}$.\par
\textit{Watch for:} 15, the difference alone. A quick sanity check on a small case
settles it: from 4 to 5 there are clearly 2 terms, not 1.}

\problem{\textbf{Find the mistake: Ravi's shift of $\sum_{n=2}^{7}(n-1)^2$.}\par
(a) Ravi changed the summand but left the limits alone. Substituting $m = n-1$ shifts
\emph{every} index value down by one, so the limits must move too: when $n = 2$, $m = 1$;
when $n = 7$, $m = 6$. His version runs $m$ from 1 to 7, which is 7 terms where the
original has only 6 --- he silently added a term.\par
(b) The correctly shifted sum is
\[ \sum_{n=2}^{7}(n-1)^2 \;=\; \sum_{m=1}^{6} m^2
   \;=\; 1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 + 36 \]
which totals $\ans{91}$.\par
\textit{Check the count first:} original $7 - 2 + 1 = 6$ terms, shifted $6 - 1 + 1 = 6$
terms. Equal counts is the signal that the shift was done correctly, and it is what
rules out Ravi's 140.}

\problem{\textbf{Evaluate $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} 3\left(\frac{2}{5}\right)^{n}$.}\par
Name the pieces before using any formula. The sum starts at $n = 0$, so the first term is
$a = 3\left(\frac{2}{5}\right)^{0} = 3$, and the ratio is $r = \frac{2}{5}$. Since
$\left|\frac{2}{5}\right| < 1$, the series converges and $\frac{a}{1-r}$ applies:
\[ \frac{3}{1 - \frac{2}{5}} = \frac{3}{\frac{3}{5}} \]
The sum is $\ans{5}$.\par
\textit{Watch for:} 2, which comes from using the $n = 1$ term $\frac{6}{5}$ as $a$. The
first term is whatever the \emph{stated} lower limit produces.}

\problem{\textbf{Find the mistake: Tess and $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 4\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^{n}$.}\par
(a) Tess never checked the convergence condition. The formula $\frac{a}{1-r}$ is valid
only when $|r| < 1$; here $r = \frac{3}{2}$ and $\left|\frac{3}{2}\right| > 1$, so the
formula does not apply. Her answer also fails an obvious sanity test: every term is
positive, so no partial sum can be negative, let alone the total.\par
(b) The terms are $6$, $9$, $13.5$, $20.25$, and adding the first four gives
\[ 6 + 9 + 13.5 + 20.25 = \ans{48.75} \]
Each term is larger than the one before, so the partial sums are not settling toward
anything --- they grow without bound.\par
(c) The series \textbf{diverges}: it has no sum. ``Diverges'' is the complete answer here,
and $-8$ is not a wrong sum so much as a number produced by a formula that was never
licensed to run.}

\problem{\textbf{(a) $\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{1}{n}$, and (b) what it does not prove.}\par
(a) As $n$ grows without bound the denominator grows without bound while the numerator
stays fixed, so the quotient can be made smaller than any positive number:
$\ans{0}$.\par
(b) Part (a) is a statement about the \emph{terms}, and convergence of a series is a
statement about the \emph{partial sums}. Terms shrinking to 0 is necessary but not
sufficient: the harmonic series $\sum \frac{1}{n}$ has terms tending to 0 and still
diverges, because the terms do not shrink fast enough (grouping them into blocks of
$2, 4, 8, \ldots$ terms gives blocks each summing to at least $\frac{1}{2}$).\par
So the $n$th-term test can only ever prove \emph{divergence} --- when the terms do
\emph{not} tend to 0. When they do tend to 0, the test is silent and another test is
needed.}

\problem{\textbf{Evaluate $\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{n}$.}\par
The ratio is $r = \frac{2}{3}$ and $\left|\frac{2}{3}\right| < 1$, so the series
converges. The trap is $a$: the sum starts at $n = 2$, so the first term is
$\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^{2} = \frac{4}{9}$, not 1.
\[ \frac{a}{1-r} = \frac{\frac{4}{9}}{1 - \frac{2}{3}}
   = \frac{4}{9}\cdot\frac{3}{1} \]
The sum is $\ans{\dfrac{4}{3}}$.\par
\textit{Watch for:} 3, the answer to the sum that starts at $n = 0$. The formula's $a$ is
always the first term actually included.}

\problem{\textbf{Rewrite $\sum_{n=4}^{12}(n-3)^{3}$ starting at 1, then evaluate.}\par
Substitute $m = n - 3$. Then $n = 4$ gives $m = 1$ and $n = 12$ gives $m = 9$, and the
summand $(n-3)^3$ becomes $m^3$:
\[ \sum_{n=4}^{12}(n-3)^{3} \;=\; \sum_{m=1}^{9} m^{3} \]
Term counts agree: $12 - 4 + 1 = 9$ and $9 - 1 + 1 = 9$. Now use the sum-of-cubes
identity, which is the square of the corresponding triangular number:
\[ \sum_{m=1}^{9} m^{3} = \left(\frac{9 \cdot 10}{2}\right)^{2} = 45^{2} \]
The sum is $\ans{2025}$.\par
\textit{Watch for:} 6084, which is $\sum_{m=1}^{12} m^3$ --- the summand shifted while the
upper limit stayed behind, the same error as problem 3 at a larger scale.}
