\problem{Write the ratio of shaded to unshaded counters three ways (3 shaded, 5
unshaded in the picture).}

\textbf{Solution:} Count the picture: 3 shaded counters and 5 unshaded counters.
The ratio of shaded to unshaded puts the shaded count first. All three forms name
the same comparison: \ans{3\ \text{to}\ 5 \;=\; 3{:}5 \;=\; \tfrac{3}{5}}

\vspace{0.3cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.3cm}

\problem{A fruit bowl holds 4 apples and 7 oranges. Write the ratio of apples to
oranges in colon form and in fraction form.}

\textbf{Solution:} Apples are named first, so 4 goes first in the ratio: 4 apples
to 7 oranges. In both forms the order must stay apples, then
oranges. \ans{4{:}7 \;=\; \tfrac{4}{7}}

\vspace{0.3cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.3cm}

\problem{Shaded and unshaded tiles (5 shaded, 3 unshaded): (a) shaded to all
tiles, (b) unshaded to all tiles.}

\textbf{Solution:} These are part-to-\textbf{whole} ratios, so first find the
whole: $5 + 3 = 8$ tiles in all.

(a) shaded to all tiles: 5 out of 8. \ans{\tfrac{5}{8}}

(b) unshaded to all tiles: 3 out of 8. \ans{\tfrac{3}{8}}

\vspace{0.3cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.3cm}

\problem{Are $6{:}8$ and $9{:}12$ equivalent ratios? Write $=$ or $\neq$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Simplify each ratio by dividing both parts by their greatest
common factor:
\[ \frac{6}{8} = \frac{6 \div 2}{8 \div 2} = \frac{3}{4}
\qquad\qquad
\frac{9}{12} = \frac{9 \div 3}{12 \div 3} = \frac{3}{4} \]
Both simplify to the same ratio, so they are equivalent. \ans{\tfrac{6}{8} = \tfrac{9}{12}}

\vspace{0.3cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.3cm}

\problem{Mr. Lee's class has 12 girls and 15 boys. Write the ratio of girls to
boys as a fraction in simplest form.}

\textbf{Solution:} Girls first: $\frac{12}{15}$. The greatest common factor of 12
and 15 is 3, so divide both parts by 3:
\[ \frac{12}{15} = \frac{12 \div 3}{15 \div 3} \]
which gives the simplest form. \ans{\tfrac{4}{5}}

\vspace{0.3cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.3cm}

\problem{Red and blue marbles in the ratio $3{:}2$, 20 marbles in all. How many
are red?}

\textbf{Solution:} The ratio $3{:}2$ makes $3 + 2 = 5$ equal parts. Divide the
total into 5 parts: $20 \div 5 = 4$ marbles per part. Red gets 3 parts:
$3 \times 4 = 12$. \ans{12\ \text{red marbles}}

\vspace{0.3cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.3cm}

\problem{Fill in the missing value: $\dfrac{5}{8} = \dfrac{?}{40}$.}

\textbf{Solution:} Ask what the denominator was multiplied by: $8 \times 5 = 40$.
Equivalent ratios multiply \textbf{both} parts by the same number, so the
numerator is $5 \times 5 = 25$. Check: $\frac{25}{40} = \frac{5}{8}$
after dividing both parts by 5. \ans{25}

\vspace{0.3cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.3cm}

\problem{A shelter cares for 6 dogs and 10 cats: (a) dogs to cats, (b) cats to
all animals, each in simplest form.}

\textbf{Solution:} (a) Dogs to cats is part-to-part: $\frac{6}{10}$, and dividing
both parts by 2 gives the simplest form. \ans{\tfrac{3}{5}}

(b) Cats to all animals is part-to-whole. The whole is $6 + 10 = 16$ animals, so
the ratio is $\frac{10}{16}$; divide both parts by 2. \ans{\tfrac{5}{8}}

\vspace{0.3cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.3cm}

\problem{Order $5{:}8$, $3{:}4$, $7{:}10$ from least to greatest.}

\textbf{Solution:} Rewrite each ratio as a decimal to compare:
\[ \frac{5}{8} = 0.625 \qquad \frac{3}{4} = 0.75 \qquad \frac{7}{10} = 0.7 \]
Order the decimals from least to greatest: $0.625 < 0.7 < 0.75$, and translate
back to the ratios. \ans{\tfrac{5}{8},\ \ \tfrac{7}{10},\ \ \tfrac{3}{4}}

\vspace{0.3cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.3cm}

\problem{25 students, walk to bus ratio $2{:}3$. Find how many walk, and explain
why the 2 does not mean exactly 2 students.}

\textbf{Solution:} The ratio $2{:}3$ makes $2 + 3 = 5$ equal parts, and
$25 \div 5 = 5$ students per part. Walkers get 2 parts: $2 \times 5 = 10$.
\ans{10\ \text{students walk}}

\textbf{Explanation (instructor judges):} A full-credit explanation says that a
ratio compares the \emph{relative} sizes of the groups, not the exact counts:
out of every 5 students, 2 walk and 3 ride. Since the class holds 5 groups of 5
students, both parts of the ratio scale by 5, giving $2 \times 5 = 10$ walkers,
not 2. Sam read the ratio as a count instead of a comparison.
