\problem{\textbf{7 packs of 2 erasers --- skip-counting by twos.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Decide what one jump is worth before counting. Each pack is a
group of 2, so every jump adds 2.

\textbf{Step 2.} Make 7 jumps: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14. Seven groups of 2 is
$7 \times 2$.
\ans{14}

\textit{Why this is an anchor:} counting by twos never has to be memorised ---
it is just every other number --- so every two-fact is free.}

\problem{\textbf{6 full racks of 10 markers --- skip-counting by tens.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Each ten-frame is one complete group of 10, so the running
total goes up by a whole ten each time rather than one marker at a time.

\textbf{Step 2.} Count the racks: 10, 20, 30, 40, 50, 60. Six groups of 10 is
$6 \times 10$.
\ans{60}

\textit{Why this is an anchor:} a ten-fact is the digit with a zero after it,
which is why tens are the anchor every other fact leans on.}

\problem{\textbf{From $8 \times 5 = 40$ to $8 \times 6$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Compare the two facts before computing anything. Both count
groups of 8; the second one has \emph{one more group}, not one more counter.

\textbf{Step 2.} So $8 \times 6 = 40 + 8$, which is 48.
\ans{48}

\textit{Picture check:} 5 circled groups of 8 dots plus one extra circled group
of 8 --- the drawing shows the single step from the anchor.}

\problem{\textbf{Doubling the two-fact $6 \times 2 = 12$ to get $6 \times 4$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} 4 is two 2s, so six groups of 4 is the same as six groups of 2
counted twice. Doubling the product is legal for exactly that reason.

\textbf{Step 2.} Double the anchor: $12 + 12 = 24$.
\ans{24}

\textit{Watch out:} a student who adds one more 2 gets 14. That step adds a
single counter, but the jump from a two-fact to a four-fact adds a whole second
copy of the picture.}

\problem{\textbf{From the ten-fact $7 \times 10 = 70$ down to $7 \times 9$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Ten groups is one group too many, so start high and remove a
group instead of building nine groups from scratch.

\textbf{Step 2.} One group is worth 7, so $7 \times 9 = 70 - 7 = 63$.
\ans{63}

\textit{Watch out:} subtracting 1 gives 69. The 9 in $7 \times 9$ counts
\emph{groups}, so what comes off is one whole group of 7 --- the difference
between removing a row and removing a dot.}

\problem{\textbf{45 beads at 5 beads per bracelet.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Division asks how many jumps of 5 fit in 45, so count up on the
five-anchor and track the jumps rather than sharing the beads out one by one.

\textbf{Step 2.} 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, 30, 35, 40, 45 --- that is 9 jumps, so
$5 \times 9 = 45$ and $45 \div 5 = 9$.
\ans{9}

\textit{Fact family:} $5 \times 9 = 45$, $9 \times 5 = 45$, $45 \div 5 = 9$,
$45 \div 9 = 5$.}

\problem{\textbf{Splitting $7 \times 7$ into a five-fact and a two-fact.}

\textbf{Step 1.} There is no seven-anchor, so build one: $5 + 2 = 7$ means each
row of 7 splits into a part of 5 and a part of 2, and the whole array splits the
same way.

\textbf{Step 2.} Left part: $7 \times 5 = 35$. Right part: $7 \times 2 = 14$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Put the parts back together: $35 + 14 = 49$.
\ans{49}

\textit{Watch out:} adding only one extra 7 to 35 gives 42, which is
$7 \times 6$. The split was into 5 and 2, so two groups come back, not one.}

\problem{\textbf{Challenge: 56 stickers in rows of 7.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Start from the anchor the student already owns,
$7 \times 5 = 35$, and ask how much of the pile is still unplaced.

\textbf{Step 2.} $56 - 35 = 21$ stickers left over after five rows.

\textbf{Step 3.} Count on by sevens: 42, 49, 56 --- three more jumps, so three
more rows.

\textbf{Step 4.} Altogether $5 + 3 = 8$ rows, and the division fact is
$56 \div 7 = 8$.
\ans{8}

\textit{Check by multiplying:} $7 \times 8 = 56$, so every sticker is used and
no row is short.}
