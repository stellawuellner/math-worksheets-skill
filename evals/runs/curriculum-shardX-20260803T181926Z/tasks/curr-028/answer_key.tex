\problem{\textbf{6 crayons, 2 given away.}

\textbf{Step 1.} This is a take-from story: the crayons that leave are gone, so
the count goes down. Start with 6 and cross out 2.

\textbf{Step 2.} Count only the crayons with no cross on them: 4 crayons remain,
so $6 - 2 = 4$.
\ans{4 \text{ crayons}}

\textit{What to listen for:} the child should say the unit --- ``four crayons''
--- not just the number.}

\problem{\textbf{9 stickers, 4 used.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Take-from again. The 4 stickers Ana uses come off the page of 9.

\textbf{Step 2.} Cross out 4 of the 9 stars and count the rest: $9 - 4 = 5$.
\ans{5 \text{ stickers}}

\textit{Counting-on check:} from 4, count up to 9 --- 5, 6, 7, 8, 9 --- that is
5 counts, the same answer reached the other way.}

\problem{\textbf{7 red cubes and 4 blue cubes: how many more red?}

\textbf{Step 1.} Nothing is taken away here. The two rows are matched one to
one, and the question asks for the leftovers in the longer row.

\textbf{Step 2.} Four red cubes pair off with the four blue cubes. The red cubes
with no partner are the answer: $7 - 4 = 3$.
\ans{3 \text{ more red cubes}}

\textit{Why subtraction fits a comparison:} the matched pairs are removed from
the bigger group in thought, not in fact. The cubes are all still there.}

\problem{\textbf{8 grapes, 3 eaten.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Take-from. Draw 8 dots for the grapes, then cross out 3.

\textbf{Step 2.} The uncrossed dots are the grapes still on the plate:
$8 - 3 = 5$.
\ans{5 \text{ grapes}}

\textit{Watch the question:} it asks what is \emph{still on the plate}, not how
many were eaten.}

\problem{\textbf{9 frogs and 5 ducks: how many fewer ducks?}

\textbf{Step 1.} A comparison, asked in the ``fewer'' direction. Matching a duck
to a frog leaves some frogs unmatched, and that gap is the answer either way you
name it.

\textbf{Step 2.} Bigger group minus smaller group: $9 - 5 = 4$.
\ans{4 \text{ fewer ducks}}

\textit{Same gap, two sentences:} there are 4 fewer ducks than frogs, and also 4
more frogs than ducks. Both describe the same matching.}

\problem{\textbf{10 buttons in a jar, 6 left: how many were taken?}

\textbf{Step 1.} Take-from with the middle of the story missing. The equation is
$10 - \square = 6$, so the unknown is the amount removed.

\textbf{Step 2.} Ask ``10 take away what leaves 6?'' Count back from 10 to 6 ---
9, 8, 7, 6 --- that is 4 counts.
\ans{4 \text{ buttons}}

\textit{Check by putting them back:} $6 + 4 = 10$, the number the jar started
with.}

\problem{\textbf{Sam has 9 shells, which is 3 more than Tia.}

\textbf{Step 1.} A comparison with the smaller group unknown. Tia's shells plus
the 3 extra shells make Sam's 9, so $\square + 3 = 9$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Draw Sam's 9 shells, circle the 3 extra, and count what is left
outside the circle: $9 - 3 = 6$.
\ans{6 \text{ shells}}

\textit{The classic error:} adding 3 to 9. ``More'' in the sentence describes
Sam, not the operation --- Tia is the \emph{smaller} group, so her number must
come out below 9.}

\problem{\textbf{Challenge: 8 apples, 3 eaten, 2 pears.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Part (a) is take-from: the eaten apples leave the bowl, so
$8 - 3 = 5$ apples remain.
\ans{5 \text{ apples}}

\textbf{Step 2.} Part (b) is a comparison, and it uses the answer from part (a)
rather than the starting 8. Match the 2 pears against the 5 apples that are
actually in the bowl now.

\textbf{Step 3.} $5 - 2 = 3$, so three apples have no pear to pair with.
\ans{3 \text{ more apples}}

\textit{Where two-step problems go wrong:} comparing the pears with the original
8 apples gives 6 and ignores the eating. Finish step one, write its answer down,
and only then start step two.}
