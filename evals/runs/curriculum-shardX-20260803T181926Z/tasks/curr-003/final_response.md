# Numerals, Number Words, and How Many — Kindergarten / Grade 1

Three PDFs are ready:

- **`ws_numbermatch_curr003.pdf`** — the student worksheet (4 pages, 8 problems)
- **`ss_numbermatch_curr003.pdf`** — the study guide / skills summary (2 pages)
- **`ak_numbermatch_curr003.pdf`** — the full step-by-step answer key (3 pages)

## What the worksheet covers

Every problem puts two or three *representations of the same number* side by
side and asks the child to connect them:

1. **Count a picture and write the numeral** (problems 1, 5, 8) — two rows of
   buttons, a full ten-frame with three loose dots, and a two-frame picture of
   sixteen.
2. **Match a number word to its numeral** (problems 2, 3, 6) — the word card
   *seven* beside tally marks, a five-frame with a choice of three number words,
   and the word card *twelve* beside a ten-frame and two more.
3. **Compare two amounts shown in different ways** (problems 4, 7) — a printed
   numeral against a picture, and a word card against a ten-frame, with the
   child writing $>$ or $<$.

Problem 8 is the closing challenge and an error-analysis item: Ben wrote $6$ for
a card reading *sixteen* because he counted only the loose dots and ignored the
full ten-frame beside them. The child recounts and writes the numeral that
really matches.

Everything is printed large, the frames are drawn as real five- and ten-frames,
and no grade level appears anywhere on the sheet the child holds.

## What was verified

All eight answers were recomputed independently by SymPy before anything
compiled, and **all eight passed. Nothing is flagged for manual review** — every
task on this sheet has a single checkable answer, including the two
comparison problems, whose $>$ / $<$ answers are checked as relations rather
than numbers.

Three misconception traps were declared and machine-checked as distinguishably
wrong, so those problems really do catch the errors they were built around:
counting a tally bundle as one mark (problem 2), writing a teen number's digits
in the order the word is said — $21$ for *twelve* (problem 6), and ignoring a
full ten-frame (problem 8). Each wrong answer is printed in the key's
common-wrong-answer block, so whoever grades it knows what the mistake was, not
just that there was one.

The study guide's three worked examples and three try-it items were verified the
same way (six more checks, all passed).

## Using them

Give the study guide first. It states the "one number, three ways" idea, then
gives a rule, a worked example and a try-it for counting-and-writing, for teen
number words, and for comparing — with the try-it answers printed upside down
inside their boxes so the child can check without being told.

The answer key is written for the adult: each problem has the counting script to
say out loud ("six, seven, eight…"), a note on what to listen for, and the
common wrong answer. It also opens with a quick-answer bank for fast checking
and a curriculum section listing the standards (K.CC.A.1–K.CC.C.7 and 1.NBT for
the teen-number problems).
