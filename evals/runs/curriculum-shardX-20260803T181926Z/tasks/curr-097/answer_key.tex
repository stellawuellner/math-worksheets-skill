\noindent\small\textbf{What this sheet is teaching, and what to listen for.} A line plot
holds two different kinds of number, and almost every error on this sheet is the two of them
swapped. The numbers \emph{along the bottom} are the thing being counted --- books, or
centimetres. The \textsf{X}'s \emph{above} them are how many times that thing happened. When
a child gives you a wrong answer, ask ``were you counting books or were you counting
children?'' rather than pointing at the plot.\normalsize

\problem{\textbf{Count one stack.} How many children read exactly $4$ books?

\par\smallskip
Put a finger on the $4$ at the bottom of the plot and count straight up. There are three
\textsf{X}'s in that stack, and each \textsf{X} is one child. \ans{3}
\par
\textbf{Say what the answer counts.} The answer is three \emph{children}, not three books.
A child who answers ``4'' has read the label instead of counting the stack --- the single
most common line-plot error, and worth catching on problem 1.}

\problem{\textbf{Find the tallest stack.} Which number of books has the tallest stack?

\par\smallskip
Compare the heights: the stack above $2$ has four \textsf{X}'s, above $3$ has six, above
$4$ has three, above $5$ has two, above $6$ has one. The tallest is the one above
$3$. \ans{3}
\par
That stack holds six \textsf{X}'s, so six children read three books --- more than any other
number of books.
\par
\textbf{Watch the wording.} ``Which number of books'' asks for a label from the bottom of
the plot; ``how many children'' asks for the size of a stack. Both questions point at the
same tall column, and the two answers are different numbers ($3$ and $6$). Ask the child
which one they wrote and why.}

\problem{\textbf{How many children in all?}

\par\smallskip
Every child put exactly one \textsf{X} on the plot, so counting the children means counting
every \textsf{X}. Take the stacks one at a time so nothing gets counted twice:
\par\smallskip
above $2$: four \quad above $3$: six \quad above $4$: three \quad above $5$: two \quad
above $6$: one
\par\smallskip
$4 + 6 + 3 + 2 + 1 = 16$ children in Room 4. \ans{16}
\par
\textbf{A check worth teaching.} If the total comes out bigger than the class, a stack was
counted twice; if it comes out smaller, a stack was missed. Crossing off each stack as it is
added is what stops both.}

\problem{\textbf{Find the shortest stack.} Which number of books was read by the fewest
children?

\par\smallskip
The shortest stack is the one above $6$: it holds a single \textsf{X}. So $6$ books were
read by the fewest children --- exactly one child. \ans{6}
\par
\textbf{The trap is position.} The stack above $2$ is at the far left, and ``fewest'' feels
like it should live at that end. It does not: left-to-right on a line plot is about the size
of the \emph{thing measured}, and up-and-down is about \emph{how many}. Have the child point
at the shortest column rather than the leftmost one.}

\problem{\textbf{How many more?} How many more children read $3$ books than $4$ books?

\par\smallskip
Count the two stacks: above $3$ there are six \textsf{X}'s, above $4$ there are three.
\par
``How many more'' is a subtraction: $6 - 3 = 3$. Three more children read $3$ books than
read $4$ books. \ans{3}
\par
\textbf{If they answered $9$:} they added the stacks instead of comparing them. Adding tells
you how many children are in the two stacks together; subtracting tells you how much taller
one stack is than the other. Line them up with two fingers, one on each stack, and count the
\textsf{X}'s that stick out above the shorter one.}

\problem{\textbf{Finish the line plot.} How many \textsf{X}'s did you draw above $6$?

\par\smallskip
Working through Ms.\ Ruiz's list and crossing off the lengths already drawn ($5$, $7$ and
$8$), what is left is the four $6$'s and the single $10$.
\par
So four new \textsf{X}'s go above the $6$, and one goes above the $10$. \ans{4}
\par
The finished plot has two \textsf{X}'s above $5$, four above $6$, three above $7$, two above
$8$, none above $9$, and one above $10$.
\par
\textbf{How to check a plot a child has built.} Count the \textsf{X}'s on the plot and count
the numbers on the list --- both must come to twelve. If the plot has more, a length was
plotted twice; if it has fewer, one was skipped. That check does not need the answer key.}

\problem{\textbf{How many shells?}

\par\smallskip
Count the \textsf{X}'s, not the numbers along the bottom:
$2 + 4 + 3 + 2 + 0 + 1 = 12$ shells. \ans{12}
\par
This matches the twelve lengths on Ms.\ Ruiz's list, which is exactly the check the plot is
supposed to survive.
\par
\textbf{If they answered $6$:} they counted the marks on the number line --- $5, 6, 7, 8, 9,
10$ --- instead of the \textsf{X}'s. Those six numbers are the lengths that were
\emph{possible}, not the shells that were \emph{found}. Ask how many shells are above the $9$
(none), and the difference between the two counts usually lands.}

\problem{\textbf{Compare two stacks.} How many more shells were $6$ cm long than $5$ cm long?

\par\smallskip
Above $6$ there are four \textsf{X}'s; above $5$ there are two.
\par
$4 - 2 = 2$, so two more shells were $6$ cm long than were $5$ cm long. \ans{2}
\par
\textbf{If they answered $1$:} they subtracted the two lengths ($6 - 5$) instead of the two
stack sizes. The question asks about shells, so both numbers must be counts of \textsf{X}'s.
This is the same swap as problem 1, one step further on, and it is worth naming out loud
every time it appears.}

\problem{\textbf{Longest and shortest.} How much longer was the longest shell than the
shortest one?

\par\smallskip
The furthest-right \textsf{X} sits above $10$, so the longest shell was $10$ cm. The
furthest-left \textsf{X} sits above $5$, so the shortest was $5$ cm.
\par
$10 - 5 = 5$, so the longest shell was five centimetres longer than the shortest.
\ans{5\text{ cm}}
\par
\textbf{This answer is a length, and the unit matters.} Problems 7 and 8 answered in shells;
this one answers in centimetres, because the two numbers being subtracted came from the
bottom of the plot rather than from the stacks.
\par
\textbf{If they answered $6$:} they counted the marks from $5$ to $10$ and included both
ends. Counting marks answers ``how many different lengths could there be'', not ``how much
longer''. Six marks, five steps between them --- walking a finger along the line while
counting the \emph{jumps} makes the difference visible.}

\problem{\textbf{Explain it to Sam.}

\par\smallskip
\textbf{(a)} There are no \textsf{X}'s above the $9$, so the number of shells that were
$9$ cm long is \ans{0}.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{(b)} (Open explanation --- listen to the reason.)
\par
\textbf{A model answer.} ``The empty space above the $9$ does not mean Ms.\ Ruiz forgot it.
It means she measured shells and none of them turned out to be $9$ cm long. Zero is a real
answer. The $9$ has to stay on the line because the numbers have to go up one at a time and
stay evenly spaced --- if you rubbed the $9$ out, the $10$ would sit right next to the $8$
and it would look as though the longest shell was only one centimetre longer than an $8$ cm
one.''
\par
\textbf{Marking guide.} Full credit for any answer that says the gap means \emph{no shells
were that long}, or that the numbers must stay in order and evenly spaced so the gaps are
readable. Partial credit for ``Sam is wrong, there were no $9$ cm shells'' with nothing said
about why the $9$ stays. Not sufficient: ``because it is on the line'' --- true, but it
repeats the plot instead of explaining it.
\par
\textbf{If the child agrees with Sam:} cover the $9$ with a finger and ask them to read the
plot again. Most children immediately say the last shell now looks $9$ cm long, which is the
whole argument.
\par
Open explanation --- $\ans{\text{see model answer}}$.}
