\sstitleblock{Writing Equations from Word Situations}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Start by naming the letter.} Write ``let $n$ be \dots'' with the unit
included. Every later step depends on knowing exactly what the letter counts.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Then sort the numbers into two jobs.} A \emph{rate} happens for each
one of something and is multiplied by the letter. A \emph{one-time amount} (a
fee, a starting value, a coupon) is added or subtracted once.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Read your equation back as a sentence.} If it does not say what the
story says, the model is wrong no matter how neatly it solves.
\end{formulabox}

\skillheading{1. One-step equations from a story}

\begin{formulabox}
A one-step story does one thing to the unknown: equal groups multiply
($rn = \text{total}$), and adding or removing an amount shifts it
($x - a = b$).\\[3pt]
Solve by undoing that single operation on both sides, then check the value in
the original sentence.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Boxes of crayons each hold $8$ crayons. A shipment holds $96$
crayons in all. How many boxes?
\step{Equal boxes means one repeated amount, so the number of boxes multiplies
the size of a box --- a single multiplication step.}
\step{Let $n$ be the number of boxes: $8n = 96$, so $n = 96 \div 8$.}

The shipment holds $\ans{12}$ boxes.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Trays each hold $7$ muffins, and a bakery made $91$ muffins.
How many trays did it fill?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{13}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{2. Two-step equations from a story}

\begin{formulabox}
A two-step story has both jobs at once: a one-time amount and a rate. The
pattern is \emph{one-time amount $+$ rate $\times$ letter $=$ total}.\\[3pt]
Solve in reverse story order: undo the one-time amount first, then undo the
rate. A division story ($\frac{x-a}{b} = c$) is the same idea with the two
operations swapped.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} A bike rental costs $8$ dollars to start plus $4$ dollars an
hour. Dana paid $28$ dollars. For how many hours?
\step{The starting charge is paid once and the hourly charge repeats, so the
rate is the number attached to the letter.}
\step{Let $h$ be the hours: $8 + 4h = 28$, so $4h = 20$ and $h = 20 \div 4$.}

Dana rented the bike for $\ans{5}$ hours.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} A kayak costs $6$ dollars to launch plus $5$ dollars an hour,
and Sam paid $41$ dollars. For how many hours?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{7}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{3. Writing an expression and evaluating it}

\begin{formulabox}
When a story asks for a value rather than an unknown, build the same
one-time-amount-plus-rate rule and then substitute the number given.\\[3pt]
Growing situations add the rate; shrinking ones subtract it. Substitute into
the rule, not into a half-finished computation.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} A printer has already produced $25$ pages and prints $15$
more each minute. How many pages after $6$ minutes?
\step{The finished pages are there from the start and the printing rate repeats
each minute, so the rate multiplies the time and the start is added on.}
\step{Pages after $t$ minutes: $25 + 15t$. At $t = 6$ this is
$25 + 15(6) = 25 + 90$.}

There are $\ans{115}$ pages.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} A tank holds $40$ litres and loses $6$ litres each hour. How
much is left after $5$ hours?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{10}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} the most common modelling error is attaching the letter
to the one-time amount instead of the rate. Test your equation with an easy
number: if the letter is zero, the equation should give the starting amount.
\end{watchoutbox}
