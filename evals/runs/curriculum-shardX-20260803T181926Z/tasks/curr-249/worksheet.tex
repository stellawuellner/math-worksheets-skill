\wstitleblock{Scatter Plots and Bivariate Association --- Does It Show Cause, or Only a Pattern?}{Grade 8 / Pre-Algebra}{}

\noindent\small Two quantities are \textbf{associated} when knowing one tells
you something about the other. That is what a scatter plot can show. It cannot,
by itself, show that one quantity \textbf{causes} the other.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=0.8cm, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item A \textbf{lurking variable} is a third quantity that moves both.
  \item \textbf{Reverse cause} means the arrow may point the other way.
  \item Only a study that \textbf{assigns} the treatment at random can support a
        cause claim.
\end{itemize}

\problem[3.5cm]{\textbf{Graph A.} Read one point off the plot: in the week when
$26$ cartons of ice cream were sold, how many bottles of sunscreen were sold?}

\problem[4cm]{\textbf{Graph A. Find and fix.} Dana is checking how much the
sunscreen sales climb across the summer. She compares the week with $10$ cartons
to the week with $38$ cartons and writes: ``Sunscreen went up by $28$.'' Dana
read the wrong axis. How many more bottles of sunscreen were sold in the
$38$-carton week than in the $10$-carton week?}

\problem[4.5cm]{\textbf{Graph A.} Dana now writes: ``Graph A proves that selling
ice cream causes people to buy sunscreen.'' Name one lurking variable that would
push both sales up together, and write one sentence explaining why an
association on a scatter plot cannot by itself show cause.
\par\vspace*{0.1cm}\noansline}

\problem[4cm]{\textbf{Table B. Find and fix.} Ravi wants the mean score of the
students who used the app. He typed every score in the table into his calculator
and reported a mean of $78.5$. Ravi averaged the wrong set of students. Find the
correct mean score for the group that used the app.}

\problem[4cm]{\textbf{Table B.} Now find the mean score of the group that did
not use the app.}

\problem[5cm]{\textbf{Table B.} The app's advertisement says: ``Our app raises
test scores by eleven points.'' Using the two means you just found, explain why
this study cannot support that claim --- name one way the two groups could
differ apart from the app --- and describe one change to how the study was run
that would let it support a cause claim.
\par\vspace*{0.1cm}\noansline}

\problem[4cm]{\textbf{Graph C. Find and fix.} Sam says, ``Watching television
makes you lose sleep,'' and reports the size of the effect as $5$ by subtracting
the television hours. Sam used the wrong axis again. Going from the student who
watched $1$ hour to the student who watched $6$ hours, what is the change in
hours of sleep? Write it as a signed number.}

\problem[5.5cm]{\textbf{Challenge --- Graph C.} Rewrite Sam's headline so that it
claims only what Graph C actually shows. Then describe the experiment a
researcher would have to run to test whether television time really causes sleep
loss --- say who is assigned what, and how the assignment is made.
