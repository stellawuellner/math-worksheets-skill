Here are three PDFs on representing teen numbers as one ten and some ones — an
8-problem worksheet, a full step-by-step answer key, and a two-page study
guide.

**Worksheet** (`ws_teennumbers_curr031.pdf`, 3 pages). Every problem is built
on the ten-frame model and each one connects the model to the notation:

- Problems 1 and 2: the numeral is given (13, 16) with a full ten-frame and an
  empty row of boxes beside it. The child fills in how many ones are left once
  the ten is set aside. The empty boxes are there to draw in — they are
  support, not a graded response.
- Problems 3 and 4: the model is described in words (one full frame plus 4, then
  8, loose counters) and the child writes the number. This is the same idea run
  backwards, which is where the "count on from ten" habit gets built.
- Problems 5 and 6: a picture only — a full frame plus loose dots — and the
  child writes the number it shows (15, 19). Problem 6 uses nine ones, the
  largest teen, so the next counter would start a second ten.
- Problem 7: compare 14 and 12, then say why the loose counters and not the
  frames decide it.
- Problem 8: a find-and-fix. Nia says "17 is one ten and seventeen ones."
  The child writes the correct number of ones and explains the double count.

Ruled lines are provided for the two written explanations. No grade level is
printed anywhere a child sees it.

**Answer key** (`ak_teennumbers_curr031.pdf`, 2 pages). Every problem is
restated and worked, and the reasoning is deliberately the same two moves each
time — "take the ten out first" going one way, "start the count at ten and
count on" going the other — so a parent can reuse one explanation. A Quick
Answers strip and a Curriculum block (1.NBT) sit at the top.

**Study guide** (`ss_teennumbers_curr031.pdf`, 2 pages). Three sections:
breaking a teen number into ten and ones, building one from ten and ones, and
comparing two teen numbers. Each has a rule box, a worked example, and a try-it
whose answer prints upside down inside the box. A watch-out box flags the
commonest error — recounting the ten that the full frame already stands for.

**How much of this is machine-checked.** 6 of the 8 problems are fully verified
with SymPy: each decomposition (13 → 3 ones, 16 → 6 ones, 17 → 7 ones) and each
composition (10 + 4, 10 + 8, 10 + 5, 10 + 9) was recomputed and matched against
the printed key, as was the comparison symbol in problem 7. The remaining 2
responses are the "explain" halves of problems 7 and 8. Those are genuinely
open, so they are flagged rather than claimed as verified: the Quick Answers
strip prints `---` for them, the "What is verified" note names problems 7 and
8, and the key gives a rubric for each saying what earns full, half and no
credit. The numeric halves of both (`14 > 12` and 7 ones) are machine-checked
like the rest.
