\aktitleblock{Statistical Questions and How Spread Out the Data Is}{Grade 6--7}{}
\input{qa_r_curr196}

\noindent\small\textbf{What this sheet is teaching.} Two ideas, and the second is the one
students skip. First, a question is statistical when its answers vary --- which is a
property of the \emph{answers}, not of the topic, so ``How tall is Priya?'' is not
statistical even though it is about height. Second, a distribution needs two numbers to
describe it, a centre and a spread, and the sheet ends by putting two classes with almost
identical means side by side so the student can see what the mean alone leaves
out.\normalsize

\problem{\textbf{Not every question is statistical.} Answers to ``How tall is Priya?'':
$148, 148, 148, 148$.

\par\smallskip
Largest $148$, smallest $148$, so the range is $148 - 148 = \ans{0}$.
\par
Every dot sits in one stack. The answers do not vary at all, so this is \textbf{not} a
statistical question --- Priya has one height, and asking four people cannot change that.
\par
\textbf{The point.} Being about a measurable quantity is not enough. Variability in the
answers is the whole test, and a range of zero is what ``no variability'' looks like as a
number.}

\problem{\textbf{Now change one word.} Answers to ``How tall are the students in our
class?'': $140, 146, 151, 155, 162$.

\par\smallskip
Largest $162$, smallest $140$, so the range is $162 - 140 = \ans{22}$ cm.
\par
The dots spread right across the number line. The answers vary, so this \textbf{is} a
statistical question.
\par
\textbf{The word that changed.} ``Priya'' became ``the students in our class'' --- a single
individual became a group. A statistical question asks about a group, precisely because a
group is what produces a spread of answers.}

\problem{\textbf{Sort the four questions.}

\par\smallskip
\begin{center}
\renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.3}
\begin{tabular}{|l|c|c|}
\hline
\textbf{question} & \textbf{range} & \textbf{statistical?} \\ \hline
(a) How old is our teacher? & $\ans{0}$ & no \\ \hline
(b) How old are the students in Grade 6? & $\ans{2}$ & yes \\ \hline
(c) How many pets does Sam have? & $\ans{0}$ & no \\ \hline
(d) How many pets do the students in our class have? & $\ans{5}$ & yes \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\par
\textbf{The rule in the student's own words} should be something like: ``if the answers can
be different from each other, it is statistical''. Both of the non-statistical questions
name \emph{one} person; both of the statistical ones name a \emph{group}.
\par
\textbf{Worth noticing about (b).} Its range is only $2$ years --- small, but not zero. A
question does not have to produce a big spread to be statistical; it only has to produce
some.}

\problem{\textbf{Class A: find the centre.} Data: $15, 20, 20, 25, 30, 30, 35, 40, 45, 60$.

\par\smallskip
Sum: $15 + 20 + 20 + 25 + 30 + 30 + 35 + 40 + 45 + 60 = \ans{320}$.
\par
Ten values, so the mean is $320 \div 10 = \ans{32}$ minutes.
\par
\textbf{A useful sanity check.} Only three of the ten students spent more than $32$
minutes, yet $32$ is the ``average''. That is the single value of $60$ pulling the mean
upward, and it is the reason problem 6 asks for the median as well.}

\problem{\textbf{Class A: find the range.}

\par\smallskip
Largest $\ans{60}$, smallest $\ans{15}$, so the range is $60 - 15 = \ans{45}$ minutes.
\par
\textbf{What the range adds.} The mean says a typical night was about half an hour. The
range says the class's experience of that same homework ran from a quarter of an hour to a
full hour --- a four-fold difference between the fastest and the slowest student. A teacher
setting homework needs the second fact at least as much as the first.}

\problem{\textbf{Class A: find the median --- carefully.}

\par\smallskip
\textbf{What Sam did.} Sam took the middle of the list \emph{as written} rather than of the
sorted values, and landed on $35$. The median is defined on sorted data; without sorting,
the ``middle of the list'' is just whichever value happened to be typed fifth.
\par
Sorted: $15, 20, 20, 25, \underline{30}, \underline{30}, 35, 40, 45, 60$. With ten values
the median is halfway between the 5th and the 6th:
\par
5th $= \ans{30}$, \quad 6th $= 30$, \quad median $= \dfrac{30 + 30}{2} = \ans{30}$ minutes.
\par
\textbf{Mean $32$, median $30$.} The gap between them is small here but it points the right
way: the mean sits above the median because the distribution has a tail to the right. Ask
the student which of the two they would quote to a parent who wanted to know what a
``normal'' night looks like.}

\problem{\textbf{Class A: the middle half.}

\par\smallskip
Sorted, split into halves of five:
\[ \underbrace{15,\ 20,\ \boxed{20},\ 25,\ 30}_{\text{lower half}} \quad\ \big|\ \quad
   \underbrace{30,\ 35,\ \boxed{40},\ 45,\ 60}_{\text{upper half}} \]
\par
$Q_1 = \ans{20}$ (median of the lower half), \quad $Q_3 = \ans{40}$ (median of the upper
half).
\par
$\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1 = 40 - 20 = \ans{20}$ minutes.
\par
\textbf{Why bother, when the range was easier?} The range of $45$ is decided entirely by
the one student who spent $60$ minutes. Delete that student and the range collapses to
$30$; the IQR does not move at all, because the middle five values are untouched. The IQR
describes the bulk of the class, the range describes its extremes, and both are honest
answers to different questions.}

\problem{\textbf{Class B, and a comparison.} Data:
$28, 30, 30, 32, 32, 33, 34, 35, 36, 40$.

\par\smallskip
\textbf{Range.} Largest $40$, smallest $28$: range $= 40 - 28 = \ans{12}$ minutes.
\par
\textbf{Quartiles.} Lower half $28, 30, 30, 32, 32$ gives $Q_1 = 30$; upper half
$33, 34, 35, 36, 40$ gives $Q_3 = 35$. So
$\text{IQR} = 35 - 30 = \ans{5}$ minutes.
\par
\textbf{The comparison.} Class A's range is $45$ and Class B's is $12$, a difference of
$45 - 12 = \ans{33}$ minutes.
\par
\textbf{Which class is easier to predict?} Class B, and the numbers say why: half of Class
B sits inside a five-minute window, while half of Class A is spread over twenty minutes. If
you had to guess how long one more student would take, you would be far more often right
about Class B.}

\problem{\textbf{One student picked at random.}

\par\smallskip
The median is $30$ minutes. Strictly more than $30$: the values $35, 40, 45, 60$ ---
$\ans{4}$ students out of $\ans{10}$.
\[ P(\text{more than } 30 \text{ minutes}) = \frac{4}{10} = \ans{\tfrac{2}{5}} \]
\par
\textbf{Why it is not exactly one half.} Two students landed exactly \emph{on} $30$, and
``more than $30$'' excludes them. With repeated values a median does not have to split the
data into two equal groups --- a detail worth surfacing, because students often assume the
answer to this kind of question must be $\tfrac{1}{2}$.}

\problem{\textbf{Write to the teacher.} (Open explanation --- read the reasoning.)

\par\smallskip
\textbf{The facts to be used.} Class A: mean $32$, range $45$, IQR $20$. Class B: mean $33$,
range $12$, IQR $5$. The centres differ by one minute; the spreads differ by a factor of
about four.
\par
\textbf{A model answer.} ``Both classes averaged about half an hour, so on the mean alone
they look identical. But Class A's homework times ran from 15 minutes to an hour --- a
range of 45 --- while Class B's ran from 28 to 40, a range of only 12. The middle half of
Class A covers 20 minutes and the middle half of Class B covers 5. So in Class B almost
everyone had a similar night, and in Class A some students finished in a quarter of an hour
while others spent four times that. I would report the median and the IQR for each class,
not just the mean, because the mean hides which students the homework is actually hard
for.''
\par
\textbf{Marking guide.} Full credit needs a spread measure quoted with its number and used
to separate the two classes. Partial credit for saying the classes differ ``in how spread
out they are'' without quoting a range or IQR. Not sufficient: repeating that the means are
$32$ and $33$, or saying Class A ``has an outlier'' without saying what that does to the
description.
\par
\textbf{A better-than-asked-for answer} notices that the two classes might need different
responses from the teacher: Class B's homework is well calibrated, while Class A contains
students for whom the same task takes four times as long.
\par
Open explanation --- $\ans{\text{open response}}$.}
