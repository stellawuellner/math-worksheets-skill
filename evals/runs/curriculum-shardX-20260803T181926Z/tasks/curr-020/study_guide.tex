\sstitleblock{Making Ten}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Ten is made of two parts.} A full ten-frame holds $10$. The dots you
see and the empty squares are the two parts.\\[3pt]
\textbf{The partners of ten:} \quad $0$ and $10$ \quad $1$ and $9$ \quad
$2$ and $8$ \quad $3$ and $7$ \quad $4$ and $6$ \quad $5$ and $5$.\\[3pt]
Learn these six pairs and every question on the worksheet becomes a lookup, not
a count.
\end{formulabox}

\skillheading{1. Finding the missing part of ten}

\begin{formulabox}
Fill the frame in your head. Count the \emph{empty} squares --- do not start
back at one. A full top row is $5$, so anything past $5$ has only the bottom
row left to fill.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad $7 + \underline{\hspace{0.9cm}} = 10$
\step{The whole is ten and one part is given, so the question is asking for the
partner of seven --- fill the frame, do not recount it.}
\step{Seven dots fill the top row and two of the bottom row, so three squares
are still empty.}

$\Rightarrow\quad \ans{3}$, because $7 + 3 = 10$.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad $4 + \underline{\hspace{0.9cm}} = 10$\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{6}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{2. Taking a part away from ten}

\begin{formulabox}
This is the same picture read backwards. If you take one part away from ten,
what is left is the \emph{other} part. So if you know the partners of ten, you
already know every subtraction from ten --- no counting back needed.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad $10 - 6 = \underline{\hspace{0.9cm}}$
\step{Ten is the whole and six is one part, so the answer is six's partner ---
the same pair as before, used the other way round.}
\step{Six dots leave four empty squares on the frame.}

$\Rightarrow\quad \ans{4}$, because $6 + 4 = 10$.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad $10 - 2 = \underline{\hspace{0.9cm}}$\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{8}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{3. Using ten to add a bigger number}

\begin{formulabox}
Adding to ten is easy, so \emph{make} a ten first. Fill the frame from the
second number, then count on with whatever is left over. Break the second
number into two pieces: the piece that finishes the ten, and the rest.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} \quad $9 + 5$
\step{Nine is nearly ten, so filling the frame first turns a hard fact into an
easy one.}
\step{Nine needs one more, so break the five into $1$ and $4$: \ $9 + 1 = 10$,
then $10 + 4$.}

$\Rightarrow\quad \ans{14}$
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} \quad $8 + 5$ \quad (how many does $8$ need to make ten?)\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{13}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} when you make a ten, do not forget the leftovers. If
you move counters into the frame, check your hand --- is it empty? Stopping at
$10$ is the most common mistake on the last few problems.
\end{watchoutbox}
