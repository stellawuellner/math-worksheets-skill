\sstitleblock{Solving Exponential Equations by Writing a Common Base}

\skillheading{Skill 1 --- Equating Exponents When the Bases Match}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{The rule:} for $b > 0$ with $b \neq 1$, the function $b^{u}$ never repeats
a value, so $b^{\,u} = b^{\,v}$ exactly when $u = v$. Matching bases is what
\emph{licenses} dropping them --- with unequal bases the step is illegal.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Method:} confirm the two bases are identical, set the exponents equal,
solve that ordinary equation, then substitute back into both exponents to check.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:}\quad Solve $2^{\,x-3} = 2^{\,2x-7}$.
\step{Both sides are powers of the same base 2, so the equation is true exactly when the two exponents agree --- no logarithm is needed.}
\step{Set $x - 3 = 2x - 7$; subtracting $x$ gives $-3 = x - 7$, so $x = \ans{4}$. Check: both exponents equal 1, and $2^{1} = 2^{1}$.}
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:}\quad Solve $7^{\,4x} = 7^{\,x+6}$.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{x = 2}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{Skill 2 --- Rewriting Both Sides Over a Common Base}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{When the bases differ,} look for one base that generates both:
$4 = 2^{2}$, $8 = 2^{3}$, $16 = 2^{4}$, $32 = 2^{5}$, $64 = 2^{6}$;
$9 = 3^{2}$, $27 = 3^{3}$, $81 = 3^{4}$; $25 = 5^{2}$, $125 = 5^{3}$.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Two laws do all the work:} $(b^{m})^{n} = b^{mn}$ --- a power of a power
\emph{multiplies} exponents --- and $b^{m}b^{n} = b^{m+n}$ --- a product
\emph{adds} them. A reciprocal is a negative exponent:
$\tfrac{1}{2} = 2^{-1}$, $\tfrac{1}{16} = 2^{-4}$.\\[3pt]
Rewrite, then equate exponents as in Skill 1. A fractional answer is normal.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:}\quad Solve $8^{\,x} = 32$.
\step{Neither side is a power of the other, but 8 and 32 are both powers of 2, so base 2 is the common ground that lets the exponents be compared.}
\step{Rewrite: $\left(2^{3}\right)^{x} = 2^{5}$, that is $2^{\,3x} = 2^{5}$. Equating exponents gives $3x = 5$, so $x = \ans{\tfrac{5}{3}}$.}
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:}\quad Solve $16^{\,x} = 8^{\,x+2}$.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{x = 6}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{Skill 3 --- Exponential Models with a Common Base}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{The shape:} a growth or decay model looks like $A = A_0\,b^{\,t/k}$ ---
starting amount $A_0$, growth factor $b$, one factor applied every $k$ units of
time.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Method:} divide by the starting amount \emph{first}, so the power stands
alone; the coefficient is not part of the exponent and cannot be rewritten with
it. Then write the isolated number as a power of the same base and equate
exponents. Finally read the exponent as a count: $t/k = 4$ means four doublings
or four half-lives have passed.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Its limit:} this works only when the isolated ratio is a whole power of
the base. It is not, logarithms are the general tool.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:}\quad A colony of 3 cells doubles hourly: $N = 3\cdot 2^{\,t}$. When are there 96 cells?
\step{The 3 is a starting count, not part of the power, so it must be divided out before the two sides can be compared as powers of 2.}
\step{From $3\cdot 2^{\,t} = 96$, divide by 3 to get $2^{\,t} = 32 = 2^{5}$, so $t = \ans{5}$ hours --- five doublings.}
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:}\quad A 400 mg sample halves every 3 years:
$M = 400\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{t/3}$. When is 25 mg left?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{t = 12}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} $\left(2^{3}\right)^{x}$ is $2^{3x}$, not $2^{3+x}$ --- a
power of a power multiplies. And never divide the exponents themselves: from
$2^{4x} = 2^{3x+6}$ the next line is $4x = 3x + 6$, not $4x/3x = 6$.
\end{watchoutbox}
