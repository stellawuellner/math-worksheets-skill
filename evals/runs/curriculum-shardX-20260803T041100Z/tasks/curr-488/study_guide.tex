\sstitleblock{Volumes by Known Cross Sections and Shells}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Known cross sections:} $V = \int_a^b A(x)\,dx$ --- slice the solid,
write the AREA of one slice as a function of $x$, integrate. Slice areas:
square $A = s^2$; \ semicircle on diameter $d$: $A = \frac{\pi}{8}d^2$;
\ equilateral triangle of side $s$: $A = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{4}s^2$.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Cylindrical shells} (about the $y$-axis):
$V = \int_a^b 2\pi\,(\text{radius})(\text{height})\,dx$, radius $= x$,
height $=$ the vertical extent of the region at $x$.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Which method?} Revolving about the $y$-axis while the region is
described as $y = f(x)$ is the shell case --- shells integrate in $x$, so
$f$ never has to be inverted. Washers in $y$ would need $x$ as a function of
$y$ first.
\end{formulabox}

\skillheading{1. Solids with known cross sections}

\begin{formulabox}
The base region only supplies the \emph{length} of one side of each slice; the
named shape turns that length into an area. Write $s$ in terms of $x$ first,
then square it (every one of the three area formulas squares the side), then
integrate over the $x$-range of the base.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} The base is the region under $y = x$ from $x = 0$ to
$x = 3$. Cross sections perpendicular to the $x$-axis are squares. Find the
volume.
\step{The slice area is what has to be integrated, and here the base gives the
side of a square directly --- the height of the region at $x$.}
\step{$s = x$, so $A(x) = x^2$ and
$V = \int_0^3 x^2\,dx = \left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^3 = \frac{27}{3}$.}
The volume is $\ans{9}$ cubic units.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Same set-up with the base under $y = x$ from $x = 0$ to
$x = 2$ and square cross sections. Find the volume.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{\tfrac{8}{3}}$ cubic units}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{2. Cylindrical shells}

\begin{formulabox}
Picture a thin vertical strip of the region at $x$. When the region turns about
the $y$-axis the strip sweeps a hollow cylinder: circumference $2\pi x$, height
$f(x)$, thickness $dx$. Multiply the three and integrate. The $2\pi$ is part of
the model, not decoration --- dropping it is the most common shell error.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} The region under $y = x$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 2$ is revolved
about the $y$-axis. Find the volume by shells.
\step{The axis of revolution is the $y$-axis and the region is given as
$y$ in terms of $x$, so shells in $x$ need no inversion.}
\step{Radius $x$, height $x$, so
$V = \int_0^2 2\pi x \cdot x\,dx = 2\pi\left[\frac{x^3}{3}\right]_0^2
= 2\pi \cdot \frac{8}{3}$.}
The volume is $\ans{\tfrac{16\pi}{3}}$ cubic units.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} The region under $y = x^2$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 2$ is revolved
about the $y$-axis. Find the volume by shells.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{8\pi}$ cubic units}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{3. Choosing and justifying the set-up}

\begin{formulabox}
Ask three questions in order: what is the axis of revolution, how is the region
described, and does a strip parallel to the axis reach across the whole region?
Strips \emph{parallel} to the axis give shells; strips \emph{perpendicular} to
it give discs or washers. If one choice forces you to invert the function, the
other choice is usually the shorter route to the same number.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} The region under $y = x^2$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 1$ is
revolved about the $y$-axis. Choose a method, justify it, and find the volume.
\step{The axis is the $y$-axis and the boundary is given as $y = x^2$; a
vertical strip is parallel to that axis, so shells in $x$ apply and no
inversion to $x = \sqrt{y}$ is needed.}
\step{$V = \int_0^1 2\pi x \cdot x^2\,dx
= 2\pi\left[\frac{x^4}{4}\right]_0^1 = \frac{2\pi}{4}$.}
The volume is $\ans{\tfrac{\pi}{2}}$ cubic units.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} The region under $y = x^3$ from $x = 0$ to $x = 1$ is revolved
about the $y$-axis. Use shells.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{\tfrac{2\pi}{5}}$ cubic units}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} for cross sections, integrate the \emph{area} of a
slice, never its side length --- and check whether the base segment is the
diameter or the radius of a semicircle, since mistaking one for the other
changes every slice by a factor of four. For shells, keep the $2\pi$ and use
the gap between the two boundary curves as the height when the region is
bounded above and below.
\end{watchoutbox}
