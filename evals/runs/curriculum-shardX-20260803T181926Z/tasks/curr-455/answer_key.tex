\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 3}(x^2-2x+5)$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Substitution is only legal when the function is continuous
there, so the first move is to notice that this is a polynomial --- continuous
everywhere.

\textbf{Step 2.} Substituting: $3^2-2(3)+5=9-6+5$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The limit is $\ans{8}$.

\textit{Why substitution is allowed:} for a continuous function the limit at a
point equals the value there. That sentence is the whole justification.}

\problem{\textbf{Table for $f(x)=\dfrac{x^2-9}{x-3}$ near $x=3$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The expression is undefined at $3$, so no single substitution
answers the question; the table has to approach from both sides.

\textbf{Step 2.} For $x\neq 3$ the fraction cancels to $x+3$, which is what
makes the cells easy to check.

\par\medskip
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
$x$ & $2.9$ & $2.999$ & $3.001$ & $3.1$ \\ \hline
$f(x)$ & $\ans{5.9}$ & $\ans{5.999}$ & $\ans{6.001}$ & $\ans{6.1}$ \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\par\medskip

\textbf{Step 3.} The values close in on the same number from both sides, so
$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 3}f(x)=\ans{6}$.

\textit{Teaching note:} the table never reaches $6$ --- and it does not have to.
A limit is what the values head toward, not a value the function attains.}

\problem{\textbf{The graph of $g$ at $x=1$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Limit and value are separate questions, so read the curve
first and the dot second.

\textbf{Step 2.} Coming in from the left along $y=x+1$ the curve heads for
height $2$; coming in from the right along the constant branch it is already at
height $2$. Both sides agree, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1}g(x)=\ans{2}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} The filled dot sits at $(1,4)$, so $g(1)=4$. The limit exists
but does not equal the value, which is a \textbf{removable} discontinuity ---
redefining $g(1)=2$ would repair it.

\textbf{Step 4 (algebraic confirmation).} On the left branch $g(x)=x+1$, so
$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1^-}g(x)=1+1=2$, matching the graph.

\textit{Listen for:} ``the limit is $4$ because that is the point.'' The open
circle, not the filled dot, is where the curve is going.}

\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2}\dfrac{x^2-4}{x^2-x-2}$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Substitution gives $\tfrac{0}{0}$, which is not a value but a
signal that numerator and denominator share the factor $x-2$.

\textbf{Step 2.} Factor both: $\dfrac{(x-2)(x+2)}{(x-2)(x+1)}$. For $x\neq 2$
the common factor cancels, leaving $\dfrac{x+2}{x+1}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} That expression is continuous at $2$, so substitute:
$\dfrac{4}{3}$, giving $\ans{\frac{4}{3}}$.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} ``does not exist'' --- $\tfrac00$ was read as a
verdict instead of as an instruction to factor.}

\problem{\textbf{Table for $f(x)=\dfrac{x^3-8}{x-2}$ near $x=2$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Again $\tfrac00$ at $x=2$, so build the numerical evidence
first and then settle it with algebra.

\textbf{Step 2.} The difference of cubes factors as
$x^3-8=(x-2)(x^2+2x+4)$, so for $x\neq 2$ the function is $x^2+2x+4$.

\par\medskip
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
$x$ & $1.9$ & $1.99$ & $2.01$ & $2.1$ \\ \hline
$f(x)$ & $\ans{11.41}$ & $\ans{11.9401}$ & $\ans{12.0601}$ & $\ans{12.61}$ \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\par\medskip

\textbf{Step 3.} The cells bracket the same number from both sides, and
substituting into $x^2+2x+4$ confirms it: $4+4+4$, so the limit is $\ans{12}$.

\textit{Teaching note:} this is the pattern to internalise --- the table says
\emph{about} $12$, the algebra says \emph{exactly} $12$.}

\problem{\textbf{The graph of $g$ at $x=3$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} When the two sides of a graph arrive at different heights the
one-sided limits must be read separately.

\textbf{Step 2.} From the left the constant branch is at height $2$, so
$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 3^-}g(x)=2$. From the right the falling branch starts
at the open circle at height $3$, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 3^+}g(x)=3$.

\textbf{Step 3.} A two-sided limit exists only when the one-sided limits are
equal. Here $2\neq 3$, so $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 3}g(x)$ does not exist --- a
\textbf{jump} discontinuity.

\textbf{Step 4 (algebraic confirmation).} On the right branch $g(x)=6-x$, so
$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 3^+}g(x)=6-3$, which is $\ans{3}$ as the graph showed.

\textit{Note:} $g(3)=2$ is defined --- a jump discontinuity does not require a
hole in the domain.}

\problem{\textbf{$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 0}\dfrac{\frac{1}{x+2}-\frac{1}{2}}{x}$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The compound fraction hides the cancelling factor, so combine
the numerator over a common denominator before touching the outer division.

\textbf{Step 2.} $\dfrac{1}{x+2}-\dfrac{1}{2}
=\dfrac{2-(x+2)}{2(x+2)}=\dfrac{-x}{2(x+2)}$.

\textbf{Step 3.} Dividing by $x$ means multiplying by $\tfrac1x$:
$\dfrac{-x}{2x(x+2)}=\dfrac{-1}{2(x+2)}$ for $x\neq 0$.

\textbf{Step 4.} That is continuous at $0$, so substitute:
$\dfrac{-1}{2(2)}$, giving $\ans{-\frac{1}{4}}$.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $-\tfrac12$ --- the $x$ in the outer denominator
was cancelled twice.}

\problem{\textbf{Choosing $a$ so that $f(x)=\dfrac{x^2+ax+3}{x-1}$ has a finite
limit at $1$.}

\textbf{Step 1 (a).} The denominator goes to $0$ at $x=1$. A quotient with a
nonzero numerator over $0$ blows up, so a finite limit forces the numerator to
vanish at $1$ as well.

\textbf{Step 2 (a).} Setting the numerator to zero at $x=1$:
$1+a+3=0$, so $\ans{a=-4}$.

\textbf{Step 3 (b).} With $a=-4$ the numerator is $x^2-4x+3=(x-1)(x-3)$, so for
$x\neq 1$ the function equals $x-3$.

\textbf{Step 4 (b).} Substituting, $1-3$, so
$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 1}f(x)=\ans{-2}$.

\textit{Check:} any other $a$ leaves a nonzero constant over a vanishing
denominator, and the one-sided limits run off to $+\infty$ and $-\infty$.}

\problem{\textbf{Table for $f(x)=\dfrac{|x-4|}{x-4}$ near $x=4$.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Absolute value is a two-case rule, so the sign of $x-4$ has to
be tracked separately on each side.

\textbf{Step 2.} For $x<4$, $|x-4|=-(x-4)$ and the quotient is $-1$; for $x>4$,
$|x-4|=x-4$ and the quotient is $1$.

\par\medskip
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|c|c|c|c|c|}
\hline
$x$ & $3.9$ & $3.99$ & $4.01$ & $4.1$ \\ \hline
$f(x)$ & $\ans{-1}$ & $\ans{-1}$ & $\ans{1}$ & $\ans{1}$ \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\par\medskip

\textbf{Step 3.} Both one-sided limits exist --- $-1$ on the left, $1$ on the
right --- but a two-sided limit requires them to be equal, and they are not.
So $\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 4}f(x)$ does not exist.

\textit{Grading note (manual):} full credit needs the reason, not just ``DNE'':
the values do not settle on one number because the two sides settle on
different ones.}

\problem{\textbf{Synthesis: $p(x)=\dfrac{x^2+x-6}{x-2}$.}

\textbf{Step 1 (a).} For $x\neq 2$ the numerator factors as $(x-2)(x+3)$, so
$p(x)=x+3$ everywhere except at $2$ --- which is what makes the table cells
one-step evaluations.

\textbf{Step 2 (a).} $p(1.9)=\ans{4.9}$ and $p(2.1)=\ans{5.1}$.

\textbf{Step 3 (b).} Since $p(x)=x+3$ near $2$,
$\displaystyle\lim_{x\to 2}p(x)=2+3=\ans{5}$. Defining $p(2)=5$ removes the
discontinuity, because then the value equals the limit.

\textbf{Step 4 (c).} The sketch is the line $y=x+3$ with an open circle at
$(2,5)$ --- one point punched out of a straight line, nothing else.

\textit{Grading note (manual):} the written explanation should say that the
table \emph{suggests} $5$, the algebra \emph{proves} it, and the sketch
\emph{shows} why the function still fails to be continuous until $p(2)$ is
defined to be that number. Any two of the three without the third is a partial
answer.}
