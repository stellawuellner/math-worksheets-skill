\aktitleblock{Series Convergence --- Geometric, $p$-Series, and the $n$th-Term Test}{AP Calculus BC}{}
\input{qa_r_curr496}

\noindent\small\textbf{How to grade this sheet:} on the AP exam these questions are scored
on the \emph{justification}, not the verdict. A correct ``diverges'' with no named test and
no parameter earns nothing. Look for three things in every answer: the family (geometric,
$p$-series, neither), the parameter that decides it ($r$ or $p$) with its comparison to
$1$, and the conclusion stated as a consequence of that comparison. The one place students
lose the most is problem 6 and problem 8 --- the belief that terms shrinking to zero is
enough.\normalsize

\problem{\textbf{A geometric series, read off directly.}\par
\textbf{(a)} Write the series with its index starting where the problem starts it, $n = 0$:
the first term is the $n = 0$ term, $a = 3\left(\tfrac{2}{5}\right)^{0} = 3$, and the ratio
of consecutive terms is $r = \tfrac{2}{5}$.\par
\textbf{(b)} It is geometric with $|r| = \tfrac{2}{5} < 1$, so the geometric series test
applies and the series converges.\par
\textbf{(c)} Use $S = \dfrac{a}{1 - r}$ with those two values:
\[ S = \frac{3}{1 - \tfrac{2}{5}} = \frac{3}{\tfrac{3}{5}} = 3 \cdot \frac{5}{3} \]
So the sum is $\ans{5}$.\par
\textit{Check it against Model A:} the running totals on the front page are $3$, $4.2$,
$4.68$, $4.872$, $4.9488$ --- climbing toward $5$ and never passing it. A student whose
formula gives a number smaller than the first term has inverted the fraction.}

\problem{\textbf{The $n$th-term test, stated correctly.}\par
\textbf{(a)} Divide numerator and denominator by the highest power of $n$ present, here
$n$ itself:
\[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{n}{2n + 5}
   = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{2 + \tfrac{5}{n}} = \frac{1}{2 + 0} \]
So the limit is $\ans{1/2}$.\par
\textbf{(b)} The $n$th-term test (test for divergence) says: if
$\lim_{n \to \infty} a_n \neq 0$, then $\sum a_n$ diverges. Here the limit is
$\tfrac{1}{2}$, which is not zero, so $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{n}{2n+5}$
\textbf{diverges}.\par
\textbf{The reason, in one sentence:} a sum can only settle down if the pieces being added
eventually become negligible; these terms stay near $\tfrac{1}{2}$ forever, so each new
term adds about another half and the partial sums grow without bound.}

\problem{\textbf{A $p$-series, classified by its exponent.}\par
\textbf{(a)} $\sum \frac{1}{n^{3}}$ has the form $\sum \frac{1}{n^{p}}$ with
$p = 3$.\par
\textbf{(b)} The comparison that decides every $p$-series is $p$ against $1$:
\[ \ans{3 > 1} \]
\textbf{(c)} The $p$-series test says $\sum 1/n^{p}$ converges when $p > 1$ and diverges
when $p \leq 1$. Since $3 > 1$, this series \textbf{converges}.\par
\textit{Note for the student who asks:} the test gives no value for the sum, and there is
no elementary formula for it. Convergence and evaluation are different questions ---
geometric series are unusual in answering both.}

\problem{\textbf{A geometric series with a negative ratio.}\par
\textbf{(a)} With the index starting at $n = 1$, the exponent $n - 1$ is $0$ in the first
term, so $a = 6$ and $r = -\tfrac{1}{3}$. A negative ratio makes the terms
\textbf{alternate} in sign: $6, -2, \tfrac{2}{3}, -\tfrac{2}{9}, \ldots$\par
\textbf{(b)} What matters for convergence is the size of $r$, not its sign:
$|r| = \tfrac{1}{3} < 1$, so the series converges. The same formula applies, with the sign
carried through:
\[ S = \frac{6}{1 - \left(-\tfrac{1}{3}\right)} = \frac{6}{\tfrac{4}{3}}
     = 6 \cdot \frac{3}{4} \]
The sum is $\ans{9/2}$.\par
\textit{Watch for:} using $1 - \tfrac{1}{3}$ in the denominator, which gives $9$. The
formula subtracts $r$, and $r$ is negative here, so the denominator gets larger, not
smaller --- and the sum must be smaller than the first term $6$, which $9$ is not.}

\problem{\textbf{The $n$th-term test on a ratio of quadratics.}\par
\textbf{(a)} Divide through by $n^{2}$, the highest power present:
\[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{3n^{2} + 1}{5n^{2} - n}
   = \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{3 + \tfrac{1}{n^{2}}}{5 - \tfrac{1}{n}}
   = \frac{3 + 0}{5 - 0} \]
The limit is $\ans{3/5}$.\par
\textbf{(b)} The limit is not zero, so by the $n$th-term test the series
\textbf{diverges}.\par
\textbf{(c)} The classmate's rule is wrong because it ignores the \emph{degrees}. A
rational $a_n$ tends to zero only when the denominator's degree is strictly higher than
the numerator's. Here both are degree $2$, so the terms settle at the ratio of the leading
coefficients, $\tfrac{3}{5}$, and never shrink at all.\par
\textit{Watch for:} answering $0$ here. That is the single most common error on this
problem, and it inverts the conclusion --- it would make the $n$th-term test say nothing
when in fact the test settles the question outright.}

\problem{\textbf{Where the $n$th-term test runs out and the $p$-series test does not.}\par
\textbf{(a)} $\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{n}} = \dfrac{1}{n^{1/2}}$, so $p = \tfrac{1}{2}$, and the
comparison is
\[ \ans{1/2 < 1} \]
\textbf{(b)} As $n$ grows, $\sqrt{n}$ grows without bound, so its reciprocal shrinks to
nothing:
\[ \lim_{n \to \infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}} = \ans{0} \]
\textbf{(c)} \emph{Inconclusive} means the test has been applied correctly and simply
returns no information. The $n$th-term test has one direction only: a nonzero limit proves
divergence; a zero limit proves \textbf{nothing}, and the series may do either. So the
work in (b) is not wasted, but it does not answer the question.\par
The $p$-series test does answer it: $p = \tfrac{1}{2} \leq 1$, so
$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{\sqrt{n}}$ \textbf{diverges}.\par
\textbf{This pairing is the point of the sheet.} Two tests, applied to the same series,
give ``no information'' and ``divergent''. Neither contradicts the other, because
``inconclusive'' is not a verdict.}

\problem{\textbf{A bouncing ball as a geometric series.}\par
\textbf{(a)} \textbf{Where the $24$ comes from:} the ball rises to $12\left(\tfrac{3}{4}\right)$
after the first bounce and then falls the same distance, so that bounce contributes
$2 \cdot 12 \cdot \tfrac{3}{4}$. Every later bounce does the same thing, so the $n$th
bounce contributes $2 \cdot 12 \left(\tfrac{3}{4}\right)^{n} = 24\left(\tfrac{3}{4}\right)^{n}$
--- the $24$ is twice the drop height, because each bounce is counted up and down.\par
This is geometric with first term ($n = 1$) equal to $24 \cdot \tfrac{3}{4} = 18$ and
ratio $\tfrac{3}{4}$:
\[ \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 24\left(\tfrac{3}{4}\right)^{n}
   = \frac{18}{1 - \tfrac{3}{4}} = \frac{18}{\tfrac{1}{4}} \]
The bouncing accounts for $\ans{72\ \text{ft}}$.\par
\textbf{(b)} The original drop is not part of that sum --- it happens before the first
bounce --- so add it:
\[ 12 + 72 \]
The total vertical distance is $\ans{84\ \text{ft}}$.\par
\textbf{The idea worth stating out loud:} the ball bounces infinitely many times and
travels a finite distance. Infinitely many nonzero pieces can have a finite total, which
is exactly what a convergent series means. (It also stops in finite time, for the same
reason applied to the durations.)\par
\textit{Watch for:} using $a = 24$ instead of $a = 18$. The index starts at $n = 1$, so the
first term already carries one factor of $\tfrac{3}{4}$; $a = 24$ gives $96$ and a total of
$108$.}

\problem{\textbf{Challenge: what the $n$th-term test can and cannot do.} (Open response ---
read the reasoning.)\par
A full-credit paragraph makes all three moves.\par
\textbf{(i) The test is one-directional.} The $n$th-term test says: if
$\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n \neq 0$ then $\sum a_n$ diverges. Its converse is not a theorem.
$\lim a_n = 0$ is a \emph{necessary} condition for convergence, never a sufficient one, so
from $\lim \tfrac{1}{n} = 0$ nothing whatever follows. The classmate has used the test
backwards.\par
\textbf{(ii) The correct classification.} $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \tfrac{1}{n}$ is the
harmonic series --- a $p$-series with $p = 1$. The $p$-series test requires $p > 1$ for
convergence, and $1 > 1$ is false, so the harmonic series \textbf{diverges}. Model B on the
front page shows it: the running totals reach only about $7.485$ after a thousand terms,
but they keep climbing, and they pass every bound eventually (each block of terms from
$2^{k}+1$ to $2^{k+1}$ adds more than $\tfrac{1}{2}$).\par
\textbf{(iii) The contrast.} $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{n}$ has terms
that also go to zero, and it converges, to $1$. So two series with the same limiting
behaviour of the terms land on opposite sides of the question. What separates them is
\emph{how fast} the terms shrink: geometrically fast is enough, while $\tfrac{1}{n}$
shrinks too slowly to stop the total from growing.\par
Accept any correct convergent geometric series in (iii). Do not accept a paragraph that
asserts the harmonic series diverges without naming $p = 1$ or otherwise giving a reason;
the verdict alone is what the classmate could have guessed.\par
Open explanation --- $\ans{\text{open response}}$.}
