# Solving Equations with Variables on Both Sides — Grade 8 / Pre-Algebra

Three PDFs are ready:

- **`ss_bothsides_curr211.pdf` — Skills Summary (study guide, 2 pages).** Read this first.
  Three sections, each with a rule box, a worked example, and a "try it" whose answer is
  printed upside down inside the box: (1) collecting the variable terms (including *why* you
  remove the smaller one), (2) building the equation from a real situation, (3) the two
  special cases where the variable disappears entirely.
- **`ws_bothsides_curr211.pdf` — Worksheet (4 pages, 10 problems).** The balance-scale model
  runs through the whole sheet: problem 1 is an actual balance, given as a table of what
  sits on each pan, and the directions turn each algebraic move back into "take the same
  thing off both pans". Two problems build the equation from a situation (gym fees, burning
  candles), two are special cases (infinitely many solutions, no solution), and the sheet
  ends with a find-the-error challenge on a student's written work.
- **`ak_bothsides_curr211.pdf` — Answer Key (3 pages).** Every problem re-printed with each
  balancing move named, plus a Quick Answers strip and a Curriculum block (standard
  8.EE.C.7, difficulty 1–5).

## What is verified, honestly

- **7 of the 10 problems are fully machine-checked**, and every numeric answer on the sheet
  is — 8 machine-checked responses in total. SymPy solved each equation independently,
  including the two special cases: problem 9's "no solution" is confirmed as an *empty*
  solution set, and problem 6's identity is confirmed as an algebraic identity that holds for
  every value of $x$. Those are real checks, not a note in the key.
- **Three problems (6, 9 and 10) also carry an instructor-judged part.** Those are the
  written explanations: how many solutions the equation has and why, and what went wrong in
  Elena's work. They print as `---` in the Quick Answers strip, and the answer key gives you
  a rubric for each — what a full-credit answer must name, and what is not enough on its own
  (for instance, "no solution" without the false statement that produced it).
- **One misconception trap was declared and proved distinguishable:** subtracting a constant
  where it needed adding. That is exactly the step Elena gets wrong in problem 10, and the
  build confirmed her value could not be mistaken for the correct solution.
- The study guide's three worked examples and three try-it answers are verified the same
  way (7 checks, all machine-checked).

Problem 7's answer is negative, which is correct and worth not "fixing" when marking — the
answer key shows the substitution check.
