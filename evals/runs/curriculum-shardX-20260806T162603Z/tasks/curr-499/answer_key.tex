\problem{A student says $\lim 1/n = 0$, so $\sum 1/n$ converges by the nth-term test. (a) Confirm the limit. (b) Name the error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The limit is correct: \ans{0}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The nth-term test runs one way only. If the terms do \emph{not} approach zero, the series diverges; if they do approach zero, the test proves nothing whatever. The student used it in the direction it does not have --- and the series in front of them is the standard counterexample, since $\sum \frac1n$ diverges with terms going to zero. Full credit states the one-way rule and names the counterexample.

\problem{$\sum x^n/(n2^n)$; a student reports the interval $(-2,2)$. (a) Radius. (b) Sum at $x=-2$. (c) Correct interval.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $R = \lim \dfrac{2(n+1)}{n}$. \ans{R = 2}

\textbf{Solution (b):} At $x = -2$ the terms become $\dfrac{(-2)^n}{n\,2^n} = \dfrac{(-1)^n}{n}$, the alternating harmonic series. \ans{-\ln 2 \approx -0.693}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} The ratio test settles only the open interval: at an endpoint its limit is exactly $1$, so it is silent there by construction. Both endpoints must be substituted back. At $x = -2$ the series converges (alternating harmonic); at $x = 2$ it is $\sum \frac1n$ and diverges. The interval of convergence is $[-2, 2)$. Full credit names both endpoint series.

\problem{$\sum (-1)^{n+1}/n^2$, first $8$ terms; the student reports the bound $\frac{1}{64}$. (a) Correct bound. (b) Name the error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The alternating series remainder is at most the first \emph{omitted} term. Eight terms run to $n = 8$, so the first omitted term is $n = 9$, of size $\frac{1}{9^2}$. \ans{\frac{1}{81} \approx 0.0123}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The student used the last term \emph{included}, $a_8 = \frac{1}{64}$, instead of the first term \emph{omitted}, $a_9 = \frac{1}{81}$. The bound reads $|S - S_N| \le a_{N+1}$ --- the index is $N+1$, not $N$. Full credit names the index and explains why the omitted term is the one that matters.

\problem{A student sums $\sum_{n\ge2}(1/4)^n$ as $\frac{4}{3}$. (a) Correct sum. (b) Name the error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The first term actually written is the one at $n = 2$, namely $\left(\frac14\right)^2 = \frac{1}{16}$, and the ratio is $\frac14$:
\[ S = \frac{1/16}{1 - 1/4} = \frac{1/16}{3/4}. \]
So the sum is \ans{\frac{1}{12} \approx 0.083}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The geometric formula divides the \emph{first term actually written} by $1 - r$, and the starting index decides what that first term is. The student used $1$, which is the term at $n = 0$ --- a term this sum does not contain. Full credit states the rule in a form that survives any starting index.

\problem{A student says the ratio test gives $1$ for $\sum \frac{n^2}{n^3+1}$, so it converges. (a) Confirm the ratio limit. (b) Limit comparison. (c) Name the error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The ratio of consecutive terms is $\dfrac{(n+1)^2/((n+1)^3+1)}{n^2/(n^3+1)}$, whose limit is \ans{1}

\textbf{Solution (b):} Comparing with $\frac1n$: $\dfrac{n^2/(n^3+1)}{1/n} = \dfrac{n^3}{n^3+1}$, whose limit is \ans{1}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} A ratio limit of $1$ is the ratio test declining to answer --- nothing at all had been established, so ``so it converges'' has no support. Worse, the failure was predictable: for terms that are a ratio of polynomials the ratio test always returns $1$. Limit comparison with the harmonic series gives a finite positive limit, and since $\sum \frac1n$ diverges, so does this series. Full credit gives both the correction and the reason the ratio test had to fail.

\problem{$\sum (2x-6)^n/n$; a student reports radius $1$ and interval $(2,4)$. (a) Correct radius. (b) Both faults, and the correct interval.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} With $(2x-6)^n = 2^n(x-3)^n$ the coefficients are $c_n = 2^n/n$, so $R = \lim \dfrac{n+1}{2n}$. \ans{R = \frac{1}{2}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} Two faults. First, the coefficient $2$ on the variable was never factored out, so the radius came out twice too large; the centre is $3$ and the radius is $\frac12$. Second, the endpoints were never tested. At $x = \frac72$, $2x - 6 = 1$ and the series is harmonic --- diverges. At $x = \frac52$, $2x - 6 = -1$ and it is alternating harmonic --- converges. The interval is $\left[\frac52, \frac72\right)$. Full credit names both faults.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{A student writes $\int_1^{\infty}x^{-3/2}dx = \frac{2}{5}$ and calls that the sum of $\sum n^{-3/2}$. (a) Correct integral. (b) Both errors.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Antidifferentiating adds one to the exponent: $\int x^{-3/2}dx = \dfrac{x^{-1/2}}{-1/2} = -\dfrac{2}{\sqrt{x}}$, so
\[ \int_1^{\infty} x^{-3/2}\,dx = \left[-\frac{2}{\sqrt{x}}\right]_1^{\infty} = 0 + 2. \]
The integral equals \ans{2}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} Two errors. (i) The exponent was decreased rather than increased: $-\frac32 - 1 = -\frac52$ appears in the student's antiderivative where $-\frac32 + 1 = -\frac12$ belongs. (ii) The integral test decides convergence but its value is not the series' sum; the sum lies between the integral and the integral plus the first term, here between $2$ and $3$. Full credit names both.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.2cm}

\problem{$\sum (x+2)^n/(n3^n)$; a student stops at $(-5,1)$. (a) Sum at $x=-5$. (b) Term limit at $x=1$. (c) Correct interval.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} At $x = -5$, $x + 2 = -3$, so the terms become $\dfrac{(-3)^n}{n\,3^n} = \dfrac{(-1)^n}{n}$. \ans{-\ln 2 \approx -0.693}

\textbf{Solution (b):} At $x = 1$, $x + 2 = 3$, so the terms become $\frac1n$, whose limit is \ans{0}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} The student stopped at the open interval the ratio test produced. Testing: $x = -5$ gives the alternating harmonic series, which converges, so that endpoint is included; $x = 1$ gives the harmonic series, which diverges, so that endpoint is not. The interval is $[-5, 1)$. Note the trap at the right endpoint: the terms there \emph{do} approach zero, so the nth-term test says nothing and the harmonic series has to be recognised for what it is. Full credit names that trap.
