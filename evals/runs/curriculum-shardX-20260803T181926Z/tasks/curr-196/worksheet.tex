\wstitleblock{Statistical Questions and How Spread Out the Data Is}{Grade 6--7}{}

\begin{center}\small\textit{Deciding which questions are statistical, then describing a
distribution by its centre and by how spread out it is}\end{center}

\noindent\small\textbf{How to use this sheet.}
\begin{itemize}[label={$\bullet$}, leftmargin=1.1cm, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item A question is \emph{statistical} when you expect the answers to \emph{vary}. The
        test on this sheet is concrete: go and collect the answers, then look at the dot
        plot. All the dots in one stack means no variability, and no variability means the
        question was not statistical.
  \item Every data set is drawn as a dot plot on a number line. One dot is one answer, and
        dots stack up when two people gave the same answer.
  \item Two numbers describe a distribution, and you need both: a \textbf{centre} (mean or
        median) and a \textbf{spread} (range or interquartile range). Reporting the centre
        alone is the mistake problem 10 is about.
  \item Quartiles here are the school method: sort the values, split them into a lower half
        and an upper half, and take the median of each half. $\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1$.
\end{itemize}
\normalsize
