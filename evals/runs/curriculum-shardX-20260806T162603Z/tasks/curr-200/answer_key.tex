\problem{Falcons scored $4$, $6$, $7$, $9$, $9$. Find the mean.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Add the five scores to get $35$, then share that total equally
between the five games: $35 \div 5$. \ans{7}

\problem{Herons scored $5$, $5$, $8$, $10$, $12$. Find the mean.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad The five scores total $40$, and $40 \div 5$ gives the fair share
per game. \ans{8}

\problem{Using Display A, how many sandwiches were sold in the whole week?}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Read each bar against the scale and add all five days:
$12 + 8 + 15 + 6 + 19$. \ans{60}

\problem{A coin is flipped and a number cube is rolled. Probability of heads and a five?}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad The two stages happen together, so the total number of outcomes is
the coin's two results times the cube's six, giving twelve equally likely pairs. Exactly one
of those pairs is heads with a five. \ans{\tfrac{1}{12}}

\problem{Ranges of the two teams, and which was more spread out.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Falcons: largest minus smallest, $9 - 4$. \ans{5}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad Herons: largest minus smallest, $12 - 5$. \ans{7}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The Herons are the more spread out: their
range is the larger of the two, so their game-to-game scoring varied more, while the
Falcons' narrower range means a more predictable performance. A full-credit answer names the
team and quotes both range values as the evidence, rather than saying only that one is
bigger.

\problem{Two number cubes are rolled. Probability the total is seven?}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Six faces on the first cube times six on the second gives
thirty-six equally likely pairs. The pairs totalling seven are one-and-six, two-and-five,
three-and-four and their three reverses --- six of them. Six out of thirty-six reduces.
\ans{\tfrac{1}{6}}

\problem{Using Display A: busiest day, quietest day, and the difference.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad The tallest bar is Friday. \ans{19}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad The shortest bar is Thursday. \ans{6}

\textbf{Solution (c):} \quad Subtract the smaller from the larger. \ans{13}

\problem{Umbrellas sold: $3$, $5$, $6$, $8$, $20$. (a) median, (b) mean, (c) which is more
typical?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad The values are already in order, and the middle one of five is
the third. \ans{6}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad The five values total $42$, and $42 \div 5$ gives the mean.
\ans{8.4}

\textbf{Solution (c) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The median is the better report. One day
sold far more umbrellas than the rest, and that single value drags the mean up above four of
the five days --- so the mean describes no typical day at all, while the median sits among
the ordinary ones. Full credit requires naming that unusually large value as the reason.

\problem{Bag of $3$ red and $5$ blue marbles, drawn twice \emph{with replacement}.
Probability both are red?}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad There are eight marbles, so a single draw is red with probability
three eighths. The marble goes back, so the second draw has exactly the same chance. The
two stages multiply: three eighths of three eighths. \ans{\tfrac{9}{64}}

\problem{Using Display A: Wednesday's total, and its fraction of the week.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Read the Wednesday bar against the scale. \ans{15}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad The week's total from problem 3 is sixty, so the fraction is
fifteen out of sixty, which reduces by dividing both parts by fifteen.
\ans{\tfrac{1}{4}}

\problem{Spinner numbered one to four, spun twice. Probability the total exceeds six?}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Four results on the first spin times four on the second gives
sixteen equally likely pairs. Totals above six come from three-and-four, four-and-three, and
four-and-four --- three cells of the table. The fraction three sixteenths does not reduce.
\ans{\tfrac{3}{16}}

\problem{\textbf{Challenge.} Class One $6$, $7$, $7$, $8$, $12$; Class Two $7$, $8$, $8$,
$8$, $9$. Means, ranges, and which class was steadier.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Class One totals $40$ over five students. \ans{8}

\textbf{Solution (b):} \quad Class Two also totals $40$ over five students. \ans{8}

\textbf{Solution (c):} \quad Class One: $12 - 6$. \ans{6}

\textbf{Solution (d):} \quad Class Two: $9 - 7$. \ans{2}

\textbf{Solution (e) --- instructor-judged.} \quad Class Two was the steadier. The two
means are identical, so centre alone cannot separate the classes at all --- a point worth
drawing out, because it is the whole reason spread is reported. The ranges decide it: Class
Two's scores span a third as much as Class One's, whose top score sits well clear of the
rest. Full credit requires both moves: noticing that the centres tie, and quoting the two
ranges as the deciding evidence.
