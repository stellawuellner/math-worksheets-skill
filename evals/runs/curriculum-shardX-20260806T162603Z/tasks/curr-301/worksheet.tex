\wstitleblock{Precise Definitions and Counterexamples}{High-school geometry}{}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=0.8cm, itemsep=1pt, topsep=2pt]
  \item A definition in geometry has to be \emph{testable}: every condition it names must be one you could check on a figure. If a condition is missing, some figure will satisfy the words and still be the wrong thing --- that figure is a \textbf{counterexample}.
  \item Each diagram shows only the labelling. All measurements are given in the problem text.
  \item Show the equation you wrote before you solve it.
\end{itemize}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.4pt}

\problem{Three students try to define the \emph{midpoint} of $\overline{AB}$.
\par\vspace{0.15cm}
\begin{center}
\begin{tabular}{|l|p{9.2cm}|}\hline
Attempt A & ``A point that is the same distance from $A$ as it is from $B$.'' \\ \hline
Attempt B & ``The point of $\overline{AB}$ that divides it into two congruent segments.'' \\ \hline
Attempt C & ``The point where the two halves of the segment look equal.'' \\ \hline
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
\par\vspace{0.1cm}
Which attempt is a precise definition? For each of the other two, name the condition it leaves out or the word that cannot be tested.
\par\vspace*{4.2cm}\noansline}

\problem{A student claims: \emph{``If two angles have measures that add to $180^\circ$, then they form a linear pair.''} The figure shows what a linear pair actually looks like: two adjacent angles whose non-common sides are opposite rays.
\par\vspace{0.1cm}
\begin{center}
\begin{tikzpicture}[scale=1.0]
  \draw[thick] (-2.6,0) -- (2.6,0);
  \draw[thick] (0,0) -- (1.1,1.7);
  \fill (0,0) circle (1.6pt);
  \node[below left] at (-2.6,0) {$P$};
  \node[below right] at (2.6,0) {$R$};
  \node[above] at (1.1,1.7) {$S$};
  \node[below] at (0,-0.05) {$Q$};
\end{tikzpicture}

\par\vspace{0.1cm}
Give a counterexample to the student's claim. State the two angle measures you choose and explain why your pair is not a linear pair.

\problem{A student proposes this definition: \emph{``The midpoint of a segment is a point on the segment that divides it into two segments.''}
\par\vspace{0.1cm}
(a) Give a counterexample: describe a point that fits every word of this definition and is not the midpoint. Use lengths in your description.
\par\vspace*{3.8cm}
(b) Rewrite the definition so that your counterexample no longer fits it. List every condition your version requires.
