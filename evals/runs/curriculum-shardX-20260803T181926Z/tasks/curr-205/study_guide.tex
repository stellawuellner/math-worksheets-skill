\sstitleblock{Putting Rational and Irrational Numbers in Order}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Rational} means writable as a fraction of whole numbers --- every
terminating or repeating decimal, and every root of a perfect square
($\sqrt{9}=3$). \textbf{Irrational} means the decimal never terminates and
never repeats: $\sqrt{2}$, $\sqrt{30}$, $\pi$.\\[3pt]
\textbf{One rule for the whole topic:} you cannot order values written in
different notations. Convert everything to a decimal first, carrying enough
places that no two of them still look equal.\\[3pt]
\textbf{On the negative side the order reverses:} $-2.3<-2.25$, because
further from zero is smaller.
\end{formulabox}

\skillheading{1. Estimate a square root}

\begin{formulabox}
Trap the radicand between the two perfect squares on either side of it; the
root lies between their roots. Then refine by squaring candidate decimals ---
if the square is too big, come down. Squares worth knowing:
$4,9,16,25,36,49,64,81,100,121,144$.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Estimate $\sqrt{40}$ to the nearest hundredth.
\step{Trapping between perfect squares turns an unknown root into a range you
can already read, so start there.}
\step{$36<40<49$, so $6<\sqrt{40}<7$. Testing: $6.3^2=39.69$ (low) and
$6.33^2=40.07$ (high), so the value is just under $6.33$.}

Rounded to the nearest hundredth, $\sqrt{40}=\ans{6.32}$.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Estimate $\sqrt{60}$ to the nearest hundredth.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{7.75}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{2. Compare two quantities}

\begin{formulabox}
Two ways, both quick. \textbf{Decimals:} convert both and read. \textbf{Squares:}
for two positive values, squaring keeps the order, so compare $(\sqrt{7})^2=7$
against $2.7^2=7.29$ and skip the estimating entirely.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Write $>$ or $<$ between $\sqrt{7}$ and $2.7$.
\step{Both values are positive, so squaring is safe and it removes the radical
without any decimal estimating at all.}
\step{$(\sqrt{7})^2=7$ and $2.7^2=7.29$. Since $7$ is less than $7.29$, the
same order holds before squaring.}

So $\sqrt{7}\ \ans{<}\ 2.7$.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Write $>$ or $<$ between $\sqrt{11}$ and $3.3$.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{>}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{3. Order a mixed list}

\begin{formulabox}
Convert every value to a decimal, order the decimals, then write the answer
back in the \emph{original} symbols. Carry enough decimal places: if two values
still look equal, you have not carried enough. Sort negatives and positives
separately --- every negative is less than every positive.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Order $\sqrt{19}$, $4.3$, $\dfrac{13}{3}$ from least to
greatest.
\step{Three notations cannot be compared as they stand, so put all three into
decimals before deciding anything.}
\step{$\dfrac{13}{3}=4.333$; $\sqrt{19}\approx 4.359$ (since $4.36^2=19.01$);
$4.3$ is given. The decimals rank $4.3$, $4.333$, $4.359$.}

Written in the original symbols:
$\ans{4.3 < \tfrac{13}{3} < \sqrt{19}}$.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} Order $\sqrt{8}$, $2.9$, $\dfrac{14}{5}$ from least to
greatest.\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{\tfrac{14}{5} < \sqrt{8} < 2.9}$}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} $\pi$ is not $\dfrac{22}{7}$ --- they separate at the
third decimal place, so an equals sign is always wrong there. And rounding too
early is the main way these problems go wrong: to two places $\sqrt{2}$ and
$1.41$ look identical, and only a third place separates them.
\end{watchoutbox}
