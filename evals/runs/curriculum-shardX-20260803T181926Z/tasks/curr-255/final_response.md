# Algebra 1 — Choosing the Form That Shows the Structure

Three PDFs are ready: the student worksheet, a full step-by-step answer key, and
a two-page study guide.

**What the worksheet covers.** Twelve problems on deciding whether the expanded
or the factored form of an expression is the one that answers the question in
front of you. The first four are a short warm-up (two straight expansions, two
straight factorings); after that the two jobs are interleaved with a third —
"which form makes this easy?" — so your student has to *choose* a method rather
than repeat one. It ends with one synthesis problem: factor $x^4-13x^2+36$
completely by treating $x^2$ as a single quantity, then use that factored form
to evaluate the expression at $x=5$.

Difficulty ramps from 1 to 5 on the skill's own ladder: routine products first,
then a square of a binomial, a non-monic trinomial, a GCF-first factoring, and
the four-factor synthesis at the end. Each problem has 5–8 cm of work space and
its own answer blank.

**What the answer key contains.** A worked solution for every problem, written
as numbered steps that start with *why* that form was chosen, not just the
algebra. Two problems carry a "common wrong answers" note generated from the
misconception traps declared in the verification data — for problem 5, a student
who writes 8 added the two factor values instead of multiplying them; for
problem 11, a student who writes 9409 used $(m-3)^2$ instead of the
difference-of-squares pattern. The key also carries the quick-answer bank and a
curriculum section listing the standards (HSA-APR.A.1, HSA-SSE.B.3) and the
difficulty range; the grade level appears only there, never on the student's
sheet.

**What the study guide contains.** Three teaching sections — expand to reveal
terms, factor to reveal structure, and choose the form that answers the
question. Each has a rule box, a worked mini-example with its strategy stated in
words, and a try-it problem with the answer printed upside down inside the box
so the student attempts it before checking. A watch-out box closes it with the
two errors this topic produces most: dropping the middle term of a square, and
stopping a "factor completely" halfway.

**Verification.** All 13 machine checks (12 problems, one of which has two
parts) were recomputed independently with SymPy and passed — every expansion,
factorisation, equivalence and evaluation, plus both declared misconception
traps confirmed to be distinguishably wrong. The six study-guide examples and
try-its were verified the same way. Nothing on this set is flagged for manual
review; there are no open-response items.
