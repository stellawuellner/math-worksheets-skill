\problem{\textbf{$P(\text{red})$ from a bag of $5$ red, $3$ blue, $4$ green.}\\
Step 1: fix the denominator before the numerator. ``Out of what?'' --- out of
every marble that could be drawn, which is $5 + 3 + 4 = 12$.\\
Step 2: the outcomes we want are the $5$ red marbles.\\
Step 3: $P(\text{red}) = \ans{\dfrac{5}{12}}$. It does not reduce, since $5$ and
$12$ share no factor.\\
\emph{Watch for:} $\dfrac{5}{7}$, which compares red with not-red. That is a
ratio of red to the rest, not a probability, and it is larger than the true
value.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Ines's $P(\text{blue}) = \dfrac{3}{9}$.}\\
Step 1: name what Ines counted. Her $9$ is the number of marbles that are
\emph{not} blue ($5$ red $+$ $4$ green). That is the count of the outcomes she
did not want --- it leaves the blue marbles out of the whole.\\
Step 2: the denominator must count every possible outcome, blue ones included:
$3 + 9 = 12$.\\
Step 3: $P(\text{blue}) = \ans{\dfrac{3}{12} = \dfrac{1}{4}}$.\\
Step 4: a quick sanity check on any probability written this way --- the three
colour probabilities should add to $1$. With Ines's method they come to more
than $1$, which is impossible.\\
\emph{Full-credit answer says:} ``the denominator counts all $12$ marbles, not
just the $9$ that are not blue.''}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Marco after one red marble is removed.}\\
Step 1: name what changed. Removing a marble and keeping it out changes the
\emph{bag}, so both parts of the fraction have to be re-checked --- not just the
one the question mentions.\\
Step 2: the numerator is genuinely unchanged: the red marble that left was not
green, so there are still $4$ green marbles.\\
Step 3: the denominator did change: $12$ marbles minus the one removed leaves
$11$. So $P(\text{green}) = \ans{\dfrac{4}{11}}$.\\
\emph{Watch for:} $\dfrac{4}{12}$, which is Marco's answer, and it is smaller
than the truth. Taking a non-green marble out makes green \emph{more} likely,
so any answer that goes down is wrong before the arithmetic is even checked.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Max's Year-7 walker.}\\
Step 1: read who is being chosen. ``One student is chosen at random from the
class'' --- so the whole set of possible outcomes is the $24$ students, not the
$10$ walkers.\\
Step 2: the outcomes we want are the $6$ students who both walk and are in Year
$7$.\\
Step 3: $P = \ans{\dfrac{6}{24} = \dfrac{1}{4}}$.\\
Step 4: what Max's fraction does mean. $\dfrac{6}{10}$ is a correct probability
for a \emph{different} question: if a walker is chosen at random, the chance
that walker is in Year $7$. Same numerator, different whole, different
question.\\
\emph{Full-credit answer says:} ``the student was drawn from the class, so the
denominator is $24$.''}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Nora at the bike rack.}\\
Step 1 (part a): scale the sample proportion up to the school. Of the $20$ asked,
$18$ cycle, so the sample fraction is $\dfrac{18}{20}$. Applying that to $300$
students gives $300 \times \dfrac{18}{20}$, which is $\ans{270}$.\\
Step 2 (part b): read the answer for plausibility. It claims $270$ of $300$
students cycle, leaving only $30$ who walk, ride or are driven --- which no
school looks like.\\
Step 3: name the cause. Nora asked at the bike rack, which is a place only
cyclists have a reason to stand. Her sample could not have included a
non-cyclist, so it was guaranteed to over-state cycling before anyone answered.
A fair sample would give every student in the school a chance of being asked ---
for example by drawing names at random from the roll.\\
\emph{Watch for:} the answer $18$, which repeats the sample count as though it
already described the school.}

\problem{\textbf{Find and fix: Sam's library survey.}\\
Step 1 (part a): add the four columns --- the total is the number of
\emph{students} who answered, not the number of clubs listed:
$22 + 12 + 10 + 6$, so $\ans{50}$ students answered.\\
Step 2 (part b): \emph{flagged for manual review.} Model answer: the survey was
taken in the library, where students who prefer quiet indoor activities are
already gathered, so chess is over-represented and sport is under-represented.
The $50$ students are a sample of library users, not a sample of the school, so
the result supports ``chess is popular among students in the library'' and
nothing wider.\\
Step 3: what Sam should do instead. Survey a group in which every student in the
school had an equal chance of being asked --- names drawn at random from the
roll, or one class chosen at random from each year --- and ask everyone in that
group.\\
\emph{Watch for:} the answer $4$ to part (a), which counts clubs instead of
students. \emph{A full-credit part (b)} must name the place as the cause, not
just say ``the sample is too small'' --- doubling the number of library students
surveyed would not fix anything.}

\problem{\textbf{Two samples of reading hours.}\\
Step 1 (part a): a median needs the values in order. Sample A sorted is
$6,\ 7,\ 7,\ 8,\ 9,\ 10,\ 11,\ 14$. With $8$ values the median is the mean of
the $4$th and $5$th: $\dfrac{8+9}{2}$, so the median of Sample A is
$\ans{8.5}$ hours.\\
Step 2 (part b): Sample B sorted is $0,\ 1,\ 2,\ 3,\ 3,\ 5,\ 6,\ 8$. The $4$th
and $5$th values are both $3$, so the median of Sample B is $\ans{3}$ hours.\\
Step 3 (part c): the school should use Sample B's median of $3$ hours. Sample A
was taken from the book club, a group that reads by definition, so it describes
book-club members rather than students in general. Sample B was drawn at random
from the roll, so every student had a chance of being included. Sample A's
median is nearly three times as large, which is the size of the error a biased
sample can hide.\\
\emph{Watch for:} $9$ for Sample A or $5.5$ for Sample B --- both come from
taking the two middle numbers as printed, without sorting first.}

\problem{\textbf{Show what you know: the later bus.}\\
\emph{Parts (a) and (b) are open --- flagged for manual review. Part (c) is
checked.}\\
\textbf{(a)} Model answer: everyone in that queue is already staying late and
already using a late bus, so every one of them has a reason to say yes. Students
who go straight home --- the ones most likely to say no --- had no chance at all
of being asked. Ben's estimate will therefore come out \emph{too high}.\\
\textbf{(b)} Model answer: give every student in the school the same chance of
being picked. Draw $150$ names at random from the school roll, or take every
tenth name from an alphabetical list of all $300$ students, and ask everyone
drawn. What makes it fair is that the selection is decided before anyone's
answer is known, and it does not depend on where a student happens to be
standing.\\
\textbf{(c)} Step 1: the denominator is everyone who was asked, which is $150$,
not just the ones who said no. So the fraction is $\dfrac{45}{150}$.\\
Step 2: divide numerator and denominator by $15$:
$P = \ans{\dfrac{3}{10}}$.\\
Step 3: apply that fraction to the school: $300 \times \dfrac{3}{10}$, so about
$\ans{90}$ students would use the later bus.\\
\emph{Watch for:} $\dfrac{45}{105}$, which uses the number who said no as the
denominator, and the answer $45$, which repeats the sample count instead of
scaling it to the school.\\
\emph{What a full-credit (a) and (b) must contain:} a reason based on \emph{who
could have been asked} rather than on sample size, a stated direction for the
error, and a plan whose fairness comes from every student having a chance of
selection.}
