\problem{Radius of the circle $x^2 + y^2 = 49$.}

\textbf{Solution:} In the standard form $(x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2$, the right-hand side is
$r^2$, not $r$ --- so the radius is the square root of $49$. \ans{7}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$9x^2 + 4y^2 = 36$. (a) Product of the squared-term coefficients. (b) Name the conic and justify.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The coefficients are $9$ and $4$, so the product is \ans{36}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} It is an \emph{ellipse}. The product is
positive, so both squared terms carry the same sign and the curve closes up; but $9 \ne 4$,
so the two axes have different lengths and it is not a circle. (In standard form,
$\frac{x^2}{4} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$.) Full credit names the conic \emph{and} gives the
same-sign, unequal-coefficient reason. A bare name, or the positive product without noticing
the coefficients differ, is partial credit.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Circle with diameter endpoints $(2,1)$ and $(8,9)$. (a) Centre. (b) Radius.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The centre is the midpoint of any diameter.
\[ \left( \frac{2 + 8}{2}, \; \frac{1 + 9}{2} \right) = \left( \frac{10}{2}, \frac{10}{2} \right) \]
\ans{(5,\, 5)}

\textbf{Solution (b):} The radius is half the diameter, so halve the distance between the
endpoints.
\begin{align*}
  \text{diameter} &= \sqrt{(8 - 2)^2 + (9 - 1)^2} = \sqrt{36 + 64} = 10
\end{align*}
Half of that is \ans{5}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Vertex of $y = x^2 - 6x + 5$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} For $y = ax^2 + bx + c$ the vertex sits on the axis of symmetry
$x = -b/(2a)$, with $a = 1$ and $b = -6$:
\[ x = \frac{6}{2} \]
\ans{3}

\textbf{Solution (b):} Substitute that $x$ back into the equation.
\[ y = 3^2 - 6(3) + 5 = 9 - 18 + 5 \]
\ans{-4}
So the vertex is $(3, -4)$ and the vertex form is $y = (x-3)^2 - 4$.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$x^2 - 4y^2 = 16$. (a) Product of the squared-term coefficients. (b) Name the conic and justify.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The coefficients are $1$ and $-4$, so the product is \ans{-4}

\vspace{0.15cm}
\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} It is a \emph{hyperbola}. The product is
negative, so the two squared terms carry opposite signs, and that is exactly the test that
separates a hyperbola from a circle or an ellipse: the curve cannot close up, it opens into
two branches. (In standard form, $\frac{x^2}{16} - \frac{y^2}{4} = 1$.) Full credit names the
conic \emph{and} ties it to the opposite signs; naming it from a remembered picture, with no
reference to the equation, is partial credit.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Ellipse $\frac{x^2}{25} + \frac{y^2}{9} = 1$. (a) Semi-major axis. (b) Focal distance.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The denominators are $a^2$ and $b^2$; the larger one, $25$, sits under
$x^2$, so the long axis is horizontal and its half-length is $\sqrt{25}$. \ans{5}

\textbf{Solution (b):} For an ellipse the focal distance satisfies $c^2 = a^2 - b^2$
--- subtract, because the foci sit \emph{inside} the curve.
\[ c = \sqrt{25 - 9} = \sqrt{16} \]
\ans{4}
So the foci are at $(\pm 4, 0)$, inside the vertices at $(\pm 5, 0)$, as they must be.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Vertex of $y = x^2 + 8x + 11$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} With $a = 1$ and $b = 8$:
\[ x = \frac{-8}{2} \]
\ans{-4}

\textbf{Solution (b):} Substituting back,
\[ y = (-4)^2 + 8(-4) + 11 = 16 - 32 + 11 \]
\ans{-5}
Vertex form: $y = (x + 4)^2 - 5$.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Hyperbola $\frac{y^2}{36} - \frac{x^2}{64} = 1$. (a) Vertex distance. (b) Asymptote slope.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The $y^2$ term is the positive one, so the branches open up and down
and the vertices lie $\sqrt{36}$ above and below the centre. \ans{6}

\textbf{Solution (b):} For this orientation the asymptotes are $y = \pm \frac{a}{b}x$ with
$a = \sqrt{36} = 6$ and $b = \sqrt{64} = 8$.
\[ \frac{6}{8} \]
The rising asymptote has slope \ans{\dfrac{3}{4}}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$x^2 + y^2 - 10x + 6y + 18 = 0$. (a) Centre. (b) Radius.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Complete the square in each variable. Half of $-10$ is $-5$ and half of
$6$ is $3$:
\begin{align*}
  (x^2 - 10x + 25) + (y^2 + 6y + 9) &= -18 + 25 + 9 \\
  (x - 5)^2 + (y + 3)^2 &= 16
\end{align*}
Reading $(x-h)^2 + (y-k)^2 = r^2$ gives $h = 5$ and $k = -3$: the centre is
\ans{x = 5} and \ans{y = -3}.

\textbf{Solution (b):} The right-hand side is $r^2 = 16$, so the radius is \ans{4}

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Where $y = x + 1$ meets $x^2 + y^2 = 25$. (a) The points. (b) Secant, tangent or miss.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Substitute the line into the circle.
\begin{align*}
  x^2 + (x + 1)^2 &= 25 \\
  2x^2 + 2x + 1 &= 25 \\
  x^2 + x - 12 &= 0 \\
  (x - 3)(x + 4) &= 0
\end{align*}
At $x = 3$, $y = 4$; at $x = -4$, $y = -3$. Both points are $5$ from the origin, as the
circle demands. The crossings are \ans{x = 3,\ y = 4} and \ans{x = -4,\ y = -3}.

\vspace{0.15cm}
\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} The substitution always produces a quadratic,
and the count of its real solutions is the picture: two distinct solutions means two crossing
points, so the line is a secant --- which is this case; one repeated solution means the line
touches at exactly one point, a tangent; no real solution means the line misses the circle.
Full credit links the count of real solutions to all three pictures, not just to the two
points found here.
