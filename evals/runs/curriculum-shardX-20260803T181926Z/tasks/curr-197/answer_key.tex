\noindent\small\textbf{What this sheet is teaching, and what to listen for.} Two questions run
through all twelve problems: \emph{what is typical here?} and \emph{how much do the values
differ?} Each has more than one honest answer, and the sheet is built so the answers disagree
on the second data set --- mean $8$ against median $6$, range $21$ against IQR $4.5$. That
disagreement is the lesson, not a complication. When a student's number is wrong, the useful
question is almost never ``what did you compute?'' but ``how many values does your number
depend on?''\normalsize

\problem{\textbf{The mode.} Which number of goals came up most often?

\par\smallskip
Read the heights of the stacks: $0$ goals has two dots, $1$ goal has three, $2$ goals has two,
$3$ goals has two, $4$ goals has one, $5$ goals has one. The tallest stack sits above $1$.
\ans{1}
\par
\textbf{The mode is a value, not a count.} Three games ended $1$--nil, but the mode is $1$ goal,
not $3$ games. A student who answers $3$ has given the height of the stack instead of the label
under it, which is the single most common dot-plot error and worth naming here.}

\problem{\textbf{The median.} What is the median number of goals?

\par\smallskip
The eleven values in order are $0, 0, 1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 3, 3, 4, 5$. With eleven values the middle
one is the sixth, because five sit below it and five above.
\par
Counting six dots in from the left lands on a game with $2$ goals. \ans{2}
\par
\textbf{Why a dot plot makes this easy.} The dots are already in order, so no sorting is needed
--- just count dots, left to right. Students who write out the list first are not wasting time;
they are doing what the plot has already done for them.}

\problem{\textbf{The mean.} What is the mean number of goals per game?

\par\smallskip
Add every game's goals. Reading stack by stack: $0 + 0 + 1 + 1 + 1 + 2 + 2 + 3 + 3 + 4 + 5$,
which is $22$ goals in $11$ games.
\par
$22 \div 11 = 2$ goals per game. \ans{2}
\par
\textbf{If they answered $2.5$:} they averaged the six labels on the axis
($0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$) instead of the eleven games. The axis lists the scores that were
\emph{possible}; the dots record the scores that actually \emph{happened}, and three of them
were $1$--nil. Ask how many games their calculation used.
\par
\textbf{Worth noticing:} on this data set the mean and the median are both $2$. That is a
property of this set, not a rule --- problems 8 and 9 are built to break it.}

\problem{\textbf{The range.} What is the range?

\par\smallskip
The rightmost dot is a game of $5$ goals; the leftmost is a game of $0$.
\par
$5 - 0 = 5$. \ans{5}
\par
\textbf{The range is a single subtraction, and it uses only two of the eleven games.} That is
worth saying out loud now, because problem 12 turns it into the whole point.}

\problem{\textbf{Quartiles.} Find $Q_1$ and $Q_3$.

\par\smallskip
Ordered: $0, 0, 1, 1, 1, \;\mathbf{2},\; 2, 3, 3, 4, 5$. The median (the sixth value) is left
out of both halves.
\par
Lower half: $0, 0, 1, 1, 1$ --- its median is the third of those five, so $Q_1 = \ans{1}$.
\par
Upper half: $2, 3, 3, 4, 5$ --- its median is the third of those five, so $Q_3 = \ans{3}$.
\par
\textbf{Leaving the median out is a convention, and it is the school one.} Some books include
it. What matters is that the student does the same thing to both halves and can say which
convention they used.}

\problem{\textbf{The interquartile range.}

\par\smallskip
$\text{IQR} = Q_3 - Q_1 = 3 - 1 = 2$. \ans{2}
\par
In words: the middle half of the team's games --- the ones that were not unusually low or
unusually high scoring --- all fell within $2$ goals of each other.
\par
\textbf{If they answered $5$:} that is the range, not the IQR. The range asks how far apart the
two most extreme games were; the IQR asks how wide the middle half is. Same data, two different
questions.}

\problem{\textbf{One game at random.} What is the probability the game had $3$ or more goals?

\par\smallskip
Count the dots at $3$ and above: two games of $3$ goals, one of $4$, one of $5$. That is $4$
favourable games out of $11$ games in total.
\par
$P = \ans{\tfrac{4}{11}}$
\par
As a decimal that is about $0.36$, so a bit better than one game in three.
\par
\textbf{If they answered $\frac{3}{11}$:} they counted the three labels ($3$, $4$ and $5$)
rather than the four games sitting on them. Every dot on the plot must have an equal chance of
being picked, which is only true if you count dots.}

\problem{\textbf{A different group.} Find the median number of books.

\par\smallskip
The nine totals are already in order: $3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 24$. With nine values the middle
one is the fifth, with four below and four above.
\par
The fifth value is $6$. \ans{6}
\par
\textbf{The $24$ does not get a vote here.} It sits at the end of the list, so it affects the
median only by being one of the four values above the middle --- it would make no difference to
the answer if Devi had read $240$. Hold that thought for problem 11.}

\problem{\textbf{The mean of the same nine.}

\par\smallskip
$3 + 4 + 5 + 5 + 6 + 7 + 8 + 10 + 24 = 72$ books read by $9$ students.
\par
$72 \div 9 = 8$ books each. \ans{8}
\par
\textbf{Look at what has just happened.} The mean is $8$, but seven of the nine students read
fewer than $8$ books. A ``typical'' value that is bigger than most of the data is a warning
sign, and it is caused entirely by Devi's $24$.
\par
\textbf{If they answered $6$:} they gave the median. Both are measures of centre and both are
correct answers to \emph{some} question --- but this problem asked for the one that shares the
total out equally.}

\problem{\textbf{The range of the same nine.}

\par\smallskip
Largest $24$, smallest $3$, so the range is $24 - 3 = 21$ books. \ans{21}
\par
\textbf{One student decides this number.} Devi is at one end of the subtraction, so the range
describes the gap between her and the least-keen reader, and says nothing at all about the
other seven students, who are packed between $4$ and $10$.}

\problem{\textbf{Devi is the student who read $24$.}

\par\smallskip
\textbf{(a)} Without Devi the eight totals are $3, 4, 5, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10$, which add to $48$.
\par
$48 \div 8 = 6$ books each. \ans{6}
\par
\textbf{(b)} Without Devi there are eight values, so the median is halfway between the fourth
and fifth: halfway between $5$ and $7$, which is $6$. The median has not moved at all.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{(c)} (Open explanation --- listen to the reason.)
\par
\textbf{A model answer.} ``The median describes this group better. The mean adds every value
up, so Devi's $24$ --- more than twice anyone else's total --- pulls it up to $8$, which is
higher than seven of the nine students actually read. Take Devi out and the mean falls all the
way to $6$, while the median stays at $6$ either way. A number that changes that much when one
person leaves is not describing the group, it is describing that person. If the question were
`how many books did the class read altogether?' the mean would be the right tool, because the
mean is the total shared out.''
\par
\textbf{Marking guide.} Full credit for identifying the $24$ as the reason the two measures
disagree \emph{and} committing to one measure with a reason. Either choice can earn full
credit: median because it describes a typical reader, or mean provided the student says the
total is what matters. Partial credit for correctly stating both numbers with no account of why
they differ. Not sufficient: ``the mean is wrong'' --- the mean is not wrong, it is answering a
different question.
\par
\textbf{If the student is stuck:} ask what would happen to each number if Devi had read $240$
books. The mean jumps to $32$; the median does not move.
\par
Open explanation --- $\ans{\text{see model answer}}$.}

\problem{\textbf{Two ways to measure spread.}

\par\smallskip
\textbf{(a)} Ordered: $3, 4, 5, 5, \;\mathbf{6},\; 7, 8, 10, 24$, with the median $6$ left out
of both halves.
\par
Lower half $3, 4, 5, 5$: its median is halfway between $4$ and $5$, so $Q_1 = 4.5$.
\par
Upper half $7, 8, 10, 24$: its median is halfway between $8$ and $10$, so $Q_3 = 9$.
\par
$\text{IQR} = 9 - 4.5 = \ans{4.5}$
\par
\textbf{(b)} The range, $21$, is far larger than the IQR, $4.5$.
\par\smallskip
\textbf{(c)} (Open explanation --- listen to the reason.)
\par
\textbf{A model answer.} ``The interquartile range gives the fairer picture. The range is
worked out from just two values, and here one of them is Devi, so $21$ really measures the gap
between her and the lowest reader rather than how spread out the group is. The interquartile
range measures the width of the middle half of the data, from $4.5$ to $9$, and Devi cannot
change it however many books she reads. Since seven of the nine students are packed between
$4$ and $10$ books, $4.5$ describes what this group actually looks like and $21$ does not.''
\par
\textbf{Marking guide.} Full credit for saying that the range depends on only the two extreme
values (or explicitly on Devi) while the IQR describes the middle half. Partial credit for
saying the IQR is better because it ignores the outlier, with no account of what the range
measures. Not sufficient: ``$4.5$ is smaller than $21$'' --- true, and not a reason.
\par
\textbf{A useful follow-up:} ask what the range would be if Devi were removed. It drops from
$21$ to $7$, while the IQR of the remaining eight barely shifts. That is the whole argument in
one calculation.
\par
Open explanation --- $\ans{\text{see model answer}}$.}
