\problem{A student evaluates $\sum_{k=2}^{6} k$ with $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$ at $n = 6$ and writes
$21$. \ (a) Correct value. \ (b) Name the error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $2 + 3 + 4 + 5 + 6 = 20$, or
$\dfrac{6 \cdot 7}{2} - 1 = 21 - 1$. \ans{20}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The formula $\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$ sums from
$k = 1$, but this sum starts at $k = 2$. The $k = 1$ term must be subtracted, so the student's
answer is exactly $1$ too large. Full credit requires naming the starting index as the problem.

\problem{A student rewrites $\sum_{n=1}^{4} 3^n$ as $\sum_{m=0}^{4} 3^{m+1}$ and gets $363$.
\ (a) Correct value. \ (b) Name the error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $3 + 9 + 27 + 81 = 120$. \ans{120}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The substitution $m = n - 1$ lowers
\emph{both} limits by $1$: the sum should be $\sum_{m=0}^{3} 3^{m+1}$. Leaving the upper limit
at $4$ adds a fifth term, $3^5 = 243$, and indeed $120 + 243 = 363$. Full credit requires the
statement that both limits shift by the same amount.

\problem{A student applies $\frac{a}{1-r}$ to $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{3}{2}\right)^n$
and reports $-2$. \ (a) Evaluate $\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\left(\frac{2}{3}\right)^n$.
\ (b) Why does the formula fail on the student's series?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Here $r = \dfrac{2}{3}$ and $|r| < 1$, and the first term is
$1$, so the sum is $\dfrac{1}{1 - 2/3} = \dfrac{1}{1/3}$. \ans{3}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The formula $\frac{a}{1-r}$ is valid only
when $|r| < 1$. With $r = \frac{3}{2}$ the terms $1, 1.5, 2.25, \ldots$ grow without bound, so
the series diverges and has no sum. The negative answer is the giveaway: a series of positive
terms can never sum to a negative number, so the formula was used outside its domain. Full
credit requires the convergence condition stated.

\problem{A student says $\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{3n+2}{n-5} = 1$. \ (a) Correct limit.
\ (b) Name the error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Divide numerator and denominator by $n$:
\[ \frac{3n+2}{n-5} = \frac{3 + 2/n}{1 - 5/n} \longrightarrow \frac{3 + 0}{1 - 0} \]
\ans{3}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The constants $2$ and $-5$ do become
negligible, but the \emph{coefficients} $3$ and $1$ do not cancel --- the numerator grows three
times as fast as the denominator. The limit is the ratio of the leading coefficients, $3$, not
$1$. Full credit requires distinguishing the constants from the coefficients.

\problem{Rewriting $\sum_{k=3}^{7}(k-2)^2$ with $j = k-2$, a student writes
$\sum_{j=1}^{7} j^2 = 140$. \ (a) Correct value. \ (b) Name the error and the term count.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $1 + 4 + 9 + 16 + 25 = 55$. \ans{55}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad With $j = k - 2$, $k = 3$ gives $j = 1$ and
$k = 7$ gives $j = 5$, so the correct rewrite is $\sum_{j=1}^{5} j^2$. The sum has
$7 - 3 + 1 = 5$ terms before the shift and must still have $5$ after it. The student kept the
old upper limit, adding $36$ and $49$: $55 + 36 + 49 = 140$. Full credit requires the corrected
upper limit and the term count.

\problem{A student evaluates $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 5\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{n-1}$ as
$\frac{5/4}{1-1/4} \approx 1.67$. \ (a) Correct sum. \ (b) Name the error in $a$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad The first term is the $n=1$ term,
$5\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^{0} = 5$, and $r = \frac{1}{4}$, so the sum is
$\dfrac{5}{1 - 1/4} = \dfrac{5}{3/4}$. \ans{\tfrac{20}{3}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The exponent is $n-1$, so substituting
$n = 1$ gives $\left(\frac{1}{4}\right)^0 = 1$ and the first term is $5$, not $\frac{5}{4}$.
The student read the term as if the exponent were $n$. The value of $a$ is always the series'
own first term as written. Full credit requires the exponent $n-1$ named as the cause.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{A student says $a_n = (-1)^n \frac{n}{n+1}$ converges to $1$. \ (a) Compute $a_{10}$
and $a_{11}$. \ (b) Name the error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $a_{10} = (+1)\dfrac{10}{11}$, so \ans{a_{10} = \tfrac{10}{11}}

and $a_{11} = (-1)\dfrac{11}{12}$, so \ans{a_{11} = -\tfrac{11}{12}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The size $\frac{n}{n+1}$ does approach $1$,
but the factor $(-1)^n$ flips the sign at every step, so consecutive terms sit near $+1$ and
near $-1$ forever. The sequence never settles on one number: it diverges, and no limit exists.
Full credit requires the alternating sign as the reason and the conclusion that there is no
limit.

\problem{A student evaluates $\sum_{n=2}^{\infty} 3\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{n}$ as
$\frac{3}{1-1/2} = 6$. \ (a) Correct sum. \ (b) Name the error and give the right $a$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad The first term of this series is the $n = 2$ term,
$3\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)^{2} = \frac{3}{4}$, with $r = \frac{1}{2}$:
\[ \frac{3/4}{1 - 1/2} = \frac{3/4}{1/2} \]
\ans{\tfrac{3}{2}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad In $\frac{a}{1-r}$, $a$ is the first term of
the series \emph{as written}. This series starts at $n = 2$, so $a = \frac{3}{4}$; the student
used the $n = 0$ term, $3$, which belongs to a longer series. Adding the missing $n=0$ and
$n=1$ terms, $3 + \frac{3}{2} = \frac{9}{2}$, to the correct $\frac{3}{2}$ recovers the
student's $6$ --- which is exactly the sum of the wrong series. Full credit requires
$a = \frac{3}{4}$ and the reason.
