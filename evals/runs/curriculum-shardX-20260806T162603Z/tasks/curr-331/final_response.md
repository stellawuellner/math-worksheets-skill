Here are the three PDFs for the quadrilateral-classification set:

- **Worksheet** — `ws_quads_curr331.pdf` (5 pages)
- **Answer key** — `ak_quads_curr331.pdf` (3 pages)
- **Study guide** — `ss_quads_curr331.pdf` (2 pages)

**What the worksheet covers.** Eight problems on naming a quadrilateral from the
properties it is given — and, just as importantly, on not naming more than the
properties force. A family-tree diagram in the directions shows how the names
nest, and the sheet fixes the definition it uses (a trapezoid here has *exactly*
one pair of parallel sides) so no answer turns on an unstated convention.

Most problems pair an algebraic step with the classification: an angle sum that
identifies an isosceles trapezoid, bisecting diagonals that force a parallelogram
and no more, four congruent sides plus congruent diagonals that force a square,
congruent-but-not-perpendicular diagonals that give a rectangle, and adjacent
congruent side pairs that give a kite rather than a rhombus. Problem 5 is a
three-part always/sometimes/never item, and problem 8 asks for a counterexample
to a plausible but false student claim.

**What is verified, honestly — please read this part.** This is a
concept-and-justification sheet, and most of what it asks for is prose, so the
verification split is unusually lopsided:

- **9 of the 20 responses are machine-checked** with SymPy: every algebraic value
  (x = 36, 6, 8, 38, 7, 6, 7 and y = 8, plus the 94° angle).
- **11 responses are instructor-judged**: every "name the figure and justify it"
  part, all three always/sometimes/never statements, the "why can it not be a
  square" explanation, and the counterexample. A classification argument is
  reasoning, not arithmetic, and nothing here pretends otherwise.

**No problem on this sheet is fully machine-checked end to end** — every one of
the eight ends in something a person must read. The answer key's "What is
verified" note says exactly that ("9 of 20 answers machine-checked … across 8
problems"), and each instructor-judged part has a written rubric beside the
worked solution saying what a complete response must contain and what earns only
partial credit. Nothing is marked `[unchecked]`: every printed blank is either
verified or explicitly flagged.

One note for grading: where a problem has more than one instructor-judged part,
the Quick Answers bank shows a single `---` for them rather than one per part
(problem 5 has three such parts, problem 6 has two). The worked solutions in the
key are labelled part by part, so grade those parts from the solutions rather
than from the bank row.

**Study guide.** A property table up front — the defining condition for each name
in the family — then three sections: the 360° angle sum, reading a defining
property into an equation, and naming the figure from what you are given (with
the deliberate lesson that "parallelogram" is the right answer when nothing
tells you more). Each has a worked example that opens with the reasoning and a
try-it with its answer printed upside down. The watch-out box covers the
commonest slip: opposite angles are congruent, consecutive ones are
supplementary.
