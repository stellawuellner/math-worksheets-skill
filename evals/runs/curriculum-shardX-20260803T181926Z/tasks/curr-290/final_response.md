# Mixed Factoring: Choosing and Combining Methods — Algebra 1

Three PDFs are ready.

**Study guide (2 pages)** — start here. It opens with the five-step factoring
checklist (common factor first, count the terms, test for a perfect square,
check the leading coefficient, factor again), then gives four skill sections,
each with a rule box, a two-step worked example, and a try-it with the answer
printed upside down inside the box:

1. common factor first, then look again — $2x^3 - 8x = 2x(x-2)(x+2)$
2. trinomials with leading coefficient 1 — $x^2 + 7x + 12$
3. trinomials where the leading coefficient is not 1 (grouping) — $3x^2 + 11x + 6$
4. special forms: difference of squares and perfect squares — $4x^2 - 20x + 25$

A watch-out box closes it with the trap this focus is really about: a correct
product is not always a complete factorization.

**Worksheet (5 pages, 12 problems)** — no problem tells the student which
method to use; choosing is the work. Problems 1–3 are a short blocked warm-up
(one GCF, one monic trinomial, one difference of squares), and after that the
four methods rotate so the student never gets two of the same kind in a row.
Six of the twelve need more than one technique in the same expression:
GCF then difference of squares (4, 8), GCF then perfect square (10), difference
of squares applied twice (11), and the synthesis challenge, $4x^3 + 4x^2 - 24x$,
which needs GCF then a monic trinomial. Each problem also asks the student to
name the technique they chose beside the answer, which is the habit the focus is
building. Problem 6 is a flower-bed area problem; problem 10 is an error
analysis where the student's factorization multiplies back correctly and is
still wrong. Work space runs 4–6 cm per problem, growing with difficulty.

**Answer key (3 pages)** — a quick-answer bank at the top for fast grading, a
generated Curriculum section (level, standards codes, difficulty range), then a
three-step solution for every problem. Step 1 is always the *decision* — why
that method and not another — because that is the skill being assessed. Each
solution names the technique used at the end.

**Verification.** All 12 problems were machine-checked with SymPy: 13 of the 14
checks passed automatically (problem 10 carries three checks — the student's
expansion, the complete factorization, and the written explanation).

**Flagged for manual review:** one item — problem 10(b), the one-sentence
explanation of why $(2x+4)(x+2)$ is not complete. That is genuinely open
writing, so it is labelled `manual` rather than claimed as verified. The answer
key tells you what to accept: any sentence saying $2x + 4$ still contains a
common factor of 2, or that the answer is a correct product but not fully
factored.

Standards: HSA-SSE.B.3 (10 checks) and HSA-APR.A.1 (2 checks). Difficulty ramps
1 → 5 across the sheet.
