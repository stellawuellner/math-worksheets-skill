\noindent\textbf{The data.} Study time $x$ (hours): $1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6$; \ test score $y$
(points): $59, 59, 68, 74, 77, 89$. Least-squares line $\hat{y} = 50 + 6x$;
$\sum(x-\bar{x})(y-\bar{y}) = 105$, $\sum(x-\bar{x})^2 = 17.5$,
$\sum(y-\bar{y})^2 = 666$.

\problem{What score did the student who studied $4$ hours earn?}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad Read the table under $x = 4$. \ans{74}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Predict the score for $3.5$ hours of study.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad $\hat{y} = 50 + 6(3.5) = 50 + 21$. (This is also the mean score:
a least-squares line always passes through $(\bar{x}, \bar{y})$, and here
$\bar{x} = 3.5$.) \ans{\hat{y} = 71}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Slope $= 6$. \ (a) predicted change over $2$ extra hours. \ (b) interpret the slope.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad The slope is a rate, so the predicted change is
$6 \times 2$. \ans{12}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad Each additional hour of study is associated
with a predicted increase of $6$ points on the test. Full credit requires the units of both
variables (hours in, points out) and language of association or prediction --- not a claim
that any particular student will gain exactly $6$ points.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{The $5$-hour student scored $77$. Find the residual.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad $\hat{y} = 50 + 6(5) = 80$, so the residual is
$y - \hat{y} = 77 - 80$. The negative sign says the point lies below the line.
\ans{-3}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Compute the correlation coefficient $r$.}

\textbf{Solution:} \quad
\[ r = \frac{105}{\sqrt{17.5 \times 666}} = \frac{105}{\sqrt{11655}} = \frac{105}{107.9583\ldots} \]
\ans{r \approx 0.9726}

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{(a) Compute $r^2$. \ (b) Interpret it.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $r^2 = \dfrac{105^2}{17.5 \times 666} = \dfrac{11025}{11655}$.
\ans{r^2 \approx 0.9459}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad About $94.6\%$ of the variation in the test
scores of these six students is accounted for by the linear relationship with study time; the
remaining $5\%$ or so is due to everything else. Full credit requires the word variation (or
variability), a reference to the scores, and a percentage --- restating "$0.9459$" is not an
interpretation.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{Intercept $= 50$. \ (a) predicted score at $0$ hours. \ (b) is it trustworthy?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $\hat{y} = 50 + 6(0)$. \ans{50}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad No. The observed study times run from $1$ to
$6$ hours, so $x = 0$ lies outside the data. The intercept is the arithmetic value of the line
there, not evidence about students who did not study at all --- using it that way is
extrapolation. Full credit requires naming the observed range and the word extrapolation (or
an equivalent statement).

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{(a) $P(\text{a randomly chosen one of the six studied more than } 4 \text{ hours})$.
\ (b) why correlation is not causation.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad Two of the six studied more than $4$ hours ($5$ and $6$), so
$P = \dfrac{2}{6}$. \ans{P = \tfrac{1}{3}}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad $r \approx 0.97$ says only that the six
points lie close to a straight line. These are observational data: a lurking variable --- prior
preparation, sleep, motivation --- could raise both the study time and the score, and the
students chose their own study times rather than being assigned them. Only a randomized
controlled experiment could support a causal claim. Full credit requires naming a plausible
lurking variable or the observational design.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{(a) Predicted score at $x = 8$. \ (b) why treat it with caution?}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $\hat{y} = 50 + 6(8) = 50 + 48$. \ans{98}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad $8$ hours is beyond the largest observed
study time of $6$ hours, so this is an extrapolation: nothing in the data says the linear
pattern continues out there. Pushed a little further the line predicts scores above $100$,
which the test cannot produce --- a clear sign the model has left its valid range. Full credit
requires the extrapolation point; the impossible-score observation is a strong bonus.

\vspace{0.15cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}\vspace{0.15cm}

\problem{\textbf{Synthesis.} A seventh student studies $4$ hours and scores $62$.
\ (a) residual. \ (b) what it means and its effect.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} \quad $\hat{y} = 50 + 6(4) = 74$, so the residual is
$62 - 74$. \ans{-12}

\textbf{Solution (b) --- instructor-judged.} \quad The new student scored $12$ points below
what the line predicts --- four times the largest residual among the original six, which was
$3$. A point that far off the line increases the scatter about it, so $r$ (about $0.97$) would
fall, $r^2$ would fall with it, and the refitted line would be pulled downward in the middle of
the $x$ range, flattening the slope slightly. Full credit requires reading the sign and size of
the residual and predicting a lower correlation.
