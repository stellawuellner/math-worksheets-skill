# Shapes and Their Names — Turning a Shape Does Not Change It (K–Grade 1)

Three PDFs are ready:

- **Worksheet** (`ws_shapes_curr046.pdf`, 5 pages) — 8 problems. Every shape on
  the sheet is drawn *turned*: triangles standing on a point or pointing
  sideways, squares balanced on a corner, rectangles leaning over. That is the
  whole idea of the sheet, so nothing on it sits the "normal" way up.
- **Answer key** (`ak_shapes_curr046.pdf`, 3 pages) — the answers plus, for each
  problem, what to say if your child gets it wrong and what a good reason from
  them sounds like.
- **Study guide** (`ss_shapes_curr046.pdf`, 2 pages) — a one-page-each reference
  the child can keep beside them: count the sides to name it, turning changes
  nothing, and sort-count-compare. Each section has a picture, a worked example,
  and a try-it whose answer is printed upside down inside the box.

## Why the sheet is built this way

Children usually meet shapes in books where triangles rest on a flat bottom and
squares have level tops. The predictable result is that a square turned onto its
corner gets renamed a "diamond" and a triangle standing on its point stops
looking like a triangle. This sheet works on exactly that.

The fix it teaches is not a rule to memorise — it is **counting**. Sides and
corners do not change when a shape is turned, and a five-year-old can count and
trust the result. So when you hear "that's a diamond," the answer to give is
"how many sides?" rather than "no." The answer key says this at every problem
where it comes up.

The 8 problems: sort a set of six turned shapes into a tally (1), say which
kind there are more of (2), find the one shape in a row of tilted triangles that
is not one (3), count the sides and corners of a square balanced on its corner
(4), pick out three rectangles that are standing, leaning and lying down (5),
compare two groups by how many four-sided shapes each holds (6), count the
corners on the same square drawn flat and drawn turned (7), and draw a triangle
that is not sitting flat (8).

## What was checked, and what needs you

Ten counts and comparisons were checked by machine against the shapes actually
drawn — the tallies in problem 1, the triangle and rectangle counts, the sides
and corners of the turned square, and all three greater-than / less-than /
equals comparisons. So the answer key cannot disagree with the pictures.

**Problem 8 needs an adult**, and is marked that way. The child draws their own
tilted triangle and says how they know it is still a triangle; there is no single
right drawing. The answer key tells you the two things to check — three straight
sides that close up, and a reason that names something countable rather than the
way it is turned — and what to do if they draw it sitting flat anyway (don't mark
it wrong; ask them to turn the paper).

## How to use it

Read every question aloud. Coloured pencils help, and so does letting the child
turn the page around — that is not cheating here, it is the lesson.
