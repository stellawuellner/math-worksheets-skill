Here are the three PDFs on deciding whether a relation is a function.

**Worksheet — `ws_functions_curr231.pdf` (6 pages, 10 problems).** Every
problem asks the same question in a different costume, and every one comes with
a picture: mapping diagrams (1, 2, 8, 10), plotted points and graphs (3, 4, 6,
9), and a data table with its chart (5, 7). Rather than leaving "is it a
function?" as a yes/no hunch, the sheet gives one countable procedure your
student uses everywhere — count the pairs, count the *different* inputs, and
write the two counts with $<$, $>$ or $=$ between them. Equal means function;
fewer inputs than pairs means some input was used twice. On graphs the same
test appears as the vertical line test.

Three problems are deliberately about the trap that costs the most marks:
problem 4 ($y=|x|$) and problem 5 (two equal temperatures) both have repeated
*outputs* and are still functions, while problems 2, 6, 7 and 9 fail because an
*input* repeats. Problem 7 and problem 10 push further: the same table can be a
function in one direction and not in the other, which is the idea "one-to-one"
names. Problem 8 is the algebra one — a diagram where one input has two arrows,
and $k$ has to be solved for so both arrows land on the same output.

**Answer key — `ak_functions_curr231.pdf` (3 pages).** Every problem worked in
full, and each verdict is justified the way it should be on a test: to disprove
a relation, the key names the offending input and its two outputs, not just
"not a function". A quick-answer bank and a generated Curriculum section sit at
the top. The closing notes point out the two things students most often get
backwards — that outputs may repeat but inputs may not, and that "function"
depends on which column you call the input.

**Study guide — `ss_functions_curr231.pdf` (2 pages).** The definition and the
count test up front, then three sections — mapping diagrams, sets and tables,
graphs — each with a rule box, a worked example (two carry the picture), and a
distinct try-it whose answer is printed upside down inside the box. Each pair is
built as a contrast: the worked example fails the test and the try-it passes,
or the reverse, so your student has to actually count rather than pattern-match
a layout.

**What was verified.** 19 of 22 checks were recomputed independently and
passed: every table and chart reading (the charts are plotted from the same
data the check reads, so figure and answer cannot disagree), every function
evaluation, the value of $k$ in problem 8, and every one of the count
comparisons — each `<` or `=` verdict was recomputed as a comparison, so a
wrong verdict could not have reached the page.

**Flagged for manual review (3 items),** all of them the written-reasoning
parts: problem 4(c), why two inputs sharing an output is harmless; problem 6(c),
naming a vertical line that meets the circle twice; and problem 10(c), why
reversing the locker relation still gives a function and what single change
would break that. The key gives a full model answer for each.

One note on the automated report: the prose checker flags the individual
numbers drawn inside the mapping diagrams, because what is machine-checked for
those problems is the *counts* (3 arrows vs 4 inputs), not the individual
arrow labels. That is expected here and not an error in the sheet.
