\problem{\textbf{Two ten-frames: one full, one with three counters.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The first frame is completely full. A full ten-frame always
holds ten --- the child should recognise it without counting the dots one by
one, which is the whole point of the frame.

\textbf{Step 2.} The second frame has three counters in it, so the ones are
$\ans{3}$

\textbf{Step 3.} Put the two parts together: $10 + 3 = \ans{13}$

\textit{Listen for:} a child who recounts the full frame from one. Ask "how
many does a full frame hold?" before they start counting.}

\problem{\textbf{One ten-stick and six loose cubes.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The stick is made of ten cubes joined together, so it counts as
one ten. It does not need counting.

\textbf{Step 2.} Count the loose cubes: there are $\ans{6}$

\textbf{Step 3.} $10 + 6 = \ans{16}$

\textit{Note:} this is the same idea as problem 1 in a different material. Say
the sentence aloud both ways --- "ten and six" and "sixteen" --- so the number
word and the model line up.}

\problem{\textbf{Draw 15; then 15 is one ten and how many ones?}

\textbf{Step 1.} Fill the first frame all the way up. That uses ten of the
fifteen counters.

\textbf{Step 2.} Fifteen take away ten leaves $\ans{5}$, so five counters go in
the second frame --- one row of five, which is easy to see.

\textbf{Step 3.} The sentence reads: 15 is one ten and 5 ones.

\textit{Common wrong answer:} $15$ in the ones box --- the whole number was
copied instead of just the leftovers. The first frame is already used up, so
the ones can never be more than nine.}

\problem{\textbf{Draw 18; then 18 is one ten and how many ones?}

\textbf{Step 1.} First frame full again: ten counters.

\textbf{Step 2.} Eighteen take away ten leaves $\ans{8}$, so the second frame
gets eight --- a full top row of five and three underneath.

\textbf{Step 3.} 18 is one ten and 8 ones.

\textit{Note:} the tens digit of every teen number is $1$, and the ones digit
is exactly the number of leftover counters. Point at both while saying it.}

\problem{\textbf{Compare the two piles.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Both piles have exactly one ten-stick, so the ten does not
help us decide. Cover the sticks with a hand and look only at the loose cubes.

\textbf{Step 2.} The left pile has four loose cubes and the right pile has
seven. Four is less than seven.

\textbf{Step 3.} $\ans{10 + 4 < 10 + 7}$ --- that is, fourteen is less than
seventeen.

\textit{Listen for:} the mouth of the symbol opens toward the bigger pile.}

\problem{\textbf{Order 16, 12 and 19 on the number line.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Every one of these is one ten and some ones, so they all sit
between ten and twenty on the line. Only the ones digit moves them.

\textbf{Step 2.} Two ones, six ones, nine ones --- reading the line from left
to right puts them in that order.

\textbf{Step 3.} Smallest first: $\ans{12,\ 16,\ 19}$

\textit{Note:} the number line is doing the same job the ten-frames did. Ask
which one is nearest twenty and why.}

\problem{\textbf{Number bond: the whole is 17, one part is the ten.}

\textbf{Step 1.} The circle on top holds the whole number, and the two circles
underneath hold its parts. One part is already filled in as the ten.

\textbf{Step 2.} We need the number that goes with ten to make seventeen. Count
on from ten: eleven, twelve, thirteen, fourteen, fifteen, sixteen, seventeen
--- that is $\ans{7}$ counts.

\textbf{Step 3.} $10 + 7 = 17$, so the missing part is 7.

\textit{Note:} this is the reverse of problems 1 and 2. There the parts were
given and the whole was missing; here the whole is given and a part is missing.
Same bond, read the other way.}

\problem{\textbf{Ella's pile against Mo's pile.}

\textbf{Step 1.} Both children have one ten-stick, so again only the loose cubes
matter. Ella has nine, Mo has five.

\textbf{Step 2 (part a).} Nine is more than five, so
$\ans{10 + 9 > 10 + 5}$ --- nineteen is greater than fifteen.

\textbf{Step 3 (part b).} "How many more" is a subtraction. Because the tens
match, subtract the ones or count up from Mo's number to Ella's: fifteen,
sixteen, seventeen, eighteen, nineteen. Either way the answer is
$\ans{4}$

\textit{Note:} the two tens cancel out. A child who tries to subtract nineteen
minus fifteen by counting all the cubes will get the same four --- slower, but
correct. Show them the shortcut afterwards, not instead.}
