# Comparing Categorical Frequencies in a Two-Way Table — Grade 8 / Pre-Algebra

Three PDFs are ready: the student worksheet, a full step-by-step answer key, and
a two-page study guide.

**Worksheet (5 pages, 10 problems).** One survey table drives the whole sheet:
200 eighth graders asked whether they play a school sport and whether they get
8 or more hours of sleep on a school night. The table is printed with all four
inner cells, both row totals, both column totals and the grand total, so the
student can see where every base comes from.

The sheet is built around the one distinction this topic lives or dies on: a row
relative frequency and a column relative frequency use the *same cell* and
different totals, and they answer different questions. Problems 1–2 read cells
and check the grand total. Problems 3 and 5 compute the two row percentages
(60 percent of athletes sleep 8+ hours; 30 percent of non-athletes do). Problems
6 and 7 turn the same cells into column percentages (40 percent and 16 percent),
which come out to different numbers on purpose. Problem 4 is a raw-count
comparison that problem 10 then criticizes. Difficulty ramps 1 → 5.

The numbers were chosen so the two rows have very different sizes (50 athletes,
150 non-athletes). That is what makes the raw counts 30 and 45 point the
opposite way from the percentages — the trap the whole standard exists to teach.

**What was machine-verified.** Nine of the ten answers were recomputed
independently and the build was blocked until each agreed with the answer key:
the cell read (30), the grand total (200), the cell difference (85), all four
percentages (60, 30, 40, 16), the percentage-point gap (30) and the median of
the five class results (60). Three misconception traps were also checked for
distinguishability and are printed in the answer key's "common wrong answers"
block: dividing by the grand total instead of the row total (gives 15), and
using the row total where the column total belongs (gives 60 instead of 40, and
40 instead of 16). Those are exactly the wrong answers a student who mixes up
rows and columns will produce, so a wrong answer tells you which mistake it was.

**Flagged for manual review.** Problem 10 asks the student to decide whether
there is an association and explain why the two row percentages are better
evidence than the raw counts 30 and 45. That is open reasoning, so it is labelled
manual. The answer key gives a full model answer and a grading note: the
explanation must name the unequal row totals as the reason the counts mislead,
not merely observe that 60 is larger than 30.

**Study guide (2 pages).** A small pet-clinic table with four sections built on
it: reading a cell, row relative frequency, column relative frequency, and
comparing the two to judge association — including a try-it whose gap is only
3 percentage points, so the student also meets the "no association" case. The
watch-out box gives the practical rule: read the phrase "of the …" in the
question, and whatever follows it names the total you divide by.

**One note on the standards tag.** Every problem is tagged `8.SP.A.1` from the
skill's standards map, which is the map's row for bivariate data. The map has no
row for `8.SP.A.4`, which is the specific two-way-table standard this sheet
exercises; `8.SP.A.1` is the same cluster and the code the task itself supplied,
so I used it rather than inventing a code, but a `8.SP.A.4` row would be the
more precise tag if one were added.
