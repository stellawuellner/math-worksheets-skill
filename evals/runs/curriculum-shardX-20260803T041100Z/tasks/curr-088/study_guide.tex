\sstitleblock{One-Step Mass and Liquid Volume Problems}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{The units.} Mass is how heavy something is: grams (g) and kilograms
(kg). Liquid volume is how much liquid fills a container: millilitres (mL) and
litres (L).\\[3pt]
\textbf{Choosing the one step.} ``In all'', ``altogether'', ``put together''
$\rightarrow$ \textbf{add}. ``Left'', ``poured out'', ``taken away'', ``how
much more'' $\rightarrow$ \textbf{subtract}. Equal groups $\rightarrow$
\textbf{multiply}. Shared equally $\rightarrow$ \textbf{divide}.\\[3pt]
\textbf{The unit rule.} The answer to a measurement question always carries a
unit, and it is the same unit the givens were measured in.
\end{formulabox}

\skillheading{1. Mass problems (grams and kilograms)}

\begin{formulabox}
Write both masses down with their unit before you choose an operation. Masses
combine exactly like plain numbers --- what the unit does is tell you the answer
is still a mass.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} A tin of beans has a mass of $320$ g. A tin of corn has a
mass of $180$ g. What is the mass of both tins together?
\step{``Together'' means the two masses are put into one amount, so this is one
addition.}
\step{$320 + 180$: make a friendly number first, $320 + 80 = 400$, then add the
$100$ that is left.}
The two tins have a mass of $\ans{500}$ g.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} A melon has a mass of $460$ g and a pear has a mass of $140$ g.
What is their total mass, in grams?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{600}$ g}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{2. Liquid volume (millilitres and litres)}

\begin{formulabox}
Liquid poured \emph{out} of a container makes the amount smaller, so the answer
must be \emph{less} than what you started with. That size check catches most
wrong answers before the grader does.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} A bottle holds $900$ mL of water. Sam pours $250$ mL into a
glass. How much water is left in the bottle?
\step{Water leaves the bottle, so the one step is a subtraction, and the answer
has to come out smaller than $900$ mL.}
\step{$900 - 250$: take away $200$ to get $700$, then take away $50$.}
The bottle still holds $\ans{650}$ mL.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} A jug holds $750$ mL of lemonade. Ada pours out $400$ mL. How
much lemonade is left, in millilitres?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{350}$ mL}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{3. Read the measurement, then take one step}

\begin{formulabox}
When the measurements sit in a table, the first job is \emph{reading}: find the
two rows the question names and ignore the rest. Then take the single step the
question asks for.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} A record table gives bag A a mass of $700$ g and bag B a mass
of $450$ g. How much heavier is bag A than bag B?
\step{``How much heavier'' compares two masses, so read those two rows and
subtract the smaller from the larger.}
\step{$700 - 450 = 250$.}
Bag A is $\ans{250}$ g heavier than bag B.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} The same table gives jug A $600$ mL of water and jug B
$375$ mL. How much more water is in jug A, in millilitres?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{225}$ mL}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} an answer bigger than what you started with is a
warning sign on a ``poured out'' or ``taken away'' problem --- it usually means
the two numbers were added. And never drop the unit: $450$ on its own does not
say whether the water is $450$ mL or the melon is $450$ g.
\end{watchoutbox}
