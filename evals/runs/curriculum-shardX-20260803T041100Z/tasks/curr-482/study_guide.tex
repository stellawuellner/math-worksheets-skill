\sstitleblock{Separable Differential Equations with Initial Conditions}

\begin{formulabox}
\textbf{Separable} means $\frac{dy}{dx} = g(x)h(y)$ --- the two variables can
be pulled apart.\\[3pt]
\textbf{The three moves:} (1) separate into $\frac{dy}{h(y)} = g(x)\,dx$;
(2) integrate both sides, with a single $+C$; (3) apply the initial condition
to turn $C$ into a number.\\[3pt]
\textbf{General vs particular:} a general solution still carries $C$; a
particular solution is the one curve through the given point. An
initial-value problem is not finished until $C$ is a number.\\[3pt]
\textbf{Useful antiderivative:} $\int \frac{dy}{y} = \ln|y|$ --- keep the
absolute value until the initial condition tells you the sign.
\end{formulabox}

\skillheading{1. Separate the variables and integrate}

\begin{formulabox}
Everything with $y$ (including $dy$) goes left; everything with $x$ (including
$dx$) goes right. If $y$ cannot be isolated from $x$ by multiplying and
dividing, the equation is not separable and this method does not apply.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} Separate and integrate $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = \dfrac{x}{y}$.
\step{Both variables appear in a single quotient, so cross-multiplying splits
them cleanly --- separate before integrating anything.}
\step{$y\,dy = x\,dx$. Integrating the left side with respect to $y$ gives
$\int y\,dy$, and the right gives $\frac{x^2}{2}$.}
Left side: $\ans{y^2/2}$, so the implicit solution is
$\frac{y^2}{2} = \frac{x^2}{2} + C$.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} For $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 5y$ the separated form is
$\dfrac{dy}{y} = 5\,dx$. What is the antiderivative of the left side?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{\ln(\text{Abs}(y))}$ --- keep the
absolute value}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{2. Apply the initial condition to find $C$}

\begin{formulabox}
Substitute the given pair into the equation you have \emph{right after
integrating} --- before solving for $y$ --- and solve for $C$. Doing it early
avoids carrying an unknown constant through a square root or an exponential,
which is where sign errors appear.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 2x$ with $y(1) = 5$. Find $C$.
\step{The general solution is one integration away, so integrate first and let
the condition do the only remaining work.}
\step{$y = x^2 + C$. Substituting $x = 1$, $y = 5$ gives $5 = 1 + C$.}
$\ans{C = 4}$, so the particular solution is $y = x^2 + 4$.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} $\dfrac{dy}{dx} = 4x^3$ with $y(1) = 7$. What is $C$?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{C = 6}$, so $y = x^4 + 6$}
\end{tryitbox}

\skillheading{3. Use the particular solution}

\begin{formulabox}
Once $C$ is a number, the solution is an ordinary function: evaluate it,
solve it for $t$, or read its long-run behaviour. For growth and decay,
$\frac{dA}{dt} = kA$ always gives $A = A_0e^{kt}$; a half-life $h$ means
$k = -\frac{\ln 2}{h}$. When solving for a time, divide out the initial amount
\emph{first} so only a ratio goes into the logarithm.
\end{formulabox}

\begin{examplebox}
\textbf{Example:} The particular solution of a growth model is
$y = 3e^{2x}$. Find $y(1.5)$.
\step{The constant is already pinned down, so this is now just function
evaluation --- no calculus left to do.}
\step{$y(1.5) = 3e^{2(1.5)} = 3e^{3}$, and $e^{3} \approx 20.0855$.}
$y(1.5) = \ans{60.26}$ to two decimal places.
\end{examplebox}

\begin{tryitbox}
\textbf{Try it:} A sample of $100$ mg has a half-life of $5$ days, so
$A = 100e^{-(\ln 2/5)t}$. How much is left after $15$ days?\\[2pt]
\rotatebox{180}{\footnotesize check: $\ans{12.5}$ mg (three half-lives)}
\end{tryitbox}

\begin{watchoutbox}
\textbf{(!) Watch out:} a square-root solution has two branches and the initial
condition picks one --- if $y(1) = 2$ then $y = +\sqrt{x^3+3}$, never the
negative root. And the interval of validity is the one containing the initial
point: $y = \frac{1}{2-x}$ from $y(1) = 1$ is a solution only for $x < 2$.
\end{watchoutbox}
