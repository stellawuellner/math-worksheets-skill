\problem{$f(x) = 4x - 7$; a student wrote $f(3) = f \cdot 3 = 3f$. (a) Evaluate $f(3)$. (b) Say what $f(3)$ instructs you to do.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} The parentheses hold the \emph{input}, so replace every $x$ in the rule by $3$: $4(3) - 7 = 12 - 7$. \ans{f(3) = 5}

\textbf{(b) --- instructor-judged.} A correct sentence says $f(3)$ is an instruction to substitute $3$ for $x$ in the rule and evaluate, not a product of a quantity $f$ with $3$. Full credit for naming substitution as the operation; partial credit for saying only that $f$ is the name of the function.

\problem{$g(x) = x^2 - 2x$; a student wrote $g(5) = 25g - 10g = 15g$. (a) Evaluate $g(5)$. (b) Name the error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Substitute $5$ for $x$ --- the letter $g$ does not enter the arithmetic at all: $5^2 - 2(5) = 25 - 10$. \ans{g(5) = 15}

\textbf{(b) --- instructor-judged.} A correct answer says the student kept the function's name as if it were a factor, writing a $g$ beside every term, instead of replacing the input variable $x$ by $5$. The letter naming the function never enters the arithmetic. Full credit for identifying the stray function name as the error; no credit for an answer that only recomputes the value.

\problem{$f(x) = x^2 + 1$; a student claims $f(2+3) = f(2) + f(3)$. (a) Compute both sides. (b) Explain why the claim fails.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Left side: the input is the whole sum, $2 + 3 = 5$, so $f(5) = 5^2 + 1 = 26$. Right side: $f(2) = 2^2 + 1 = 5$ and $f(3) = 3^2 + 1 = 10$, so the sum is $15$. \ans{f(5) = 26 \quad\text{and}\quad f(2) + f(3) = 15}

\textbf{(b) --- instructor-judged.} A correct answer says the parentheses in $f(2+3)$ hold the input, so the whole sum is substituted once and squared, while $f(2) + f(3)$ squares each number separately and adds the constant $1$ twice. Splitting across a plus sign is a distributive move; substitution is not multiplication, so it is not allowed. Full credit for tying the failure to the parentheses holding an input rather than a factor.

\problem{$f(x) = 6x - 4$; a student solved $f(x) = 20$ as $f \cdot x = 20$, $x = \frac{10}{3}$. (a) Solve correctly. (b) Name the error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $f(x) = 20$ asks which input makes the rule output $20$, so solve $6x - 4 = 20$. Add $4$: $6x = 24$. Divide by $6$: $x = 4$. Check: $6(4) - 4 = 20$ $\checkmark$ \ans{x = 4}

\textbf{Common wrong answer:} $x = \frac{10}{3}$ comes from reading the notation as a product and dividing $20$ by $6$ without first undoing the subtraction.

\textbf{(b) --- instructor-judged.} A correct answer says the student treated the notation as a product of two quantities and divided, when the equation actually asks which input makes the rule output $20$, so the rule must be undone term by term. Full credit for naming the false product step; partial credit for showing the correct solution with no diagnosis.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{Order cost $C(n) = 12n + 25$ dollars; a student wrote $C \cdot 4 = 4C$. (a) Find $C(4)$ in dollars. (b) Explain the meaning of the $25$.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Substitute $n = 4$: $12(4) + 25 = 48 + 25$. \ans{C(4) = 73}

\textbf{Common wrong answer:} $48$ comes from using only the per-shirt rate and dropping the fixed setup fee.

\textbf{(b) --- instructor-judged.} A correct answer states that $25$ is the one-time setup charge in dollars, paid whatever the order size, and that the notation names the cost of an order of $4$ shirts rather than four copies of some number. Full credit for both halves; partial credit for the meaning of the setup charge alone.

\problem{$A(t) = 45 - 5t$ litres after $t$ minutes; a student read $A(t) = 0$ as ``$A$ times $t$ is $0$''. (a) Solve $A(t) = 0$ in minutes. (b) Name the error.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $A(t) = 0$ asks for the time when nothing is left, so solve $45 - 5t = 0$. Then $5t = 45$ and $t = 9$. Check: $45 - 5(9) = 0$ $\checkmark$ \ans{t = 9\ \text{min}}

\textbf{Common wrong answer:} $t = 0$ comes from reading the notation as a product set equal to zero and concluding that one factor must vanish.

\textbf{(b) --- instructor-judged.} A correct answer says that a product being zero forces a factor to be zero, but this expression is not a product of two quantities: it is the amount of water left after $t$ minutes, so setting it to zero asks when the tank empties. Full credit for rejecting the zero-product move by pointing out that there is no product.

\problem{$f(x) = 4x - 12$. (a) Find $f(0)$. (b) Solve $f(x) = 0$. (c) Explain the difference.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} Substitute $0$ for the input: $4(0) - 12 = -12$. \ans{f(0) = -12}

\textbf{Solution (b):} Here the \emph{output} is fixed at $0$ and the input is unknown: $4x - 12 = 0$, so $4x = 12$ and $x = 3$. \ans{x = 3}

\textbf{(c) --- instructor-judged.} A correct sentence says the first question fixes the input at zero and asks for the output, while the second fixes the output at zero and asks for the input; one is an evaluation, the other is an equation to solve. Full credit for naming which quantity is given in each.

\vspace{0.2cm}\noindent\rule{\linewidth}{0.2pt}

\problem{$f(x) = x^2 + 2x$; a student claims $f(6) = 2f(3)$. (a) Compute $f(3)$ and $f(6)$. (b) Show the claim is false.}

\textbf{Solution (a):} $f(3) = 3^2 + 2(3) = 9 + 6 = 15$ and $f(6) = 6^2 + 2(6) = 36 + 12 = 48$. \ans{f(3) = 15 \quad\text{and}\quad f(6) = 48}

\textbf{(b) --- instructor-judged.} A correct answer computes twice the value at input $3$, gets $30$, compares it with $48$, and says the two disagree, so doubling the input does not double the output. It then states that the notation instructs substitution of $6$ into the rule, not multiplication of a quantity by $6$. Full credit for the numerical comparison plus the statement about substitution.
